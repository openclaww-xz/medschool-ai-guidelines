## The three models compared

### 1. Default-permit (Buffalo, BU, GW-half, UMass-clinical)
AI use is presumptively allowed within enumerated zones (study aids, outlining, editing, literature work), with prohibitions reserved for assessment integrity, PHI, and clinical-reasoning substitution. Strengths: keeps policies honest about actual student behavior, supports AI literacy as a graduate competency (Buffalo's supervision model, BU's provisioned Terrier GPT/Noodle Factory, UMass's embedded-workflow clause). Weaknesses: relies on faculty to publish per-course boundaries; disclosure burden can be noisy (Buffalo expects citation even for editing/idea-gen); risk of drift toward normalization of AI-dependent work unless assessment design keeps pace. Works best where an OME actively supports faculty (Buffalo, GW) or where tool provisioning is real (BU).

### 2. Default-deny (CWRU, Touro, UC Davis, VCU, LECOM, both Arizona campuses, Howard-by-implication)
AI use on graded work prohibited unless permission is granted — via express faculty permission (VCU), written permission (UC Davis), instructor discretion (LECOM, Touro default), course-director permission (Arizona pair), or purpose-built AI-required exercises (CWRU). Strengths: cleanest integrity story; protects the process-of-learning rationale every school invokes; lowest ambiguity for assessments; easiest to enforce through existing honor codes (LECOM embeds AI exam use directly in its infraction list; Touro makes unacknowledged use plagiarism regardless of syllabus silence). Weaknesses: pushes use underground rather than eliminating it; free-rides on instructor effort to grant permissions course-by-course; typically silent on clinical environments (LECOM, CWRU, Touro) or thin on equity; detection-reliance risk (Tucson endorses plagiarism detectors — the cohort's only pro-detection clause). Two of these (Touro, UC Davis) show that default-deny can be highly engineered — precision definitions, prompt-level citation, machine-level tool bans — proving the model is not intrinsically naive.

### 3. Tiered (ECU explicitly; Penn State's syllabus-statement menu; GW's permitted/limited/prohibited distinction informally)
Every graded assignment carries a declared level (Green/Level 1 open; Level 2 conditional + Methods Note; Level 3 prohibited), with disclosure burden proportional to stakes. Strengths: resolves the permit/deny argument per-assignment instead of per-institution; makes faculty expectations legible and auditable; enables proportionate disclosure (Level 1 needs none); ECU uniquely extends tiering to faculty conduct including grading governance, and pairs it with equity-of-access guarantees (free Copilot floor, non-AI alternatives) and oral-defense integrity checks. Penn State achieves a lighter version by shipping three approved syllabus models with a non-waivable floor (no AI on any exam format or SOAP/procedure notes in ANY model) — arguably the most transferable design in the cohort because it needs no new bureaucracy. Weaknesses: tier discipline decays if declarations lapse (ECU mandates the syllabus line; Penn State's statements are officially "suggestions"); three-way systems demand faculty development to avoid mislabeling.

### Model verdict
The **tiered model dominates** on every evaluation axis that matters in medical education: it protects assessment validity (Level 3 / Penn State's floor), legitimizes beneficial use (Level 1), scales disclosure to stakes, and gives honor codes an unambiguous reference point (the declared tier). Default-deny is the strongest *integrity-floor* model and the cheapest to adopt, but its silence on clinical learning and equity makes it a floor, not a policy architecture; it also ages worst as AI becomes ambient in word processors (a problem CWRU uniquely acknowledged in 2023). Default-permit is the strongest *clinical-readiness* model (Buffalo's supervision framework, UMass's ambient-scribe regime) but is only as good as its faculty-support and tool-governance infrastructure. The observed frontier is **hybrid**: ECU = tiers + equity + faculty parity; GW = disclosure culture + data taxonomy on a permissive base; Penn State = tiers-as-statements + hard floor + anti-detector evidence posture.

## Ranking (cohort B)

1. **ECU Brody** — complete tiered implementation: declared tiers on all graded work, ready-to-paste syllabus language, three-element Methods Note, faculty parity including a grading-governance ban on autonomous AI, equity-of-access principles with non-AI alternatives, oral-defense integrity mechanism, emergency-amendment procedure, policy expiration date. The reference implementation of the tiered model.
2. **GW SMHS** — best disclosure architecture in the corpus (mandatory disclosure elements, AMEE-Guide-192-derived framework, fill-in template, prompt-log retention), a no-punishment-for-disclosure guarantee, data-classification appendix with worked PHI/FERPA/restricted scenarios, anti-vendor-HIPAA-claim hard line, accessibility/financial-equity principle, anti-ban safeguard, task-force self-disclosure. Held below ECU only because tiers are described, not systematized, so consistency rests on faculty uptake.
3. **Buffalo Jacobs** — best clinical-environment policy: NEJM-grounded four-part supervision model (Detect/Evaluate/Frame/Teach), BAA requirement for site-deployed tools, UBIT tool whitelist, anti-ban safeguard, enumerated HIPAA consequences, OME support loop with student feedback. Clinical-documentation stance deliberately conservative.
4. **Touro** — best default-deny engineering: defined "substantially," prompt-level citation with style examples, mandatory syllabus statement + verbatim privacy reminder (Jan 2026), the corpus's only agentic-AI ban, TouroOne approved-tool regime with machine-level prohibition, expulsion-backed enforcement, Provost-for-AI office maintaining detector intelligence. Held at 4 because it is integrity/security-centric: no clinical, pedagogical, or equity content.
5. **Penn State COM** — de facto tiers via three approved syllabus models with a non-waivable exam/SOAP floor, Grammarly-in-the-definition closure, "Sourced Tool" disclosure, and the cohort's only evidence-based anti-detector stance. Losses points for optionality ("suggestions"), thin privacy, no faculty-side rules.
6. **UMass Chan** — finest task-level clinical granularity (ambient-scribe opt-in, embedded-AI carve-out, meeting-summary PHI ban, supervision keyed to judgment influence, principle-based catch-all) and named authorship with its own AI-use disclosure — but it is an unapproved v2 draft with blank approval fields and no preclinical coverage.
7. **BU Chobanian** — richest philosophy (developmental learning, environmental cost, algorithmic inequity, a stated evidence bar no current point-of-care tool meets) plus real provisioning (Terrier GPT, Noodle Factory) — but almost no structure: no mechanism, enforcement, or faculty duties.
8. **UC Davis** — tightest short default-deny: written-permission gate, minimal permissive band, EHR-only clinical AI, curricular-ingestion controls, full governance/sanction metadata. A model small policy, just a floor.
9. **U Arizona (Tucson & Phoenix)** — well-governed sibling policies (policy numbers, committee approvals, review dates) with two standout clauses (mandatory training opt-out at Tucson; curriculum-wide H&P/A&P ban and blunt accountability at Phoenix) but no structure, disclosure format, or faculty duties; Tucson's detector endorsement is a cohort outlier in the wrong direction; Phoenix's "perform physical exams" prohibition is drafting-by-analogy.
10. **VCU SOM** — proper SOP form (LCME anchor, VIP professionalism routing, five-body enforcement matrix, grammar-tool carve-out, EHR-native automation distinction, study-aid permission) — solidly built, purely restrictive beyond study uses.
11. **Howard University** — sophisticated university-wide framework (no-training rule, contract review, ask-first default, accessibility duties, AI Advisory Council) whose teaching-and-learning section is minimal and authorization-gated; as a medical-education policy it is only a floor, but the enterprise layer (esp. vendor/IP sovereignty) exceeds every SOM-only document.
12. **LECOM** — competent handbook default-deny with real teeth (AI enumerated as an Honor Code cheating infraction) and a sensible no-style-guide citation fallback; no clinical, privacy-beyond-caution, or faculty content.
13. **CWRU** — brief but genuine: "effort and struggle" pedagogy, foresight about ambient AI integration (2023!), and a required-AI exercise valve inside a blanket own-work rule. The best short ban in the corpus, but a ban.
14. **Morehouse** — minimal handbook note: usable citation rule (tool/version/URL) and hallucination literacy, but tool-name-dependent, no clinical/data awareness, no governance. The cohort's only conservative-naive document.

## Cross-cohort-B observations worth carrying forward
- **Two anti-ban safeguards** (Buffalo, GW): faculty blanket bans require central approval — the strongest structural signal that a school treats AI as a competency rather than a threat.
- **Two policy self-disclosures** (GW task force's Copilot use; UMass's ChatGPT-5.2 review; Touro's AI-summary acknowledgment makes three): disclosure norms being modeled by the policies themselves — a new genre marker of 2026-vintage documents.
- **Agentic AI** appears exactly once (Touro). None of the medical-school policies contemplate autonomous agents — the next drafting wave.
- **Grammar tools are the sharpest unresolvable divergence**: exempted (VCU explicitly; Touro/UC Davis functionally), or swept in (Penn State names Grammarly as a covered generative tool). Tool-list-based scope is incoherent across the corpus.
- **Clinical documentation converges on EHR-nativism**: AI-generated H&Ps/notes banned outside EHR-supported or site-approved tools (VCU, Buffalo, UC Davis, both Arizonas, Penn State, UMass) — the closest thing cohort B has to a consensus rule.
- **Detection splits three ways**: endorsed (Tucson), discouraged with cited local research (Penn State), monitored-with-disclaimers by a provost office (Touro). No consensus; false-positive risk is now an explicit policy concern.
- **Equity of access** is almost exclusively an ECU/GW theme (free-tool floors, non-AI alternatives, financial-burden language, BU's free Terrier GPT) — the least-diffused of the mature policy elements.
