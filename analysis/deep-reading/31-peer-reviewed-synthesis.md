# Run 31 Synthesis — What the Peer-Reviewed Literature Establishes, and What Only Our Corpus Can Answer

## Scope of this read

All 47 peer-reviewed files in `raw/peer-reviewed/` were read in full; per-file notes (mirroring source filenames, YAML front-matter: `source_file` / `read_status` / `approx_words_read`) sit alongside this synthesis. One file (Clin Teach policy-gap study) is abstract-only in our corpus — full text paywalled at capture time; noted accordingly. The set spans: 15+ learner surveys (local → national → multinational), 3 meta-analyses/systematic reviews of perceptions, 2 licensing-exam benchmark studies, 1 worldwide exam systematic review, 4 ambient-scribe studies (time-motion, UK GP survey, evidence synthesis, governance review), 2 census-style policy audits (ASPPH public health schools; Iranian journals), 7 framework/consensus papers (CARE-AI Delphi, NAIL-G, competency frameworks ×3, governance frameworks ×2), 3 disclosure bibliometrics, 5 curriculum/faculty reviews, and 2 capability-policy pieces (SGIM position statement; AI-governance documentation analysis).

## 1. What the empirical literature establishes

### Adoption (the demand side)
- **Adoption is near-universal among learners and rising fast.** Medical students: 88.7% ever used ChatGPT (US national, n=177); 86% familiar (Israel); daily use among Swedish physiotherapy students rose 13%→57% in one year; first-year Cypriot students 70.5–100% weekly+; 52.6% of French health-professions students used GenAI *during clinical placements*. Meta-analysis across 96 studies (>45,000 students, 37 countries): prior AI use 63.2% pooled (95% prediction interval 8.5–100%), familiarity 63.3% (PI 7.8–100).
- **Clinicians follow.** ~21% of US physicians used AI for documentation in 2024; 1 in 5 UK GPs use GenAI; Kaiser Permanente NC scaled ambient scribes to 7,260 physicians (3,442 adopters in first 10 weeks of opt-in).
- **Use is task-tiered.** Learners use GenAI heavily for low-stakes cognitive work (information extraction 77.9%, bibliographic search 74.5%, concept explanation, question generation) and much less for patient-facing work (drafting patient documents 38.2%) — but 21–46.5% already use it for clinical notes/differentials, and 23.5% of placement users admitted entering patient-identifying data at least once; 47.1% processed real (putatively anonymized) medical data.
- **Trust is moderate and conditional.** Usefulness–trust gap replicated everywhere (usefulness 4.1–4.2/5 vs trust 3.1–3.3/5; 62.7% "neither" trust nor distrust). Students fear dependency (90.9%), confidentiality breach (87.4%), and skill erosion (84.8%) far more than job replacement (>60% dismiss).

### Effectiveness
- **Ambient scribes deliver engagement and well-being gains, not throughput.** Best direct-observation evidence (Singapore time-motion, 169 consultations, mixed models): documentation time −0.8 min (−15%, P=.04), eye contact +10.6% (P=.009), consultation duration and cycle time unchanged. Consistent with US single-system estimates (−0.4 to −0.6 min) and RCTs; Penn: −20.4% note time, −30% after-hours charting. Atrium Health controlled study: no significant productivity gain; PHTI 2025: no clear financial ROI. Conclusion crystallizing across studies: scribes are quality-of-care/burnout interventions, not productivity tools.
- **Educational effectiveness is thin and mixed.** RCT-level evidence: ChatGPT-blended teaching improved satisfaction/skills/exams in some trials; a Norwegian pharmacy RCT showed a non-significant gain; a pediatric-nursing study found the *textbook group outperformed the ChatGPT group* on complex ethical reasoning. Meta-analysis of GenAI teaching vs traditional (BMC Med Educ 2025): improvements exist but heterogeneous. LLM grading: ChatGPT-3.5 error rate 1.0% vs standardized patients 7.2% in one study; 26–83% agreement in another; GPT-4 grades stricter than humans. Every effectiveness claim in this literature is single-site, short-term, self-reported, or all three.
- **Capability baseline is settled.** Multiple free models now score 72–92% on USMLE Step 1 sample items (Grok 91.6%, including 91.3% on image items); a worldwide systematic review confirms passing-or-better performance across licensing exams. The technical substrate for both student use and assessment-integrity concern is fully in place — and model rankings shift within months, so any static benchmark (or static policy listing banned tools) decays.

### Policy (the supply side)
- **Formal policy is rare, late, and shallow wherever censused.** ASPPH (our closest methodological sibling): only 18/155 (11.6%) public-health-school documents met formal policy criteria; content skews to classroom integrity (usage guidance 83%, integrity 78%) while legal compliance (22%), ethics (28%), and discipline/detection (22%) lag. Iranian medical journals: 28.5% adoption; disclosure 99.1% and author-responsibility 88% where present, but consequences of violation 11%. Med-ed journals: 2.5% AI-disclosure rate against certain higher actual use ("transparency paradox"). US research universities 2024: <half offered classroom guidance. The Clin Teach study (our corpus anchor for this claim) and every review in this set agree: **adoption has outrun governance everywhere it has been measured.**
- **Who leads policy — the multi-level pattern.** Supranational bodies (UNESCO, WHO, OECD, EU AI Act) supply principles; national actors lag (US: FDA oversight "does not yet approach" pharma rigor per SGIM; state AI laws a minority patchwork; HHS strategy and the 2026 WH AI framework only recent signals). Professional societies issue position statements (SGIM, AMA, ANA, FDI, specialty societies for GME scribes; Joint Commission/CHAI for governance). **In practice, institutions — medical schools, health systems, journals — are the operative policy units**, and most delegate downward: the dominant instrument is the tiered, instructor-level syllabus statement (prohibited/restricted/permitted), not school-wide mandates. Health systems act through steering committees, approved-tool lists, and vendor contracts. The most consistent finding across frameworks (CARE-AI, NAIL-G, role-differentiated competencies, nursing governance): accountability is being *institutionalized* — individuals + teams + institutions share duty — precisely because no higher level has filled the vacuum.
- **Prescription far outstrips description.** The 2024–2026 literature pivoted from "should we" to normative frameworks: CARE-AI (10 principles, 303 participants, 96% endorsement), competency frameworks (6D×Miller levels; user/developer/leader roles), nursing governance (9-step lifecycle checklists, 5 trust domains), faculty-development models (4 readiness domains), ambient-scribe governance (Leavitt's Diamond, credentialing-style competency). These are checklists awaiting adoption data. Almost no study examines whether actual institutional policies contain these elements.

## 2. What remains open — and what our 378-source corpus answers better than any published study

1. **Policy prevalence and content at census scale, across sectors, over time.** The only comparable census (ASPPH) covered 153 public-health institutions, one sector, one window (Oct 2024–Mar 2025), 18 policies. Our corpus spans medical schools (allopathic + osteopathic), GME, health systems, and national frameworks — 378 sources — enabling the first cross-sector, multi-year map of who issued what, when, and at what governance level (school-wide vs instructor-delegated vs system-level).
2. **The policy–practice gap, supply side.** Survey after survey documents adoption under "little guidance" (US national, French placements, 241-study scoping review). No published study links measured student use to the specific policies (or absence) at those institutions. Our corpus is the missing supply-side variable: policy presence/typology can now be joined to the demand-side estimates the literature already provides.
3. **Which framework elements actually diffused.** The normative literature specifies concrete artifacts — approved-tool lists, disclosure norms, tiered syllabus statements, AI-error reporting, equity audits, steering committees, trainee note-composition protections, vendor-contract clauses. Zero studies measure their real-world adoption. Coding our corpus against CARE-AI/NAIL-G/nursing-governance checklists turns 2025–2026's consensus frameworks into testable diffusion hypotheses.
4. **Temporal dynamics.** Every empirical study is a snapshot; the Swedish physiotherapy repeat-cross-section (use 13%→57% daily in 12 months; guidance clarity unchanged despite new policy) is the only published longitudinal hint. Our corpus's date-stamped documents can chart policy issuance waves against the capability timeline (ChatGPT → GPT-4 → GPT-4o/multimodal → agentic tools) and test whether policies that name specific tools (77.8% of ASPPH documents named ChatGPT) were revised as those tools aged.
5. **Who leads, empirically.** The multi-level accountability story is currently normative assertion. Our corpus can establish, descriptively and at scale: which actor issued each governance artifact first (national body vs school vs health system vs specialty society), the text-level dependencies between them (do school policies cite AAMC/AMA/WHO?), and whether early movers (pioneer schools) shaped later cohort language — a diffusion analysis no Delphi or single-site study can support.
6. **Sector boundary effects.** The literature treats schools, placement sites, health systems, and journals as separate worlds, yet students cross all of them (a trainee faces an syllabus policy, a hospital approved-tool list, and a journal disclosure regime in one week). Only a multi-sector corpus can surface contradictions between layers — e.g., disclosure required by journals but unaddressed by clerkship policy; scribes governed by health systems while GME guidance is silent.

**Novelty statement (for publication):** Published empirical work has thoroughly documented the demand side — near-universal learner adoption, moderate trust, task-tiered use, and modest engagement/well-being gains from ambient AI — while census-style audits in adjacent sectors (public health schools, journals) uniformly find formal policy rare, shallow, and enforcement-poor. What no study to date provides is a census-scale, multi-sector, longitudinal inventory of AI governance documents across US medical education and its clinical learning environments. Our 378-source corpus delivers exactly that: it can quantify policy prevalence and content by sector and issuer, date the diffusion curve against model-capability milestones, code real policies against the consensus frameworks (CARE-AI, NAIL-G, lifecycle-governance checklists) that 2024–2026 scholarship produced but never validated against practice, and — by joining the supply side to the literature's rich demand-side estimates — test the field's central, still-unverified claim: that institutional governance, not technology, is the binding constraint on responsible AI in medical education.

## 3. Corroboration/contradiction ledger vs. our corpus anchors

- **Clin Teach policy-gap study**: corroborated independently by the ASPPH census (11.6% formal policy), the 241-study scoping review (0.6% literacy studies; students self-teaching), the French placement survey (10% say use is regulated), and every framework paper's rationale section. No contradiction found in 47 files.
- **Osteopathic/journal/disclosure findings**: the medRxiv bibliometric (2.5% disclosure) and Iranian journal census (28.5% policy, 11.1% enforcement) extend the same gap to publishing — cautioning that policy-on-paper ≠ behavior, a validity limit our document corpus must state plainly.
- **Effectiveness claims** (ambient scribes; GenAI teaching) are single-site and modest — our corpus should not cite them as justification narratives without noting the weak ROI evidence (Atrium, PHTI).
