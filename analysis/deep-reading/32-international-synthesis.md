# Run 32 — International corpus synthesis (26 documents, 12 jurisdictions)

## 1. The four national governance models

### A. UK — the professional-judgment model
**Instruments:** GMC Good medical practice 2024 (101 duties); GMC AI & innovative technologies Q&A; joint statutory regulators' statement on AI in education (6 regulators); NHS England GP long read; HEE capability framework (+structure page +launch webinar); Medical Schools Council/HDR UK data-science curriculum; Council of Deans of Health GenAI principles.
**Mechanism:** regulate the *doctor*, not the tool. GMP is explicitly "not a set of rules" — professional judgment applies technology-neutral duties to any technology; the single AI-specific amendment enumerates "software, diagnostic tests, and digital tools" inside a pre-existing must-report adverse-incident duty (para 73c). MHRA regulates products; GMC explicitly declines ("We do not regulate or approve new technologies"). The stack is layered by audience: conduct (GMC) → education expectations (joint regulators: accountability, integrity, AI literacy, "AI explainability" as a learner-demonstrable duty, preparation for practice) → workforce capability (HEE: 6 domains, 11 sub-domains, 195 capability statements, 4 levels, 5 role archetypes) → undergraduate curriculum (MSC: 12 competency domains incl. automation bias, real-world validation, bias mitigation; DECODE-derived, 60% consensus threshold; survey proof that half of schools teaching "data science" teach only statistics) → non-medical professions (CoDH 8 principles: sustainable, "AI augmenting education rather than driving it").
**Signature moves:** rule-extension-by-enumeration (GMP 73c); model cards + MHRA Yellow Card + EHIA at GP level (NHS long read — distribution-shift literacy: "Used for a different purpose or on a different population, AI will produce misleading and potentially harmful results"); candor in the HEE webinar (UKMLA 2024 silent on digital health; "students upskilling us").
**Strength:** durability under technological churn — nothing to rewrite when the next model generation arrives. **Weakness:** relies on institutions actually building the capability layer; the webinar admits licensing exams lagged.

### B. Singapore — the lifecycle-regulatory model (AIHGle 2.0, Mar 2026)
**Mechanism:** regulate the *tool through its lifecycle* with three accountable stakeholder classes (Developers / Deployers / Users) across a seven-stage lifecycle (Plan&Design → Develop → Evaluate → Deploy → Monitor → Review → End-of-life), under statutory anchors (HPA/MD registration, HCSA licensing, PDPA, professional Acts, Civil Law s37) and ISO 13485/HSA GL-04. Mandatory human oversight for all Clinical/Clinical-Ops AI; performance floor (outcomes "maintained or improved compared to if AI were not deployed"); risk × oversight matrix with prohibition bands (no autonomous clinical decision-making; escalation-only design pattern); AI registry with risk classification; governance three-way separation (policy/operations/audit); MD/non-MD boundary table that decides e.g. whether an ambient scribe is regulated by its function; TPLC incl. end-of-life decommissioning duty.
**Signature moves:** deepest technical content in the corpus (drift, PETs, RAG, red teaming, logit-certainty, aleatoric/epistemic uncertainty, IMDRF, AI Verify, Feb 2026 AI-MD Exemption Sandbox); DTC duty ("patients are clearly informed that they are interacting with AI"); professionals' deskilling-contingency duty ("maintain skills in non-AI-augmented workflows").
**Strength:** actual safety architecture with enforcement hooks; zero ambiguity about who owes what. **Weakness:** education is absent — it governs deployment environments a school inherits, not teaching; heavy documentation burden; presumes a regulator of MHRA/HSA competence.

### C. Canada — the task-force model
**Instruments:** Royal College task-force report (Feb 2020 — Delphi + 22 interviews + ~4,000 surveys + roundtable + commissioned scoping review); AFMC federal-AI-strategy response (June 2026); U of T hub (AI-ready University + SGS graduate guidance); Temerty MD index (no local AI policy — deference demonstrated).
**Mechanism:** the specialty accreditor *rewrites the competency constitution*: digital health literacy into CanMEDS (possibly an eighth role); Clinical Informatics as a new discipline; AI teaching across all residency programmes + CPD; four-adopter-role workforce taxonomy (innovator/early/majority/late — with metacognition skills for majority adopters and the right of late adopters "to continue to practise medicine in traditional ways"); "no recommendations to alter the number of specialist physicians"; human-primacy doctrine ("we do not imagine a machine ever making a final decision... without human supervision"; physicians must stay able to "take full control of an autonomous task"); multi-party liability analysis incl. the refusal-to-use counterfactual and Canada's health-data ownership vacuum. AFMC runs the opposite direction — outward lobbying for platform accountability, watermarking, and online-safety legislation against health misinformation.
**Strength:** evidence-saturated, workforce-realistic, durable (2020 and still the template). **Weakness:** pre-generative (no LLM/assessment content); recommendations are hortatory — no school-level or conduct enforcement; the national association's two voices (competency-inward vs regulation-outward) don't join up.

### D. Australia — the two-lane assessment model
**Instruments:** University of Sydney two-lane policy + 82k-char implementation FAQ (Bridgeman/Liu); AMC 2019 ethics-exploration annual-report piece (Beauchamp & Childress; robot-breaks-bad-news scenario); University of Melbourne governance taskforce (GAIT) + student guidance.
**Mechanism:** govern the *assessment portfolio*, derived from one axiom — "**any unenforceable restriction damages assessment validity**." Lane 1 (secure, in-person, mainly program-level: interactive orals, vivas, supervised tasks — AI restricted or scaffolded as relevant, may even include AI for authentic outcomes like architecture) + Lane 2 (open, unit-level: AI as a "menu" with educator guidance not control; "there is no amber traffic light"). Default flipped to AI-allowed in open assessment (S1 2025); coordinators cannot prohibit what they cannot enforce; detection rejected on evidence AND equity grounds ("only catches those who use the tools poorly or cannot afford the latest models"); contract cheating still banned because it "is not a graduate quality we wish to develop" — a principled boundary, not an enforcement claim; grading may migrate to lane-1 hurdles + pass/fail lane 2; TEQSA co-development; free Copilot + 1,000+ Cogniti steer-able agents as equity/compliance infrastructure; explicit recognition that "programmatic assessment" originates in medical education and suits competency-based medicine.
**Strength:** the only model that is honest about enforcement physics and rebuilds the credential around it. **Weakness:** integrity now rests wholly on lane-1 quality and resourcing (exam capacity, invigilation of wearables, remote-proctoring risk assessments); expensive to run; assumes regulator partnership (TEQSA).

## 2. Which model a US medical school would most benefit from importing

**Primary import: the Australia two-lane assessment model.** Reasons grounded in the corpus:
1. **It solves the problem US schools actually have.** The recurring pain point across the US school policies already in this repo is assessment integrity under unenforceable prohibition — AI-detector reliance, per-course prohibition patches, honor-code whack-a-mole. Sydney's FAQ is a worked, objection-by-objection answer (~50 answered staff questions, systems changes, policy language, hurdle architecture) that a US school can adapt almost directly. The UK model presupposes a statutory regulator and national licensing apparatus a school cannot conjure; Singapore's presumes HSA-class product regulation; Canada's operates at accreditor scale. Two-lane operates at exactly the unit of a school.
2. **It is medically native.** The FAQ itself notes programmatic assessment "is associated with... medical education in particular" and suits "dentistry, medicine and veterinary science." US MD programs already run the lane-1 toolkit (OSCEs, shelf exams, clerkship observership, Step-style secure exams) and already have program-level outcomes (LCME/NBME machinery) — the migration cost is lower for a medical school than for any other faculty. Program-level secure orals/vivas map naturally onto residency-interview and boards logic.
3. **It is equity-forward and detection-skeptical on stated empirical grounds** — the position US med schools are converging on but rarely articulate, with the bonus of institutional tool provision (Copilot/Cogniti pattern) closing the paid-tool gap.
4. **It imports cleanly alongside US-specific layers:** two-lane governs *assessment*; the school's clinical-AI governance (ambient scribes, clinical tools) is better served by importing Singapore's structures in miniature (below).

**Secondary import: Singapore AIHGle 2.0 as the clinical-environment governance template** — not the statute, but the transposable artifacts: the oversight × risk matrix (human-in/over/out-of-the-loop with prohibition bands), the AI registry with risk classification for every deployed clinical AI, governance three-way separation, the materiality-based patient-communication doctrine, and the deskilling-contingency duty for residents. A US academic medical center can adopt these as policy without any regulator.
**Tertiary import: the UK's rule-extension-by-enumeration technique** — put "software and digital tools" inside existing professional-conduct and incident-reporting duties rather than writing a new AI code of conduct; and the MSC curriculum competencies (automation bias, real-world validation) as the UME curriculum layer.

## 3. Regulatory density vs professional autonomy — the tradeoff surface

The four models are points on a single axis: **who holds the judgment, and how much text surrounds them.**

| Dimension | UK | Singapore | Canada | Australia |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Density | Low (conduct) / high (capability) | Highest | Medium (competency, hortatory) | Medium (structural, not behavioral) |
| Judgment seat | Individual professional | Distributed by lifecycle stage | The accreditor (framework) | The assessment designer (program level) |
| Failure mode | Capability lag; silent non-compliance | Box-ticking; innovation drag; SME burden | Recommendations without enforcement; churn risk | Lane-1 resourcing; equity of secure assessment |
| Churn resilience | Highest (technology-neutral) | Medium (versioned, sandbox-assisted) | Medium (monitoring mandate) | Highest for assessment (roles, not tools) |

- **Density buys determinacy, costs adaptability.** Singapore can say *who must do what when a model drifts* because it wrote 42 pages of lifecycle rules; GMC cannot — it can only say the doctor "must use professional judgment" and report incidents. When the failure mode is known and stable (drug-device safety, drift, distribution shift), density wins — errors there are exactly the ones individual judgment handles worst. When the technology mutates faster than the rulebook (GenAI in coursework), density loses — which is why Sydney legislated *roles and structures* (two lanes, menu doctrine, no-amber rule) rather than tool-specific rules, and why GMC's enumerated amendment inside a 30-year-old duty will outlive most AI policies.
- **Autonomy is not free; it is debt.** The UK model's elegance (GMC: "We can't tell doctors what to do in individual situations") works only because an entire invisible infrastructure carries the judgment: HEE's 195 capability statements, MSC's competencies, the joint regulators' expectations, royal-college CPD. The HEE webinar transcript shows the debt being serviced in public — licensing exams missing digital health, faculty being upskilled by students. A jurisdiction that imports the judgment model without the capability stack gets conservative-naive practice, not professional judgment.
- **The two-lane model relocates rather than removes autonomy**: from the individual assessor (who can no longer prohibit) to the program-level designer (who must decide which outcomes are secured where). That is autonomy at the layer where accountability can actually be exercised — a structural answer to the same distrust-of-individual-discretion problem that Singapore answers with density.
- **Density and equity interact.** UvA (the institutional expression of EU density) reaches "no compulsion without a licence; every unit passable without GenAI" — a strong equity floor that permissive models must replicate by provisioning (Sydney/Melbourne's free Copilot). Sydney shows the permissive model *also* regulates equity, just through procurement and assessment design rather than mandates.
- **For a US school the practical synthesis:** maximal density where failure is physical and known (clinical AI deployment — import Singapore's registry/matrix/monitoring), maximal autonomy where failure is epistemic and fast-moving (assessment — import Sydney's structure, which deliberately strips unenforceable discretion), enumerated-rule-extension for conduct (GMC 73c pattern), and competency frameworks (MSC/Royal College) as the slow layer that keeps judgment legitimate. The one import to refuse: India's approach — naming AI in a curriculum without governing it — which is what a school produces by default if it does nothing.
