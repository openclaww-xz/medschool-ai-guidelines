# ACP — Artificial Intelligence in the Provision of Health Care: Policy Position Paper (Annals of Internal Medicine, June 4, 2024)

## (a) Authority and authorship
- Authors: **Nadia Daneshvar, JD, MPH; Deepti Pandita, MD; Shari Erickson, MPH; Lois Snyder Sulmasy, JD; Matthew DeCamp, MD, PhD** — for the **ACP Medical Informatics Committee and the Ethics, Professionalism and Human Rights Committee**. Methods stated: review of laws/regulations, ethical principles, empirical studies (Google/Google Scholar searches); input from Board of Governors, Board of Regents, Council of Early Career Physicians, Council of Resident/Fellow Members, Council of Student Members, Council of Subspecialty Societies. Approvals: both committees Jan 2024, **Board of Regents Feb 2024**. Peer-reviewed in Annals — the only peer-reviewed framework in the corpus.
- Powers claimed: ACP policy/advocacy for internal medicine (~160k members). Binds members' professional expectations; aims to inform FDA/ONC/regulatory policy. Companion follow-up on ethics announced in comments ("the College's ethics committee plans to expand on this initial paper") — delivered as the 2026 ethics paper elsewhere in this corpus.

## (b) Policy philosophy
- Posture: **cautious-welcome, evidence-and-oversight-first**. Framing quotes:
  - "the proliferation of these technologies is **cautiously welcomed by some physicians**. Others think it presents challenges to the patient–physician relationship and the professional integrity of physicians."
  - "AI-enabled tools and systems **may not only be expected but required in the future of medicine**" (benefit ceiling) vs "clinical decision support based on AI output without a basic understanding of AI technology can have **serious, even fatal consequences**" (risk floor).
  - Terminology stance: "when being used for clinical decision making, **the more appropriate term is 'augmented' intelligence**."
  - Generative AI deference: "**it is too early to comment on its full scope and implications. The ACP will continue to consider this evolving technology as it matures.**"
  - Balance doctrine: "Even though skepticism is warranted, being overly skeptical of AI can have ramifications, too... **A balanced approach to AI technologies is in order.**"

## (c) Complete clause inventory — the 10 position statements (verbatim) + key rationale clauses
1. "**ACP firmly believes that AI-enabled technologies should complement and not supplant the logic and decision making of physicians and other clinicians.**" Rationale: "physicians must be careful not to place too much faith or decision-making power in AI- and ML-enabled tools"; "**'Automation complacency' and 'automation bias,' or the tendency to 'trust algorithms without sufficient skepticism,' are not uncommon** among users... **experts have been just as likely as beginners to overly rely on autonomous systems, and this overreliance is mostly unaffected by training.**"
   - Anthropomorphism clause (unique): "ACP believes that everyone, especially persons in the fields of AI academia and health care, **has a responsibility to avoid anthropomorphizing AI models** and their functionality... **Anthropomorphic language can include terms such as think, learn, and hallucinate.** Some have argued that describing certain AI outputs as 'hallucinations,' in addition to being inaccurate and anthropomorphic, is not ideal because of stigmatization. **Confabulation may be a better term.**"
2. "**the development, testing, and use of AI in health care must be aligned with principles of medical ethics**, serving to enhance patient care, clinical decision making, the patient–physician relationship, and health care equity and justice." Rationale grounds in Ethics Manual principles (beneficence, nonmaleficence, autonomy/truth-telling, justice). Ambient documentation could "promote better communication"; chatbots "could interfere with patient–physician relationships" if "required or burdensome." System-level: "ensuring patients, physicians, and other clinicians are involved in the design, development, implementation, and testing of AI tools... **throughout the entire product cycle**." Peril noted: "the peril of AI is that it could exert too much influence on clinical decision making and be subject to hidden or unconscious biases or agendas... (for example, if AI outputs exclude a clinically indicated option because of cost)."
3. Transparency: "ACP recommends that **patients, physicians, and other clinicians be made aware, when possible, that AI tools are likely being used** in medical treatment and decision making." Rationale: patient-preference evidence (95.7% want to be told when AI played a big role, 86.5% small role; concerns: misdiagnosis 91.5%, privacy 70.8%); endorses "The Physicians' Charter for Responsible AI"; "**ensuring that physician knowledge and discretion can supersede AI-generated 'defaults'**"; "there must be readily available mechanisms for clinicians to call attention to, and for AI developers to correct, errors in AI-generated output"; feasibility caveats for embedded AI (EHR, glucometer) and opt-out variability; calls on FDA/ONC for "**standard, easy-to-understand model labels and standardized reporting checklists**... **These labels must be updated as models are changed and updated.** Training and testing data sources and attributes should be transparent and accessible to physicians, regulators, and auditors. **Algorithmic impact assessments, audits, and postmarket monitoring are also recommended.**"
4. Privacy: "AI developers, implementers, and researchers **should prioritize the privacy and confidentiality of patient and clinician data**"; "If patient, physician, or other clinician data must be used... **the data should first be deidentified and aggregated**"; "**deidentification of data, particularly if the data is unstructured, can be a substantial challenge**"; reaffirms **federated learning** and "renew our call for **comprehensive federal privacy legislation**, with special provisions regarding privacy protections for AI data sets."
5. Safety/effectiveness/equity: "**clinical safety and effectiveness, as well as health equity, must be a top priority**... approached by using a **continuous improvement process that includes a feedback mechanism**... **end-user testing in diverse real-world clinical contexts, using real patient demographics, and peer-reviewed research. Special attention must be given to known and evolving risks**." Rationale: models "trained, maintained, and updated using the latest clinical practice guidelines"; IBM Watson case; "**Pragmatic randomized controlled trials are recommended**"; PDSA cycles with the caveat "**they can contribute to model deterioration when model-informed decisions are introduced into the model**" (feedback-loop drift); FDA MAUDE analysis: 266 adverse events, 25 devices — "malfunctions (n = 238), injuries (n = 25), or death (n = 1)."
6. Disparities (a–c): "**AI model development data to include data from diverse populations**"; "**calls on Congress, HHS, and other key entities to support and invest in research and analysis** of data in AI systems to identify any disparate or discriminatory effects"; "**multisector collaborations**... prioritize research and development of ways to mitigate biases." Rationale: Obermeyer cost-proxy case; dermatology algorithms on darker skin tones; AHRQ Dec 2023 review (13 algorithms may perpetuate disparities vs 4 may lessen); "**Flawed or incomplete data sets that are not inclusive can automate inequality**"; bias "not only a data problem, it is also an ethical problem"; deployment bias "if they are used for certain patient groups and not others"; "Therefore, research evaluating the effect of AI technology... **including assessments of whether AI use in medicine contributes to or drives biased or discriminatory health practices or inequitable health outcomes, is necessary.**"
7. Accountability & governance: "**developers of AI must be accountable for the performance of their models. There should be a coordinated federal AI strategy, built upon a unified governance framework**" ensuring (a) oversight, (b) enforcement, (c) "**mechanisms to enable and ensure the reporting of adverse events resulting from the use of AI.**" Rationale: Joint Commission for government-procured AI; HHS OIG; "outcome-focused regulations"; supports "**a public-private partnership to establish a 'nationwide network of health AI assurance laboratories'**"; adverse events "reported to the appropriate regulatory bodies... and logged in public databases"; supports "HHS Office for Civil Rights enforcement against violations of **section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act**... for the use of clinical algorithms in discriminatory ways."
8. Burden: "**in all stages of development and use, AI tools should be designed to reduce physician and other clinician burden**"; "sufficiently tested (that is, used by actual end users under real-world circumstances...) **before deployment**"; feedback mechanisms "should not be burdensome"; "**The effects of AI-enabled burden reduction tools on burnout should be assessed**"; payment clause: "**New payment initiatives, especially those for value-based care, must support the use of AI technology** as a mechanism to reduce burden."
9. Education: "**training be provided at all levels of medical education**... undergraduate, graduate, and attending"; content: "foundational concepts of AI; the ethics; clinically effective and appropriate uses; and the risks and unintended consequences, including its effect on health disparities"; the independence clause: "Training should ensure that **physicians remain able to make appropriate clinical decisions independently, in the absence of AI decision support** should such technology become unavailable and more importantly, **for vigilance against errors in AI generated or guided decisions**."
10. Environment (unique in corpus): "**the environmental impacts of AI and their mitigation should be studied and considered throughout the AI cycle.**" Rationale: health sector = 8.5% of US GHG; "training just 1 AI model for NLP can emit **284,019.13 kilograms of carbon dioxide equivalent—nearly 5 times the lifetime emissions of the average American car**"; "dearth of standardized measures impedes our ability to address the potentially negative climate effects of AI."

## (d) Sophistication markers
- Technology: deep and current — ambient documentation/"digital scribes," transformers, LLMs (GPT-4 ~1T parameters), automation bias/complacency literature, feedback-loop model deterioration, MAUDE adverse-event epidemiology, Watson autopsy. The **anti-anthropomorphism argument with the hallucination→confabulation terminological debate** is the most linguistically self-aware passage in the corpus.
- Evidence: the strongest in the corpus — 90 references, heavy peer-reviewed base (Obermeyer/Science, JAMA, Nature, AHRQ review), plus quantitative FDA data. This is a literature-grounded policy paper, not a statement.
- Mandates: "must" appears in positions 2, 5, 7 (ethics alignment, safety priority, developer accountability, coordinated federal strategy) — but ACP mandates government/developers more than its own members. Physician-side duties are "should/reaffirms."
- Published exchange (comments + DeCamp/Sulmasy reply) engages privacy-as-dignity ("wrong without harm") — live scholarly debate appended.

## (e) Distinctives vs sibling frameworks
- Only framework with an **environmental/climate position** (#10) and with the **anti-anthropomorphism stance** (incl. challenging "hallucination" itself).
- Only one that explicitly **defers on generative AI** ("too early to comment") — a dated-by-publication stance its own 2026 successor abandons.
- Model labels/reporting checklists + assurance-laboratory network + Sec. 1557 enforcement: the most concrete federal-regulatory ask set in the corpus (rivals ACC's RFI but with broader machinery).
- Feedback-deterioration caveat (PDSA loops feeding model-informed decisions back into models) shows understanding no other US body displays.

## (f) Provisional classification
**cautious-informed** (leaning progressive) — risk-literate, superbly evidenced, balanced to the point of deliberate generative-AI deferral. The intellectual heavyweight of the clinical-ethics strand in 2024; its caution (not its structure) is what the field outgrew by 2026.
