# ACP — Ethics and Professionalism in Artificial Intelligence and Medical Practice (Annals, Sep 1, 2026)

## (a) Authority and authorship
- Authors: **Matthew DeCamp, MD, PhD; Lois Snyder Sulmasy, JD; Kyle E. Karches, MD, PhD** for the **ACP Ethics, Professionalism and Human Rights Committee (EPHRC)**. Methods: narrative review of peer-reviewed literature and guidelines (PubMed + online); reviewed by Board of Governors, Board of Regents, Councils of Early Career Physicians/Resident-Fellow Members/Student Members; **approved by Board of Regents April 14, 2026**. The announced sequel to the 2024 paper (ref 18 throughout). Peer-reviewed.
- Powers claimed: ethical guidance "at the point of care" — where it says consensus is missing: "To date, **200 AI ethics guidelines and 17 consensus statements have been released worldwide**... Although consensus around transparency, justice and fairness, beneficence and nonmaleficence, and privacy may be emerging, **ethical guidance for AI use at the point of care is lacking.**" Binds ACP members' ethics; complements (does not repeat) the 2024 governance asks.

## (b) Policy philosophy
- Posture: **relational humanism — the patient–physician relationship as the load-bearing structure**. Three guideposts: "**Relationality**" (practical reasoning per patient), "**Self-governance**" (professional self-regulation, independent judgment), "**Competence**" (expertise, equity obligation). Framing quotes:
  - "Patients entrust physicians to care for the whole patient, **which is a commitment AI cannot make**." Followed by a list of impossibilities: AI "**cannot be accountable to and for patients; fully appreciate individual patient values; relate data and information to patient values... engage in eye contact or therapeutic touch; facilitate trust, hope, or closure; form a therapeutic alliance; seek patient-centered outcomes; or evaluate real-world decisions under conditions of uncertainty** based on clinical experience and judgment."
  - Still "Better thought of as **augmented intelligence**, as a tool that provides assistance."

## (c) Complete clause inventory — four positions + key rationale clauses
**Position 1**: "**The patient–physician relationship is the core of health care. AI must be implemented clinically to support, not weaken, this relationship.**"
- "Ambient scribes, for example, show promise in reducing EHR burden... But at present, AI scribes **may introduce 'hallucinations,' reflect implicit or explicit bias, or unintentionally influence clinical decision making**."
- Attention: "**Deep, sustained focus on another person cannot be replicated by machines**"; "AI could diminish attention by creating ever-increasing distractions in risk scores, algorithmic output, and automated messages; or could support attention... by reducing administrative tasks."
- "**Writing a note can aid physician reasoning and judgment about diagnosis and treatment**... A human response to a patient message can express care, **which a chatbot can only mimic**."
- Time/access: "**Denials of coverage enabled by AI can necessitate time-consuming appeals**... Yet AI tools could counter AI-enabled denials. **Coverage decisions should be based on individual patient needs, not just AI aggregate population data. Potential efficiency gains from AI should yield better quality time with patients, not expectations that AI will enable shorter visits so that physicians can see more patients.**"
- Access/digital twins: "**Digital twins—AI-generated bots that can mimic voice and likeness—could undermine the patient–physician relationship**"; "some EHR chatbots **do not share patient interactions with the care team**"; "**A shift to AI-initiated care raises questions of fairness**... unless there are alternatives"; derm/radiology AI "should **not engender second-tier care** for some populations."
**Position 2**: "**Physicians and trainees must recommit to the competencies of caring and develop new competencies on the appropriate use of AI.**"
- Education: "using AI for education may be especially concerning for **less experienced physicians who may not identify a hallucination or inaccuracy** in AI output. **Learning critical reasoning in traditional ways may be necessary to effectively use AI tools.**"
- Deskilling: "Using AI tools can result in '**deskilling**'... **Deskilling is an ethical issue** because it can result in patient harm when automated systems err or fail, disrupt patient–physician relationships, and **threaten physician self-governance**"; "Individual physicians and trainees **must be attentive to deskilling**, advocating for skills essential for the caring art"; "Ideally, AI will lead to **upskilling**"; "Emerging frameworks emphasizing **AI-independent competencies and supervision** show promise."
- Caring: "**AI-generated experiences cannot replicate how an embodied and encultured patient participates in a history and physical examination**"; "they **cannot create a moral community** and support independent practical reasoning"; "**Role models who demonstrate the ethical use of AI while maintaining the competencies of caring are needed.**"
- New competencies: generative responses "vary with how a question is asked... and the algorithms a specific model uses to prioritize sources (**creating conflicts of interest if advertising or financial relationships influence content**)"; "Physicians **must develop 'prompt literacy'**"; "Critical appraisal of AI responses requires assessing **what AI says and the level of certainty**."
**Position 3**: "**When physicians use AI to support medical decision making, that use should respect and promote—not replace—physician-independent clinical judgment and recommendations based on the patient's individual needs and values.**"
- "because AI is trained using historical data, it could **narrow choice, locking individuals into past selves or manipulate decision making for financial gain**."
- "**The ability of AI to pass a licensing examination cannot be confused with real-world medical competence, commitment, and professional discretion.**" — direct engagement with the USMLE/ChatGPT issue.
- Transparency proportionality: "transparency **does not require disclosing and documenting every detail of every AI model**. Physicians should tailor the information disclosed... **Nevertheless... erring on the side of disclosure. For instance, a physician using ambient note taking should disclose that use and basic information about data privacy.**"
- "AI '**nutrition labels**' show promise... when basic features of AI models are clearly presented and **updated regularly or dynamically in real time, as models change**."
- "**Without flexibility, disclosure and documentation requirements may inadvertently reinforce AI fear.**"
- Explainability realism: "many patients and physicians **would choose a model that is more accurate over one that is 'explainable'**."
- Responsibility: "**Complexity or inexplicability does not mean lack of responsibility. Physician responsibility to the patient does not change when AI technology is involved** any more than when another human is involved"; "Keeping a physician 'in the decision-making loop'... **is not a panacea**"; "'**Meaningful human control**'... may better conceptualize physician involvement."
- Autonomy clause: "fully autonomous AI is possible. **Caution is in order... Autonomous AI use should be rare, limited to low-risk, low-complexity decisions, and always allow for bringing a clinician into the loop.**"
**Position 4**: "**Physicians, in coordination with clinics, hospitals, and health systems, must seek to identify and mitigate the effects of algorithmic bias in the use of AI, including by addressing root social and structural causes.**"
- "the faulty perception that algorithms are inherently neutral make addressing AI bias particularly important"; "**Rapidly evolving federal rules and state laws may hold health care–covered entities responsible** for addressing possible discrimination in algorithms."
- "**Mitigating bias in AI will require more than weighting or adjusting data and/or models after the fact or creating simulated data. It requires moving upstream to address health inequity and the social determinants of health.**"
- Fairness theory: "the **impossibility theorem of algorithmic fairness** suggests that under real-world conditions, **all statistical definitions of fairness cannot be met simultaneously**... the ethical obligation shifts to **ensuring relevant tradeoffs are identified and examined honestly and transparently**."
- Practical warning: "**Not using AI for certain groups of patients could deny them the benefits of AI and, if the AI model continues to learn from this behavior, create a negative feedback loop that reduces AI performance for those patients.**" (selective non-use penalty — sophisticated).
- Metrics: "**Urgent attention is needed to develop diverse AI fairness metrics**"; cites HEAL framework "examines not just whether an AI model does no harm or improves all groups equally ('a rising tide lifts all boats')... the effect of AI on **underlying health disparities**"; "Validated fairness metrics can be incorporated into AI nutrition labels."
- Governance: "**Limitations in current FDA review of AI-enabled devices (including lack of clinical performance data) make local governance essential**"; "Decisions about managing ethical issues **should be calibrated to the riskiness of a particular clinical use; with greater risk should come greater obligations** about transparency, disclosure, fairness."
- Environmental stewardship: "current training and use of AI models require, for example, **substantial electricity and water consumption**; **Locating AI data centers in socially vulnerable communities raises issues of harm to individuals and health equity**."
- "**Diverse, multidisciplinary teams... are likely to make better decisions... Physicians should lead these efforts**... robust and authentic patient and community engagement."
- Conclusion anchor: ethics "based in '**a code of ethics and a duty of service that, in medicine, puts patient care above self-interest**,' and medicine's societally granted privilege of **self-regulation**."

## (d) Sophistication markers
- Technology: the most philosophically sophisticated document in the corpus — deskilling with 2025 Lancet colonoscopy evidence, ambient-scribe literature (DAX cohort studies), hallucination (name-checked despite 2024's terminological caution), digital twins, LLM advertising conflicts of interest, prompt literacy, dynamic nutrition labels, selective-non-use feedback loops, impossibility theorem of fairness (Kleinberg et al.).
- Assessment-validity: the licensing-exam clause connects to the NBME/USMLE thread.
- Evidence: 78 references, current through 2026, including empirical deskilling studies and energy-consumption literature.
- Mandates: physician-side "must"s (positions 2 and 4) — the duties land on ACP's own members, unlike the 2024 paper's government-directed asks.

## (e) Distinctives vs sibling frameworks
- The **point-of-care ethics gap** framing (200 guidelines, no bedside guidance) positions ACP as doing what no one else is.
- Guidepost architecture (relationality/self-governance/competence) is genuinely novel — no other framework theorizes physician involvement as "meaningful human control" vs "in-the-loop" or defends non-explainable-but-accurate models.
- Sharp differences from its own 2024 paper: now embraces hallucination vocabulary, ambient scribes, generative AI fully — the generative-AI deferral is gone.
- Digital twins, AI-vs-AI coverage appeals, data-center environmental justice: freshest topical coverage in the corpus.

## (f) Provisional classification
**pioneer** — the intellectually leading edge of the corpus: philosophically grounded, evidence-current, self-revising (explicitly corrects/refines its 2024 predecessor), with genuinely novel normative content (deskilling-as-ethical-issue, autonomous-AI rarity clause, selective-non-use warning). Where FSMB leads regulation and NBME leads measurement, ACP 2026 leads ethics.
