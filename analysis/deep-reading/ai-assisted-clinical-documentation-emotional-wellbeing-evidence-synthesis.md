# AI-assisted clinical documentation and healthcare workers' emotional wellbeing: scoping review (Frontiers in Psychology, 2026)

## Research question
What types of AI documentation tools have been studied, and what favorable/adverse/mixed findings on healthcare workers' emotional wellbeing at work have been reported?

## Methods
JBI scoping review, PRISMA-ScR, OSF-registered protocol; PubMed/Web of Science/Embase/CINAHL/PsycINFO to Mar 2026; dual screening (κ=0.80/0.82); MMAT appraisal; proximal–relational–distal outcome model; no meta-analysis; funding/vendor/COI extraction.

## Sample
35 studies (2023–2026; 20 in 2025), 31 US; designs: 8 mixed-methods, 6 QI, 5 before-after, 5 RCTs, 4 qualitative, 3 pilots, plus cross-sectional/observational/cohort. 30 of 35 evaluated ambient AI scribes (DAX/DAX Copilot, Abridge, Nabla, Ambience, Lyrebird). Populations: 17 mixed HCW, 14 physicians, 2 resident-only.

## Key findings (with counts)
- Direction of findings: 23/35 predominantly favorable, 12/35 mixed, 0 predominantly adverse.
- By domain: documentation burden, cognitive load, work satisfaction, patient connection predominantly favorable; burnout, professional fulfillment, stress/frustration, emotional burden, turnover mixed.
- Central thesis: burden is REDISTRIBUTED, not eliminated — production burden falls but AI-management burden (reviewing drafts, correcting inaccuracies, restoring clinical voice) rises.
- MMAT: 4 studies few concerns, 21 some, 10 substantial.
- Commercial entanglement: pervasive vendor provision/investment/author-affiliation across the evidence base; risk of publication/implementation bias toward favorable findings.
- Subjective–objective discordance: clinician-perceived gains often precede or exceed objective EHR-metric changes.

## What it says about WHO leads policy
Implicit: implementation quality, governance, and fit — set by health systems, not vendors — determine wellbeing outcomes. Warns against health systems implementing for throughput. Notes evaluation frameworks (note quality, harms, patient experience) are institutional responsibilities. No direct statement on medical school policy.

## Limitations
Scoping review (no effect estimates); heterogeneous outcome constructs; US-dominated, ambient-scribe-dominated; short follow-up; gray literature excluded (misses newest implementation evidence — explicitly flagged); disclosures not independently verifiable.

## Corroboration/contradiction with our corpus
Corroborates our health-systems corpus (UCSF, Duke, Kaiser ambient-scribe deployments): favorable proximal outcomes, unresolved accountability. The "burden redistribution" thesis matches what our GME policy files (e.g., OHSU GME-48, UChicago IM residency) try to address via mandatory review/sign-off requirements. Its explicit gap — no systematic inclusion of institutional policy documents — is precisely the gap our 378-source corpus fills: we hold the actual governance texts this review says are missing from the evidence base.
