# AI in Family Nursing: Evidence-Informed Policy Framework (International Nursing Review, 2026)

## Research question
What policy framework should govern safe, equitable integration of AI into family nursing?

## Methods
Non-empirical evidence-informed policy analysis; structured search of PubMed/CINAHL/IEEE + policy documents to Jun 2025 (updated Jul 2026); narrative synthesis by 3 authors; Japan as illustrative context.

## Sample
N/A (policy literature synthesis; ~27 references).

## Key findings (with counts)
- Four opportunity areas: proactive risk identification, personalized intervention design, extended digital support (e.g., breastfeeding chatbot RCT showing self-efficacy gains), enhanced family-level outcome evaluation.
- Five policy domains: ethical/data governance (family-level consent, role-based access, bias monitoring), workforce development (AI literacy in curricula/CPD), interdisciplinary co-design (with families, low-digital-access groups), adaptive regulation & accountability (locked vs adaptive systems, adverse-event reporting, override procedures), sustainable funding/reimbursement (payment for nurse-led digital follow-up).
- Anchors: WHO 2021/2023/2025/2026 AI guidance, EU AI Act, ICN 2023 digital-health position, American Academy of Nursing 2026 position statement.
- Core stance: AI outputs are "prompts for professional assessment, not decisions."

## What it says about WHO leads policy
Explicit multi-actor assignment: regulators + health ministries (standards, funding), nursing schools + professional bodies (workforce), health organizations (staged implementation), nursing leadership (co-governance). Notable for naming FUNDING/reimbursement as a policy domain — absent from medical-education policy literature.

## Limitations
Non-systematic; family-nursing-specific empirical evidence thin (inferences from adjacent fields); Japan illustration not generalizable; framework unevaluated.

## Corroboration/contradiction with our corpus
Nursing corpus parallel: same governance architecture we see in medical-education policies (literacy, oversight, equity, monitoring) but adds reimbursement — a structural driver our corpus barely touches. Confirms the pattern that professional bodies (ICN, AAN) lead with position statements while institutional policy lags — mirroring AMA/AAMC vs medical schools.
