# AI Literacy in UME: Competency-Based Interpretive Framework (Frontiers in Medicine, 2026)

## Research question
How should AI literacy be bounded for undergraduate learners and translated into teachable, observable, assessable outcomes?

## Methods
Focused conceptual narrative review; PubMed + ERIC, Jan 2020–Apr 2026; 94 records → 66 screened → 40 full-text → 30 publications; interpretive synthesis via CBME lens; no formal quality appraisal (conceptual purpose); "conceptual sufficiency" standard.

## Sample
30 publications (largest share 2025): frameworks, reviews, Delphi/consensus, curriculum reports, empirical readiness studies.

## Key findings (with counts)
- Field has moved from "whether to include AI" to "how to bound and assess AI literacy."
- Five recurring competency domains: (1) foundational AI knowledge, (2) applied clinical interpretation and use, (3) data literacy and critical appraisal, (4) ethics/law/professional responsibility, (5) human–AI collaboration and professional formation.
- Undergraduate boundary: students should NOT be trained as AI engineers or system governors; supervised interpretation, not deployment leadership.
- Empirical anchors: 148 final-year students — median 56% correct evaluation of LLM responses; RCT (n=111) — plausible-but-misleading AI explanations reduced diagnostic accuracy.
- Translation pathway: domains → learning outcomes → integration into existing curricula (not parallel AI course) → contextualized tasks → programmatic assessment; mapping to AI-PACE framework provided; introduces "Cognitive Integrity Threshold" construct.
- Assessment is the weakest link: literature supports targets, not validated instruments; readiness surveys ≠ competence.

## What it says about WHO leads policy
Institutions carry curricular translation; no national curricular mandate exists; faculty development positioned as the binding constraint. Assessment/validity responsibility sits with schools and, prospectively, boards.

## Limitations
Narrative not systematic; terminological heterogeneity (AI literacy vs competence vs readiness); no validated assessment evidence; framework itself a "design proposition."

## Corroboration/contradiction with our corpus
Confirms our corpus's curriculum-vs-policy imbalance: our school policies govern conduct but almost none specify assessable AI-literacy outcomes. The five-domain structure gives us a coding rubric to test how many of our 100+ school policies map onto each domain (our hypothesis: domains 1 and 4 dominate; 2, 3, 5 rare). The 56%-correct-evaluation datum quantifies the verification skill gap our policies assume away.
