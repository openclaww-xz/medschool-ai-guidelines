# AI in UK Medical Education: A Framework for Curriculum Reform (JMIR Medical Education, 2026)

## Research question
What framework should govern AI integration into the UK medical curriculum, given central regulation (GMC) and NHS funding structures?

## Methods
Viewpoint with structured narrative synthesis; PubMed/MEDLINE/Embase/Cochrane/Scopus 2015–2025; international case-study analysis (Stanford, Mayo, Toronto, CUHK) mapped to UK regulatory context; competencies mapped to GMC Outcomes for Graduates.

## Sample
N/A (synthesis; UK survey anchors: 432/484 (88%) students rate AI important with little formal instruction; 92% (193/210) trainees say AI training insufficient; 88% (916/1041) UK undergrads use genAI, up from 53% in 2024, but only 36% report formal training; faculty: 91% positive but only 12% "very familiar"; Alan Turing/GMC survey: 99% (814/822) of physicians would prioritize own judgment over AI).

## Key findings (with counts)
- Three international models: elective-specialist (Stanford; flexible but inequitable), postgraduate fellowship (Mayo; no undergraduate baseline), fully embedded longitudinal (Toronto, CUHK; equitable but structurally demanding).
- UK structural constraints: GMC central regulation means curricular reform must align with national graduate outcomes (unlike decentralized US); NHS fixed budgets; uneven digital infrastructure risking a digital divide between analog and flagship trusts.
- KEY GOVERNANCE FACT: "no ethical framework from the GMC or NHS guiding AI use in UK medical education" exists; HEE's DARTE program exists but is not widely implemented.
- Proposes phased framework: needs assessment → competency mapping to GMC outcomes → embedded (not standalone) curriculum → assessment integration (OSCE AI stations, appraisal items) → tiered faculty development → progressive scale-up with 2–3-year evaluation.
- Ethics: FUTURE-AI consensus (117 experts, 50 countries, BMJ 2025) as the trustworthiness anchor; institutions must distinguish legitimate AI-assisted learning from misconduct.

## What it says about WHO leads policy
In a centrally regulated system the regulator (GMC) is the necessary leader — the paper's core argument is that without GMC/NHS framework alignment, institutional initiative cannot scale. Good Medical Practice update already requires professional accountability for AI-informed decisions. Direct contrast with US decentralized model where schools lead.

## Limitations
Viewpoint; no new empirical data; UK-specific transferability; case-study selection criteria soft.

## Corroboration/contradiction with our corpus
Confirms the regulator vacuum is international, not just US (LCME): GMC also has no AI-specific education framework — supporting our corpus-wide finding that accreditors are the last movers in every system studied. The adoption/training gap (88% use vs 36% trained) quantifies the policy-implementation lag we observed qualitatively. Useful contrast case for our synthesis: national-curriculum systems (UK) vs institutional-policy systems (US).
