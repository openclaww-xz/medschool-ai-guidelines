# AMA — Augmented Intelligence Development, Deployment, and Use in Health Care (Board report with adopted HOD policy, November 2024)

## (a) Authority and authorship
- AMA Board of Trustees report (BOT 15-A-24 lineage), built on **advocacy principles approved by the Board in Nov 2023, developed by the Council on Legislation (COL)**, "vetted among multiple AMA business units," refined with "several medical specialty societies that have an expertise in AI" and outside experts. **Policy adopted by the House of Delegates November 12, 2024** as new HOD policy (supplementing H-480.940, H-480.939, D-480.956, H-480.935, Privacy Principles).
- Powers claimed: AMA's central advocacy charter — this is the master policy text from which AMA lobbies Congress, agencies, and states. Binds AMA action; addressed to government (Congress/FDA/FTC/OCR/ASTP-ONC/CMS), developers, health care organizations, payors, and physicians.

## (b) Policy philosophy
- Posture: **regulated-adoption — "voluntary compliance is not sufficient"**. Framing quotes:
  - "these technologies must be designed, developed, and deployed in a manner that is **ethical, equitable, responsible, accurate, transparent, and evidence-based**" (repeated as the report's refrain).
  - "the federal government has largely defaulted to public 'agreements' representing promises by large AI developers... **Voluntary agreements or voluntary compliance is not sufficient.**"
  - "it is critical that physicians engage only with AI that satisfies **rigorous, clearly defined standards** to meet the goals of the quadruple aim, advance health equity, prioritize patient safety, and limit risks to both patients and physicians."
- The report's own theory of change: transparency → assigned accountability → managed liability → trustworthy adoption.

## (c) Complete clause inventory — the adopted policy (9 sections, verbatim key clauses)
**1) General Governance**
- "**Health care AI must be designed, developed, and deployed in a manner which is ethical, equitable, responsible, accurate, transparent, and evidence-based.**"
- "Use of AI in health care delivery **requires clear national governance policies**... Development of national governance policies should include interdepartmental and interagency collaboration."
- "**Compliance with national governance policies is necessary**... Voluntary agreements or voluntary compliance is not sufficient."
- "AI systems should be developed and evaluated with a specific focus on **mitigating bias and promoting health equity**..."
- "**Health care AI requires a risk-based approach** where the level of scrutiny, validation, and oversight should be proportionate to the overall potential of disparate harm..."
- "**Clinical decisions influenced by AI must be made with specified qualified human intervention points** during the decision-making process. A qualified human is defined as a licensed physician with the necessary qualifications and training to independently provide the same medical service without the aid of AI. As the potential for patient harm increases, the point in time when a physician should utilize their clinical judgment... should occur earlier in the care plan. With few exceptions, there generally should be a human in the loop... capable of intervening or overriding the output of an AI model." — the earliest-mover formulation of what ACR later operationalizes as "qualified end-user" (same definition nearly verbatim).
- "Health care practices and institutions **should not utilize AI systems... that introduce overall or disparate risk that is beyond their capabilities to mitigate**."
- "Medical specialty societies, clinical experts, and informaticists are best positioned and should identify the most appropriate uses of AI... and set the standards for AI use in their specific domain."
**2) When to Disclose**
- "Decisions regarding transparency and disclosure... should be based upon a **risk- and impact-based approach**... The need for transparency and disclosure is greater where the performance of an AI-enabled technology has a greater risk of causing harm."
- "**When AI is used in a manner which impacts access to care or impacts medical decision making at the point of care, that use of AI should be disclosed and documented to both physicians and/or patients in a culturally and linguistically appropriate manner. The opportunity for a patient or their caregiver to request additional review from a licensed clinician should be made available upon request.**"
- "**AI tools or systems cannot augment, create, or otherwise generate records, communications, or other content on behalf of a physician without that physician's consent and final review.**" — the consent-and-final-review rule for physician-attributed content.
- Payor AI "use of those systems or programs **must be disclosed** to impacted parties."
- Patient-facing AI "should be clearly disclosed to patients **at the beginning of the encounter**"; AI-generated patient-facing content "should be disclosed or otherwise noted within the content."
- Conceptual hygiene: "**disclosure should not be confused with informed consent**" — disclosure denotes "the 'when' and not the 'what'"; documentation vs disclosure distinguished.
**3) What to Disclose** — the 11-item mandatory vendor disclosure list: regulatory status; consensus standards/clinical guidelines used; problem formulation + instructions for use; intended population/setting; limitations/risks "including possible disparate impact"; how impacted populations were engaged; **training data detail (provenance, size/completeness, timeframes, diversity, labeling accuracy)**; **validation evidence: clinical expert validation, "constraint to evidence-based outcomes and mitigation of 'hallucination'/'confabulation' or other output error," algorithmic validation, "external validation processes for ongoing evaluation of the model performance, e.g., accounting for AI model drift and degradation," bias mitigation, performance at peer institutions, post-market surveillance**; data use policy (privacy/security/protected populations); maintenance incl. "any use of active patient data for ongoing training"; **"composition of design and development team, including diversity and conflicts of interest, and points of physician involvement and review."** Plus: "Purchasers and/or users (physicians) should carefully consider whether or not to engage... if this information is not disclosed."
**4) Generative AI**
- "Generative AI should: (a) **only be used where appropriate policies are in place** within the practice or other health care organization...; and (b) follow applicable state and federal laws..."
- Governance policies must mitigate risks of: "incorrect or falsified responses; lack of ability to readily verify the accuracy of responses"; "training data set limitations that could result in responses that are out of date"; "lack of regulatory or clinical oversight"; "bias, discrimination, promotion of stereotypes, and disparate impacts"; data privacy; cybersecurity; physician liability.
- "**Data and prompts contributed by users should primarily be used by developers to improve the user experience and AI tool quality and not simply increase the AI tool's market value or revenue generating potential.**" — anti-free-training clause.
- Clinicians "should be prepared to counsel patients on the limitations of AI-driven medical advice."
**5) Physician Liability**
- "liability and incentives should be aligned so that the individual(s) or entity(ies) **best positioned to know the AI system risks and best positioned to avert or mitigate harm** do so..."
- "**Where a mandated use of AI systems prevents mitigation of risk and harm, the individual or entity issuing the mandate must be assigned all applicable liability.**"
- "**Developers of autonomous AI systems with clinical applications (screening, diagnosis, treatment)... must accept this liability with measures such as maintaining appropriate medical liability insurance** and in their agreements with users."
- **Gag clause**: "Health care AI systems that are subject to non-disclosure agreements concerning flaws, malfunctions, or patient harm (referred to as gag clauses) **must not be covered or paid and the party initiating or enforcing the gag clause assumes liability for any harm.**"
- "When physicians do not know or have reason to know that there are concerns about the quality and safety of an AI-enabled technology, **they should not be held liable** for the performance of the technology."
- Report-level analysis: negligent selection liability (tools outside intended use/population); "AI-transparency is of critical importance"; anticipates "questions regarding liability when physicians **fail to use AI**."
**6) Data Privacy**
- Entity responsibility: "Entities, e.g., AI developers, should make information available about the intended use... Individuals should know how their data will be used or reused."
- "**Individuals should have the right to opt-out, update, or request deletion of their data from generative AI tools.** These rights should encompass AI training data..."
- "**Generative AI tools should not reverse engineer, reconstruct, or reidentify** an individual's originally identifiable data... Preventive measures should include... **secure enclaves, federated learning, and differential privacy.**"
- Report-level: "'HIPAA-compliant' generative tools rely on **antiquated notions of deidentification**... data can easily be reidentified. Rather than aiming to make LLMs compliant with HIPAA, **all health care AI-powered generative tools should be designed from the ground up with data privacy in mind.**"
**7) Cybersecurity**
- "**AI systems must have strong protections against input manipulation and malicious attacks.**"
- "Entities... should regularly monitor for **anomalies or performance deviations**"; breach duty to individuals; user education on "AI cybersecurity fundamentals."
- Report-level threat taxonomy: **model stealing attacks, data poisoning, adversarial attacks**, AI-operated ransomware/phishing; Change Healthcare ransomware case study.
**8) Misinformation**
- "AI developers should ensure transparency... by disclosing how their models are trained and the sources of their training data."
- "**Algorithms should be developed to detect and flag potentially false and misleading content before it is widely disseminated.**"
- "Developers of AI should have mechanisms in place to allow for reporting of mis- and disinformation."
- "Academic publications and journals should establish clear guidelines... requiring the disclosure that AI was used in research methods... **requiring the exclusion of AI systems as authors**, and should outline the responsibility of the authors to validate the veracity of any referenced content generated by AI."
- Report-level: hallucination/"confabulation," **falsified references**, fraudulent literature generation, **retracted papers persisting in LLM training data**.
**9) Payor Use**
- "Payors should **only** use automated decision-making systems to improve or enhance efficiencies... Automated decision-making systems should **never create or exacerbate overall or disparate access barriers**... should not replace the individualized assessment of a patient's specific medical and social circumstances... **Payors should always make determinations based on particular patient care needs and not base decisions on algorithms developed on 'similar' or 'like' patients.**"
- Denial review rule: "Any automated decision-making system recommendation that indicates limitations or denials of care... **should be automatically referred for review to a physician** (a) possessing a current and valid non-restricted license... and (b) be of the same specialty as the physician who typically manages the medical condition... Prior to issuing an adverse determination, the treating physician must have the opportunity to discuss the medical necessity of the care directly with the physician who will be responsible for determining if the care is authorized."
- Audits: "Payors... should be required to engage in **regular system audits**... make statistics regarding systems' approval, denial, and appeal rates available on their website... with patient population demographics."
- Report-level evidence: Cigna/ProPublica 300k denials at "1.2 seconds of review"; nHPredict class actions ("90 percent of the tool's denials were faulty").
**Regulatory landscape analysis** (report body): 882 FDA AI/ML devices (May 2024); Pre-Cert tabled; FDA/Health Canada/UK transparency principles non-binding; Section 1557 final rule "reasonable efforts" duty (which "the AMA strongly opposed" as new physician liability); ASTP/ONC Predictive DSI transparency regime (2025) — "still the EHR developer that is regulated with few requirements on the AI developer itself."

## (d) Sophistication markers
- Technically excellent: hallucination AND confabulation (both named, echoing the ACP terminological debate), model drift/degradation, data poisoning, model stealing, differential privacy, federated learning, reidentification of "HIPAA-compliant" data, falsified LLM references, retracted-paper contamination.
- Evidence: survey data (9/10 patients; 83% physicians cyberattacked), ProPublica/STAT investigations, court cases, Obermeyer. Trade-press + peer-reviewed mix, all cited.
- Mandates: many "must"s, including the strongest vendor-liability regime in the corpus (autonomous-AI developers must accept liability + insurance; gag clauses punished; mandated-use liability assignment).
- Distinctive power analysis: precise regulatory-gap map (who has authority, what's missing) — the best "state of federal AI regulation" briefing in the corpus.

## (e) Distinctives vs sibling frameworks
- **The payor section is unique** — no other national body governs algorithmic claims denial (D-480.956 lineage); the specialty-matched physician review rule for denials is the corpus's most detailed procedural mandate.
- The disclosure/documentation/consent conceptual triage and the when-vs-what distinction resolve the exact ambiguity ACP 2026 later navigates; both cite each other's lineage (AMA quotes ACR and ACP positions here).
- Vendor disclosure list (11 items) anticipates/synchronizes with ACR-SIIM's IFU+ requirements; AMA and ACR effectively co-developed the "qualified human/qualified end-user" definition.
- Consent-and-final-review rule for physician-attributed AI content = the ambient-scribe governance clause others state more softly.

## (f) Provisional classification
**pioneer** — the most comprehensive single policy text in the corpus: covers governance, disclosure, generative AI, liability, privacy, cybersecurity, misinformation, and payors with mandate-grade language, current technical vocabulary, and a real liability-allocation theory. Where FSMB leads the regulator strand and ACP the ethics strand, this document makes AMA the leader of the advocacy/standards strand.
