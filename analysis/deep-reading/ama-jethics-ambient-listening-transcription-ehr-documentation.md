# Ambient Listening/Transcription in EHR Documentation: AMA Journal of Ethics (2025)

## Research question
Case commentary: how do ambient AI documentation tools affect informed consent, trust, and the patient–clinician relationship, and what should best practice be?

## Methods
Peer-reviewed ethics case commentary (fictional case of patient XY declining recording then capitulating); legal analysis (informed consent doctrine, HIPAA, wiretap laws, ONC HTI-1, California AB-3030); normative recommendations.

## Sample
N/A (normative/legal analysis; ~30 references; EU-funded).

## Key findings (with counts)
- Legal gaps: physicians likely have NO legal duty to disclose ambient AI use (not a "treatment"/"device" if transcription-only; informed-consent doctrine covers procedures); HTI-1 transparency binds IT developers, not physicians; CA AB-3030 covers only patient-facing generative-AI communications.
- Partial fill-ins: HIPAA (business-associate vendor), federal/state wiretap laws (criminal conviction precedent US v Hollern; civil Yockey v Salesforce).
- Consent-as-default problem: "most patients consent" framing, opt-out defaults, and power asymmetry convert refusal into friction; disproportionate effect on low health literacy, deference cultures, and patients with constrained provider choice.
- Hallucination/accent-bias risks (Hutiri & Ding speaker-recognition bias) can erode information-sharing (patients withhold stigmatizing disclosures).
- Best practices: consent videos explaining risks, staff Q&A, portal access to AI-generated notes + right-to-correct info, measured pre/post workload effects, honest default framing.

## What it says about WHO leads policy
Health systems and clinicians must self-impose what law doesn't require; explicit finding that regulation leaves disclosure to institutional ethics. AMA publishing venue itself signals professional-body moral leadership without legal force.

## Limitations
Case-based normative analysis; US-law specific; fictional case; no empirical data.

## Corroboration/contradiction with our corpus
Sharpens our health-systems corpus finding: most system policies we collected (UC Davis, Michigan, Hopkins) mandate clinician review but say little about patient consent processes — this commentary shows consent practice defaults to opt-out framing in reality (matches UK GP survey's 37% non-consenters). Fills the regulatory-vacuum explanation for why institutional policy is the only lever in the US — the same conclusion our national-framework run reached from the policy side.
