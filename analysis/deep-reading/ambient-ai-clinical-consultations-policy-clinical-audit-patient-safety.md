# Ambient AI in Clinical Consultations: Reframing Policy Around Clinical Audit and Patient Safety (Frontiers in Digital Health, 2026)

## Research question
Policy perspective: should ambient AI transcripts be governed as clinical-audit/patient-safety infrastructure rather than purely liability/privacy risk?

## Methods
Policy analysis (Perspective); GDPR/EU AI Act legal analysis; stakeholder table (regulators, vendors, patients, vulnerable populations, clinicians, health systems, auditors); WHO audit-and-feedback framework anchoring. Not empirical; cites authors' unpublished pilot.

## Sample
N/A (normative/policy argument); literature + law.

## Key findings (with counts)
- Claims: regulation "permits but does not supply" the clinical purpose for recording — purpose must come from health policy (WHO Global Patient Safety Action Plan 2021–2030).
- Proposal: minimum audit-ready transcript standard as procurement precondition — timestamped speech turns, speaker diarization, audio deletion after short verification window, pseudonymized transcript retention, WER reported and disaggregated by language/population.
- Governance requirements: independent audit body, stratified random case selection, version-anchored guideline criteria, second-reviewer adjudication, clinician right to contest/appeal, firewall between formative audit and discipline.
- Equity: WER disparities (Koenecke: 2x error for Black speakers) make aggregate accuracy reporting inadequate.
- COI disclosed: three authors hold equity in an AI clinical-audit company; one WHO staff member.

## What it says about WHO leads policy
Health policy bodies (WHO, regional procurement frameworks) should set the standard and make it a purchase condition; regulators (GDPR/AI Act) set constraints; vendors must redesign products; health systems run governance. Explicit multi-actor leadership assignment — WHO named as coordinator.

## Limitations
Policy argument, not validated; pilot is a preprint; jurisdiction-dependent legality; effects on clinician behavior unknown.

## Corroboration/contradiction with our corpus
Goes beyond our health-systems corpus: our collected health-system policies (UCSF, Hopkins, Michigan) govern use, but none treat transcripts as audit infrastructure — this proposes an affirmative purpose obligation, complementing the defensive posture we catalogued. The "regulation permits but doesn't supply purpose" framing parallels our US finding: AMA/WHO principles exist, but purpose-driven institutional policy is rare. WER-disaggregation demand matches our corpus's equity provisions (mostly aspirational in school policies).
