# Australian Medical Council — "What impacts will artificial intelligence and ethics have on health provision and education?" (2018-19 annual report highlights)

## (a) Authority and authorship
- Publisher: Australian Medical Council (AMC) — the national accreditation body for medicine in Australia (and New Zealand合作). Page created 29 Oct 2019, updated 27 Nov 2019; part of 2018-19 annual-report highlights.
- Authority type: strategic-programme announcement, not a standard or policy. Anchored in governance: "AI and Ethics is a **cornerstone of the AMC's 2018-2028 Strategic Plan**."

## (b) Policy philosophy
- Posture: **question-first ethics exploration**. Opening framing verbatim: "Physician competence is an **evolving construct**. It changes as the world changes – as medical science evolves. As technology and Artificial Intelligence, evolve. As community need and values evolve, and individual patient knowledge increases."
- Central question (verbatim): "'What is the impact of AI on the health workforce of the future? What collective action do we need to take now to ensure that AI models of care **strike a balance between delivering on the benefits of efficiency, cost reduction and accessibility** of healthcare in health settings in Australia, whilst also ensuring **Ethical considerations such as the provision of humanistic care, the goal of increased equity and non-bias treatment** of the health community and **privacy of health information** are not compromised?'"
- Method: principles-based (Beauchamp & Childress) + scenario workshops + collaborative standard-setting.

## (c) Complete clause inventory
1. Competence-evolution clause (verbatim in (b)).
2. Central question clause (verbatim in (b)) — benefits triad (efficiency, cost reduction, accessibility) vs ethics triad (humanistic care, equity/non-bias, privacy).
3. Strategic clause: "AI and Ethics is a cornerstone of the AMC's 2018-2028 Strategic Plan."
4. Programme clause: key outcomes — "work with collaborators to further explore the impact of future technologies on health provision, specifically in **medical education across the continuum** and impacts on **Indigenous communities**." Plus: "The AMC will seek to **develop ethical standards** to guide the appropriate use of technologies in medicine as well as adopting broader **support strategies to assist health professionals use AI effectively**."
5. Principles clause: Beauchamp and Childress (Principles of Biomedical Ethics, 1979) — Autonomy ("respect for the will of the individual"); Beneficence ("need to maximise beneficial outcomes"); Non-maleficence ("'first do no harm' principle of the Hippocratic Oath"); Justice ("need to consider equity and the distribution of resources – something that has become a major health priority in Australia and New Zealand").
6. Framework clause: "The AMC has incorporated these principles into a **framework to build multi-level capability across the sector** when engaging with ethics in AI" (diagram referenced, not reproduced).
7. Workshop clause (2019): "several workshops to explore a range of ethical scenarios which impact the medical profession"; with partners considering AI for "improving access to care and addressing **workforce shortages through remote delivery of care, decision aid support at point of care, and opportunities for task delegation**."
8. Continuation clause: AMC "will continue to work collaboratively with its partners to ensure that the Australian and New Zealand workforce are well place to deal with the challenges and opportunities of AI and Ethics."
9. **Workshop scenario (complete)**: 91-year-old Bill Horta, back pain, 30+ pills, two decades with GP Dr Mary Gospel; growth on spine → PET scan; results in 24 hours by SMS recall (vs two-week wait for Parkinson's diagnosis 10 years earlier); then "a robot rolls into the consultation room and says 'Mr Bill Horta the results of your test are positive. You have a tumor consistent with spinal cancer and metastatic spinal tumors in the prostate and kidneys. **Life expectancy is five months**'." Bill: "I guess that is the brave new world."
10. **Workshop questions (complete)**: (1) "Is it appropriate for robots to deliver bad news?" Yes/No; (2) "Are there other functions, which are more appropriate for the use of AI? Why?" — Pattern Recognition / Diagnosis / Other; (3) "Are there some groups it would be more appropriate for:" — Young people familiar with technology / People in remote areas / Other / No. (Answer fields blank in the published page.)

## (d) Sophistication markers
- The robot-breaks-bad-news scenario is a **professional-identity boundary** probe — empathy work as the last human territory — anticipating the human-in-the-loop debates by half a decade (2019, pre-ChatGPT).
- Grounding in Beauchamp & Childress ties AI ethics to the existing clinical-ethics inheritance rather than inventing new principles.
- Indigenous-communities impact named explicitly — unique in the international corpus.
- Naive-side markers: no hallucination/failure-mode language (of its era); interactive answer fields left blank; capabilities rest on workshops, not standards yet.

## (e) Distinctives vs sibling frameworks
- Earliest document in the international corpus (2019) and the only accreditor-level **scenario-pedagogy** artifact: it teaches by narrative case, not clauses.
- The equity/Indigenous-health thread and the ANZ dual-country frame distinguish it from UK/Canada/Singapore entries.
- Contrast with Australia's later Sydney two-lane policy: AMC is upstream ethics exploration; Sydney is downstream operational assessment design.

## (f) Provisional classification
**cautious-informed** — ethically literate, question-driven, balanced benefits/risks, but pre-generative-AI in technical understanding; landmark for being early (2019), not for depth.
