# AMEE Guide No. 178 — Artificial Intelligence in Health Professions Education Assessment (Masters, MacNeil, Benjamin, Carver, Nemethy, Valanci-Aroesty, Taylor, Thoma & Thesen; Medical Teacher, Jan 2025)

## (a) Authority and authorship
- Nine named authors from nine institutions across Oman, Canada, UAE, and the US (Baylor, McGill, U Toronto, Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons of Canada, Dartmouth Geisel): **Ken Masters (lead), Heather MacNeil, Jennifer Benjamin, Tamara Carver, Kataryna Nemethy, Sofia Valanci-Aroesty, David C.M. Taylor, Brent Thoma, Thomas Thesen**. Peer-reviewed AMEE Guide (Medical Teacher), received 9 Dec 2024, accepted 17 Dec 2024. Companion to Guide 158 (ethics) and Guide 172 (AGI) in an AMEE trilogy.
- Powers claimed: none; advisory guide for "all HPE stakeholders." But it is the international reference text for the assessment problem the US bodies (NBME, USMLE, LCME) treat territorially.

## (b) Policy philosophy
- Posture: **pedagogy-first embrace — redesign assessment rather than police it**. Framing quotes:
  - "we cannot simply plug AI tools into our current assessment methods and carry on as before: **our assessment methods need to be adjusted, some scrapped, and new methods introduced. Because AI is ever-advancing, this will not be a once-off exercise, and should be revisited repeatedly.**"
  - On the essay: "the aim is not to write the essay, and it never has been... **AI renders the standard take-home essay mostly irrelevant in gauging learning**" — but also "when we do this [specify minimal usage], **we are no longer preparing learners for the real world, in which they will be expected to use AI tools competently.**"
  - "Institutions **should provide explicit guidance on the acceptable AI use in assessments**, and foster an environment of critical inquiry and transparency."
  - Anti-detector doctrine: "AI detectors exist, but **should be avoided**."

## (c) Complete clause inventory (by the Guide's 14 sections)
**Theory base**
1. TPACK and PICRAT frameworks operationalized: PICRAT = "Passive... Interactive (e.g. Socratic type assessment)... Creative (learners create content using AI)"; RAT = "**Replacement** (e.g. MCQ generation, virtual patients), **Amplification** (improve assessment using AI... personalized learner feedback), **Transformation** (assessment that cannot be achieved without the use of AI...)."
2. Programmatic Assessment + CBME Core Components; Bloom's Taxonomy; UTAUT; MAIRS-MS readiness scale.
3. Modeling clause: "educators need to model best practices for AI use in assessment by **ensuring learner anonymity, reducing the impact of algorithm bias through transparency, being explicit about learner use of AI, supporting learner autonomy and modelling critical evaluation of AI outputs**."
**Human responses**
4. Anti-anthropomorphism (contra his own Guide 158's sentience excursion, grounded): "**AI systems, regardless of their performance, are not human, and do not possess gender, race or other human demographic traits. Projecting one's own demographic perceptions onto AI systems reflects personal biases**... they should always bear in mind that they are not interacting with a human, but a machine." (Also dismisses gendered-voice criticisms as anthropomorphic: "akin to assigning gender or racial characteristics to a computer mouse.")
5. Fear management: "'AI-detectors' are employed by both those fearing learner misuse and by those who overly-embrace AI" — extremes converge on misuse.
**Personalised/standardised summative assessment**
6. On-demand competency-based progression "aligning with their individual learning paths... rather than a set timeline"; caveat: "Solely focusing on rigidly-defined competencies may overlook the nuances of learning."
7. Psychometric standard: "Standardised tests like the USMLE rely on a rigorous validation process, rooted in **classical test theory (CTT) and item response theory (IRT)**... Questions generated by GenAI, particularly if created in real-time or without established psychometric validation, present challenges for reaching equivalent rigour in high-stakes settings."
8. "**LLMs are not yet sufficiently accurate to consistently generate reliable, high-stakes assessment items without human oversight**."
9. OSCE standardisation: "GenAI can improve the standardisation of clinical exams like the OSCE by utilising **speech-to-text transcription** to capture learner-patient interactions and analyse these based on established rubrics... **eliminates variability inherent between human raters**"; "fine-tuned LLMs that have been proven to score reliability across different sites can potentially enhance consistency and fairness."
10. Human-in-the-loop rule: "**Until LLMs consistently demonstrate reliability much better than human raters, however, a 'human-in-the-loop' approach remains essential. This requires human oversight in the generation and scoring of assessment items**."
11. Verdict: "high-stakes assessments should ensure that such items have been reviewed by humans, or continue to rely on well-established test items that have undergone extensive validation... **More rigorous and systematic research is needed before GenAI can be reliably used in high-stakes standardised assessments**... Currently, AI's most appropriate role... may lie in **low-stakes, formative assessments and adaptive learning environments**."
**Formative assessment best practices** (5-item list): align assessments with certification competencies; leverage adaptive tools (virtual patients adjusting complexity); provide timely actionable feedback; integrate reflective practice; use AI to facilitate collaborative learning (PeerWise). Limitations: "risk of feedback inconsistency, which can impact fairness"; AI "may not replace the need for nuanced, human feedback."
**The take-home essay** (the Guide's most original section)
12. Reaction history: "banning LLMs, insisting on rough drafts of essays written by hand..., resignations, or widespread use of 'AI-detection' software, followed by the outright failing of assignments"; Professor Mumm's 'X' grades: "'I have to gauge what you are learning[,] not a computer.'"
13. Closed writing tools critique: "still in their infancy, sometimes expensive, **relatively easy to trick, and enter a murky area of ethical learner tracking and over-surveillance, including their brainstorming and rough drafts**."
14. Redesign: "not to block or minimise AI and retain the essay, but rather to **embrace AI**... instead of seeing the learner as an author, **see the learner as a director**, using multi-modal AI research and input systems to create a range of multi-modal AI outputs... The essay becomes a component of a greater whole."
**Other assessment types**: authentic assessment per Constructivism and UDL; examples (annotated portfolio, virtual patients incl. Dartmouth's Patient Actor App, infographics). Best practices: design "higher-order thinking such as analysis, evaluation, and creation—**that GenAI alone cannot easily replicate**"; "clearly articulate how and when GenAI can be used considering any guidelines set by your institution"; "rubrics and exemplars that reflect GenAI-specific criteria... **requiring documentation of AI usage**. Promote critical evaluation, and verification of GenAI output." Equity caveat: "especially as **paid versions can create disparities**."
**AI as tutor**: ITS benefits (real-time feedback, cognitive-load reduction, performance prediction); examples (Virtual Operative Assistant, AI Patient Actor, custom GPTs whose "customisability allows the educator to personalise the responses, **reduce bias and hallucinations**"); limits: simulations "should be used for building foundational skills, rather than substitute hands-on experience"; "learner-analytics raises concerns about learner safety and privacy."
**AI as learner/more-knowledgeable-other**: Vygotskian framing; ChatGPT-as-standardized-patient exercises; LLM-debate analysis; Kung et al. USMLE performance cited; caveat: chatbot "is easier to ignore than a professor or physician at the patient's bedside" (dependency risk).
**Competency-based assessment**: three amenable components (tailored experiences, competency-focused instruction, programmatic assessment incl. "identifying learners ready for promotion or at-risk"); best practices: "ensuring the participation of learners in their development, being clear regarding the intended actions of AI tools, evaluating their quality, and **avoiding focus on factors that are unactionable or nonmodifiable by learners**"; limit: "the simple expedient of a human individual to ensure that the AI-generated conclusions for high-stakes events are correct."
**Difficult areas and ethical issues**
15. Source ethics: "sourcing original training data, usually by large-scale harvesting... **usually without acknowledgment or compensation**... tracing the source is not possible, leaving institutions reliant on systems that **may be deemed to be inherently unethical**."
16. Bias: "biases can make their way into these examination questions, resulting in examination questions that are either unrepresentative and/or **play into inaccurate stereotypes of structurally marginalised communities**"; also notes bias arising "including a legitimate decision by population groups to not share their information."
17. Faculty-use duties: "**Usage transparency: Just as institutions require learner transparency of GenAI usage, faculty usage of GenAI requires transparency.** Institutions may also wish to have an 'opt-out' possibility for learners and faculty"; "**Learner Privacy**... the unconsented use of learner data used as training data for the models. Depending upon the model, this problem can be mitigated by using **institutional licenses, APIs, or disabling such training through settings. If this is not possible, then fully informed consent is required**"; "**Response validation**: GenAI responses should be verified for accuracy, appropriateness, and adherence to established guidelines"; "Significant work should be done exploring the validity of the use of AI tools for higher-stakes... assessments"; "**Appeals: the appeals process must be adapted to address issues specifically related to inappropriate AI usage.**"
18. Detectors: "**should be avoided**" — "neither accurate nor reliable," defeated by "'humanising' tools like Twixify," "bias towards classifying outputs as human-written," and "**tend to disadvantage people writing in their second language by flagging their writing as AI-generated**"; "AI prohibition would discourage the use of an important new tool."
19. Learner-use duties: "Policies regarding learners' appropriate AI usage... are required"; "Guarding against 'gaming' the system... aim their assessment responses at those [AI idiosyncrasies], rather than providing their own solutions"; "**Access equity: equitable access must be provided; this includes training, licenses, and hardware**"; "**Dependency and over-reliance**... assessments should balance allowing for circumstances in which AI systems may not be available" (offline-competence testing).
20. Future/AGI: "the rate of change will increase beyond our current response, and there is a strong ethical imperative to **live with the change, direct the change, and even control the change**."
**Faculty development**: TPACK-based competency build (TK/TCK/TPK/TPCK enumerated); "**institutions should ensure access to GenAI tools for assessment, both for faculty and learners. Policies without access will be meaningless**"; explicitness about faculty use "especially in high-stakes assessments"; microlearning updates, communities of practice; "institutions should understand that their faculty will have different affinities and risk-acceptance"; micro/meso/macro levels.
**Acknowledgment and citing**
21. Two opposing citation philosophies (AI content "should be credited and cited... to avoid plagiarism" vs "AI is not human; it cannot write or take responsibility for its writing. Therefore **AI cannot be an author**"; outputs "cannot be perfectly re-created").
22. Acknowledgment standard: "in a separate paragraph, stating as a minimum, **that AI was used in the preparation of the work, the date, to what extent, for what task, and whether authors reviewed the final output**... use the correct words: created, enhanced, edited, reviewed, assisted."
23. Citation format (APA-modified): "OpenAI. (2024). ChatGPT (July 6 version) [Large language model]. https://chat.openai.com/chat."

## (d) Sophistication markers
- The deepest **assessment-validity** treatment in the corpus: CTT/IRT psychometric equivalence, black-box item generation, OSCE rater-variability replacement, fine-tuned scoring reliability, detector unreliability with non-native-writer bias (Liang et al.), obfuscation tools.
- Hallucination named and handled (custom-GPT mitigation); prompt engineering; custom GPTs; AGI horizon (Guide 172 cross-reference); surveillance ethics of closed writing tools.
- Evidence: ~98 references, heavily 2023–24, including RCTs and arXiv; balanced potential/limitations per topic.
- Mandates: consistent best-practice imperatives ("must," "should," "are required") but educational-guide authority only.

## (e) Distinctives vs sibling frameworks
- Where NBME guards the exam and LCME mandates a content clause, AMEE 178 **redesigns the assessment paradigm**: learner-as-director, essay-as-process, programmatic AI-augmented assessment. It treats the USMLE/ChatGPT question (Kung et al., Gilson et al. cited) with more methodological composure than the US institutions' own communications.
- The anti-detector position (with ESL-bias evidence) is the strongest anywhere in this corpus; no US body states it.
- Faculty-transparency symmetry ("just as institutions require learner transparency... faculty usage requires transparency") and the appeals-process-adaptation clause are unique.
- Citation/acknowledgment operational detail (date, extent, task, review status) exceeds anything from the national bodies.

## (f) Provisional classification
**pioneer** (assessment strand) — the most methodologically complete treatment of AI-and-assessment in the corpus: theory-grounded, evidence-current, prescriptive, and genuinely redesign-oriented rather than defensive. Its influence flows into US practice through IACAI (Masters is a member) and through the BEME/AMEE citation network the AMA hub indexes.
