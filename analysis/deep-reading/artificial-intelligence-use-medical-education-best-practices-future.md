# AI Use in Medical Education: Best Practices and Future Directions (Current Urology Reports, 2025)

## Research question
Narrative review: how is AI used in medical education, and what best practices should govern it?

## Methods
Narrative review (urology-anchored), no systematic search reported.

## Sample
N/A (narrative literature synthesis; ~49 references).

## Key findings (with counts)
- Use cases: standardized-exam prep (ChatGPT ≈ third-year student on USMLE; ChatGPT-4 passing EBU urology MCQs but weak in oncology/contextual/spatial tasks), VR surgical simulation with AI feedback (annotated urology video platforms), personalized learning/differential generation, quick-reference tool, AI scribe, research reading/summarization.
- Risks: hallucination (fabricated but plausible citations), misinformation, dependency/deskilling, plagiarism, privacy (FERPA; re-identification), inequity of access.
- Best-practice pillars: humanism, equity, integrity, privacy. Specific proposals: authors/students may use AI for outlines/first drafts with full verification responsibility; AI-free assessments to preserve critical thinking; third-party data storage; physician involvement in AI design.
- Notes CANGARU guidelines for AI use in research reporting; notes few and variable institutional rules on student AI coursework writing ("few rules, varying greatly between educational institutions").
- Notes B- to B grade of ChatGPT alone on an Operations Management exam.

## What it says about WHO leads policy
Argues institutional (school-level) policy must define writing-use boundaries since rules are "few" and inconsistent; physicians should co-design tools; journals establish disclosure norms. No national-body prescription — implicitly local leadership.

## Limitations
Narrative, non-systematic; urology perspective; no counts of its own; rapidly dating.

## Corroboration/contradiction with our corpus
The observation that "few rules vary greatly between educational institutions" (2025) is precisely what our corpus can now quantify — our school policies show convergence on disclosure+verification but real divergence on permitted-use tiers. Their outline/first-draft-allowed proposal matches the tiered-traffic-light schemes in our corpus (e.g., Penn, UVA). Adds the FERPA privacy angle, which most school policies in our corpus underaddress.
