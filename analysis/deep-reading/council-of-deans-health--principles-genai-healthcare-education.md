# Council of Deans of Health (UK) — Principles for the use of Generative AI in healthcare education (Jan 2026)

## (a) Authority and authorship
- Publisher: Council of Deans of Health — "represents over 100 university and further education faculties engaged in education and research for **nursing, midwifery, and the allied health professions**... Our members teach over 200,000 healthcare students at any one time." January 2026.
- Authorship chain fully named: Innovation in Education Strategic Policy Group chaired by **Dr Ruth Paterson** (Edinburgh Napier); 9-institution generative AI working group (10 named members); consultation with regulators (NMC, HCPC, General Osteopathic Council) and a service-user/caregiver group (Oxford Brookes, incl. "a retired software engineer"); industry conversations (JISC, Elsevier, GOsC). 19 member-submitted examples collected July–Oct 2025.
- Explicit status: "These principles are **guiding, not mandatory**, and should be interpreted alongside institutional policy, professional regulatory requirements, and programme level judgement."

## (b) Policy philosophy
- Posture: **balanced, principle-layer guidance for the non-medical health professions**. Foreword framing verbatim: "As leaders of professionally regulated programmes, our duty is **verifying that students' work is their own**... Academic integrity and professional standards must be upheld whilst fostering equitable access to AI-driven advancements."
- Authentic-assessment turn, endorsed: plagiarism concerns have "resulted in universities and colleges **rethinking how they assess students using more authentic methods such as oral examination, in-class tests, and group discussions**. This is welcomed but we must also **evaluate how effective students are in verifying information derived from AI tools**."
- Adoption datum cited: "In 2025, it was reported that **88% of higher education students were using generative AI** to support studies, assessments, knowledge and understanding" (HEPI).
- Position on tech: "Generative AI is considered the **fourth industrial revolution**"; embedding "must be 'real-world', sustainable"; UNESCO alignment invoked for "human rights, dignity, and well-being."

## (c) Complete clause inventory
**Methodology clauses**: staged approach — Scoping (June 2024 Summer Conference workshop) → Analysis (Jan 2025 working group of 9 institutions; expert consultation; AI-platform curation; literature review; "**Narrative meta-inference** synthesised the data... convergence... divergence... or expansion") → Concurrence (convergent + expansion data included, divergent excluded; consensus from SPG, professional regulators, service users). Regulator consultation split "sustainability, research and innovation" into two principles → **eight principles**. 10 themes emerged; 7 convergent/expansion.
**The eight principles (complete):**
1. **Professionally accountable**: "Regulation of AI is **essential** for appropriate and effective use and for maintaining public trust in healthcare education delivery." "Generative AI in healthcare education **must adhere to standards of professional bodies**." "Continuous regulatory oversight and monitoring should occur to incorporate technological developments." (Examples 1–2.)
2. **Sustainable**: "We need to be aware that **using AI has a large environmental impact and data centres contribute to the carbon footprint**." Counterweight: AI may enhance simulated practice with "'real world' learning which may reduce the need for travel, provide a more effective approach to healthcare education particularly in smaller professions (e.g., dietetics)." (Examples 3–4.)
3. **Innovative**: "Research should evaluate real world impact with **AI augmenting healthcare education delivery rather than driving it**." "Any innovation... should be responsible, minimising the risk of unintended consequences." "Learners should be taught how to use AI to enhance research and critical thinking skills." (Examples 5–6.)
4. **Ethical**: "**Human oversight in any use of AI is essential** in healthcare education." "Students should be provided with **equitable and reliable access** to AI." "AI... should prevent harm, reduce bias, uphold person centred care and dignity." (Examples 7–9.)
5. **Lawful**: "Data protection and privacy for all, **including vulnerable populations**, are considered when using AI." "Privacy handling is considered when using AI." (Examples 10–11: governance frameworks, operational policies, data protection standards.)
6. **Continuous upskilling**: "Users of AI are appropriately trained, educated, and kept up to date with most recent advances." "Higher education institutions should play a role in helping users and professionals to understand the use of AI." (Examples 12–13.)
7. **Collaborative and inclusive**: "**Interprofessional collaboration and co-design** should be at the centre of educational developments." "Regular dialogue with employers, placement providers, and service users supports shared understanding of emerging technologies, evolving professional roles and changing service contexts." (Examples 14–16.)
8. **Transparent**: "There is an expectation that users (students and academic) are **detailing their sources when using generative AI**." "This will be evidenced through a transparent approach to outline **how AI tools are developed and how generative AI has been accessed to develop academic work**." (Examples 17–19.)
**Conclusion clauses**: report targets "nursing, midwifery and AHP programmes"; self-declared obsolescence: "We do recognise that this report **will have an expiry date** as generative AI... developing extremely rapidly. We therefore recommend continuous ongoing sector collaboration"; commitment to "effective, transparent and human-centred" use.
**Further guidance list**: AHPF Principles for AI and education; Health AI Knowledge Hub; HCPC AI in education; HEPI "AI and the future of universities"; JISC principles; NHS AI Lab learning resources; Russell Group principles.
**References** (14): four-nation NHS digital strategies (England 10-year plan, Scotland Discover Digital, Wales DHCW 2024-30, NI 2022-2030); HEPI/Kortext survey; McDonald et al. 2025 institutional-policy analysis; Harvard metaLAB AI Pedagogy Project; UNESCO 2023 Ethics of AI; Guetterman/Creswell mixed-methods; Alnasser et al. 2024; Duke 2022 ("Deny, dismiss and downplay: developers' attitudes towards risk"); Lukkien et al. 2023; Zahlan et al. 2023; Fu & Weng 2024.

## (d) Sophistication markers
- **Sustainability as a co-equal principle** (with the data-centre carbon footprint named) — shared only with Amsterdam UMC's news guidance; no US document in the wider corpus carries it.
- Mixed-methods rigor: named analytic technique (narrative meta-inference with convergence/divergence/expansion), disclosed exclusion rule, regulator + service-user consensus rounds — the most methodologically elaborated principles development in the corpus.
- "AI augmenting healthcare education delivery **rather than driving it**" — a primacy clause (pedagogy first) stated as a governance boundary.
- Service-user co-design including a retired software engineer — lay+technical review combined.
- Developer-risk citation (Duke 2022 "Deny, dismiss and downplay") shows awareness that AI risk originates upstream of education.

## (e) Distinctives vs sibling frameworks
- Covers **nursing/midwifery/AHPs**, the largest health-professions student population in the UK — complements Medical Schools Council (doctors) and the joint regulators statement; together the three complete the UK professional-judgment model's education stack.
- 19 live institutional examples organized by principle — evidence-first principle publication (principles + use cases in one release).
- The "augmenting not driving" formula and environmental-impact clause are its unique doctrinal contributions to the corpus.

## (f) Provisional classification
**progressive-structured** — collaborative, methodologically rigorous, principle-based with worked examples; explicitly non-mandatory and self-expiring. Its weaker flank: thin technical specificity (no hallucination/detection/assessment-validity mechanics), delegated deliberately to regulators and institutions.
