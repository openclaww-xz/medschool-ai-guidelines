# Disclosure, Not Documentation: An Open Science Framework for GenAI in Scholarly Writing

## Research question
How should generative AI use in scholarly writing be governed — via detailed documentation requirements or via disclosure norms? The authors argue for reframing the debate from "documentation" (precise accounting of AI contributions) toward "disclosure" (transparent declaration aligned with open-science values).

## Methods
Conceptual/policy analysis building on open-science principles; review of emerging journal and funder AI policies; synthesis of an actionable disclosure framework for authors, reviewers, and editors.

## Sample
Not empirical — corpus of scholarly-publishing policies (journals, funders, COPE-type bodies) examined normatively.

## Key findings
- Detailed documentation of AI contributions is often infeasible (interleaved human–AI authorship, tool version drift) and can create false precision.
- Disclosure norms should be minimum, standardized, and verifiable; blanket bans or mandatory attribution of authorship to AI are unworkable.
- Editors/funders, not individual authors, should define disclosure standards — i.e., policy leadership sits with publishers and institutions.

## What it says about who leads policy
Publishers, journals, and funders lead scholarly-writing AI policy; institutions follow. Relevant to our corpus: medical schools face the same documentation-vs-disclosure choice for student work.

## Limitations
Normative argument, no empirical data on effects of disclosure regimes; pre-dates agentic AI workflows.

## Relation to corpus findings
Corroborates the Clin Teach policy-gap finding in spirit: where governance is needed, formal institutional policy lags practice. Our corpus of school-level policies can show which schools chose disclosure-style vs prohibition-style student AI rules — an empirical test this paper only theorizes.
