# Faculty Development and Institutional Readiness for AI in Health Professions Education: Narrative Review (Frontiers in Dental Medicine, 2026)

## Research question
What strategies drive AI transformation in higher/dental education — specifically faculty preparedness, barriers to adoption, assessment reform for academic integrity, institutional readiness, and stakeholder trust?

## Methods
Structured narrative review; PubMed, Scopus, Google Scholar, ScienceDirect; 2015–2025; English; Mendeley dedup, title/abstract then full-text screening by consensus; no formal risk-of-bias tool; narrative synthesis by thematic expertise.

## Sample
Literature 2015–2025 across dental and health professions education (exact included-study count not stated); international policy guidelines and organizational websites included.

## Key findings
- 80–86% of university students reported using AI tools for learning (cited surveys).
- 61.9% of educators NOT routinely using digital/AI tools in assessments, mainly from training/familiarity gaps.
- Scoping review cited (178 studies): only 12.4% of dental-AI studies explicitly addressed ethical concerns.
- Institutional readiness model: 4 interdependent domains — governance & policy; faculty capacity & curriculum integration; academic integrity & assessment reform; leadership & strategic change management.
- Policies vs guidelines distinction: policies are formal/authoritative/regulatory; guidelines flexible, department-level. "Paucity of clearly defined policies in many universities regarding acceptable use of generative AI in teaching, authorship, and assessment" — a direct policy-gap claim.
- AI effectiveness data cited: adaptive learning raised scores 15–30%; automated grading agreement 85–90% with human graders; ChatGPT-assisted PBL improved exam performance/interviewing; diagnostic AI accuracies (caries AUC 0.94; periodontal AUC >0.88).
- Equity: HIC–LMIC digital divide; FDI World Dental Federation policy statement on AI (bias reduction, training-data quality); Rokhshad et al. 11-principle checklist for dental AI.

## What it says about who leads policy
Explicit: UNESCO recommendations and EU AI Act as supranational anchors; national governments and accrediting bodies "must enforce regulatory compliance"; institutional governing bodies (with leadership, faculty, admin, learners represented) approve policies while departments issue guidelines; transformational institutional leaders drive change. Multi-level leadership — supranational → national → institution → department.

## Limitations (authors')
Narrative review, no risk-of-bias appraisal, heterogeneous studies; dental emphasis; many effectiveness claims imported from cited primary studies of variable quality.

## Relation to corpus findings
Strongly corroborates the Clin Teach policy-gap study: this review independently documents a "paucity of clearly defined policies" on acceptable GenAI use at many universities and treats governance/policy as the FIRST readiness domain. Our corpus can quantify that gap — how many schools have formal policies vs guidelines, at which governance level each was issued — precisely the descriptive empirical base this review lacks.
