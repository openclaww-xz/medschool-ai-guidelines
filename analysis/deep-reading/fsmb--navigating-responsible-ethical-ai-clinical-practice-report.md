# FSMB — Navigating the Responsible and Ethical Incorporation of Artificial Intelligence into Clinical Practice (adopted by House of Delegates, April 2024)

## (a) Authority and authorship
- Drafted by the **FSMB Ethics and Professionalism Committee 2023–24** — chair Mark B. Woodland, MS, MD (PA State Board of Medicine); 10 state-board members; subject-matter experts Eric Eskioğlu (Health Management Academy) and **Sara Gerke (Penn State Dickinson Law, health-law scholar)**; ex officio FSMB Chair Jeffrey Carter, Chair-elect Katie Templeton, CEO Humayun Chaudhry; staff Eric Fish (Chief Legal Officer), Frank Meyers, Mark Staz. **Tasked by the FSMB Chair**; adopted by the House of Delegates April 2024.
- Powers claimed: the pivotal jurisdictional claim — "Although medical boards are limited from directly regulating AI as a healthcare device or tool, **they are the only regulatory agency with the explicit authority to regulate physicians who use AI to provide care**." Recommendations addressed to state medical boards → translatable into licensure/discipline reality for all US physicians. Preceded by a 2019 FSMB/McDermott Will & Emery symposium and taskforce that concluded "AI should be thought of as a tool for medical practice."

## (b) Policy philosophy
- Posture: **principles-based regulation, anti-overreach, physician-accountability-first**. Framing quotes:
  - "state medical boards can promote the safe, effective, and ethical use of AI as a tool **to enhance, but generally not replace, human judgment and accountability** in medical practice."
  - "it is essential that state medical boards **avoid over-regulation and regulatory overreach** by attempting to regulate that which is not in their purview."
  - "attempting to regulate its specific applications in healthcare will prove ineffective as the regulatory process will not be able to keep pace with AI's technological advancement. As such, medical boards should instead focus on **governing the use of AI through established ethical principles**, including respect for patient autonomy, non-maleficence, beneficence, and justice."
  - Continuity doctrine: "The statements and recommendations offered in this report **apply existing and well-established regulatory considerations to new technologies**... Professional responsibilities and expectations of medical licensees remain the same; how they are fulfilled may differ."

## (c) Complete clause inventory
**Education (Section II)**
1. "A physician **has the duty to maintain the requisite skill and knowledge** to provide safe and effective health care."
2. "it is imperative that physicians remain attuned to developments in AI and strive to understand the benefits and risks it poses."
3. Double-edged competence: "Underappreciation of the ability of AI to improve healthcare delivery **may restrict a physician from practicing to the top of their license**... At the same time, **over-reliance on AI can lead to real harms in independent clinical thinking and critical decision making such as misdiagnosis, medical errors, dependence, and skill degradation.** This risk of harm increases in situations of algorithmic bias or where misinformation is present."
4. "medical education, at all levels, **should include an emphasis on advanced data analytics and use of AI** in a clinical setting."
5. "physicians **should regularly engage in accredited continuing medical education programs** designed to improve competence in understanding the application, benefits, and risks of AI."
**Accountability (Section III)**
6. "**State medical boards do not regulate tools or technologies, only the licensed physicians that use those tools.**"
7. "**the physician is ultimately responsible for the use of AI and should be held accountable for any harms that occur.**"
8. Risk-proportionality: "as AI tools perform functions that more closely model the practice of medicine, the risk to patients... generally increases. The appropriate level of regulatory scrutiny and accountability... should increase commensurately." (Fig. 1 risk gradient.)
9. "Physicians may consider AI as a decision-support tool that **assists, but does not replace, clinical reasoning and discretion**."
10. "Physicians **should understand the AI tools they are using** by being knowledgeable about their design, training data used in its development, and the outputs of the tool in order to assess reliability and identify and mitigate bias."
11. Rationale duty (following AI): "if a physician chooses to follow the course of treatment provided by an AI-generated response, then **they should be prepared to provide a rationale** for why they made that decision. **Simply implementing the recommendations of the AI without a corresponding rationale, no matter how positive the outcome may be, may not be within the standard of care.**"
12. Rationale duty (deviating): "they **should document the rationale behind the deviation** and be prepared to defend the course of action."
13. Documentation duty: "once it produces a result, the outcomes **cannot be ignored; there must be documentation** reflecting how it was or will be utilized."
14. "**failure to apply human judgement to any output of AI is a violation of a physician's professional duties.**" — the hardest single normative sentence in the corpus.
15. Black box: "Such 'black box' algorithms may still hold tremendous benefit for patients and **should not be disallowed outright**. While a licensee may not be able to explain in step-by-step fashion... **they should still be expected to offer a reasonable interpretation of how the AI arrived at a particular output** and why following or ignoring that output meets the standard of care."
16. Scribes/records: "physicians should be aware that the use of AI to generate medical records, **without proper oversight, may lead to inaccurate documentation and subsequent patient harm for which the physician will likely be accountable**." (Cites disciplinary data: record-keeping = 6% of actions 2015–19.)
17. PHI security: "Physicians should be aware of what security measures are in place... **Physicians retain their duty to review records created with AI** to ensure that the data captured is accurate and properly managed."
**Informed consent and privacy (Section IV)**
18. "A physician **must be able to independently explain** components of diagnosis and treatment options in order to fulfill their professional responsibilities relating to the informed consent process."
19. "**Informed Consent is not a list of AI-generated risks and benefits, but instead a meaningful dialogue and shared decision-making** between the physician and patient. AI may be used to assist in this process but **the ultimate responsibility rests with the physician**."
20. "physicians **should receive a patient's consent prior to application of a tool** to a patient's care."
21. "Physicians **should disclose to patients when and how AI is used** in their care and clearly communicate about the capabilities and limitations of their tools... including how they use and share any patient data."
22. "Physicians should also be prepared to disclose **how they used AI in their diagnosis and treatment planning**, discuss the continued role and responsibilities of the physician, and describe any **safeguards**... A lack of transparency... **may serve to highlight the physician's lack of understanding of how the AI tool works**."
**Equity and bias (Section V)**
23. "The principle of justice dictates that physicians **have a professional responsibility to identify and eliminate biases** in their provision of patient care, including those that may arise through biased AI algorithms."
24. "**Efforts must be made to ensure that all patients have equitable access** to the benefits of AI and that existing disparities are not further exacerbated."
25. "FSMB recognizes that it and its member medical boards have an interest in assisting other regulatory agencies and responsible developers of AI to **promote systemic standards that require disclosure of information about the training data set, such as race/ethnicity breakdown**, and further information about potential biases and risks."
26. "the FSMB **should join with other interested parties** to understand and resolve the issue of algorithmic bias."
**The seven recommendation clusters (Section VI, quoted key items)**
27. Transparency & Disclosure: "**Licensees should be required to maintain transparency** about the use of AI"; "State medical boards should develop clear guidelines... about the disclosure of AI usage to patients... but **do not create unnecessary administrative burden**"; "FSMB should develop documentation detailing the capabilities and limitations of the most commonly used AI tools"; "FSMB should develop a frequently asked questions and best practices document."
28. Education: "FSMB and its partners in the medical education community **should identify structured educational resources** for physicians, medical boards, and patients"; "FSMB should collect resources, recommendations, guidelines and commentary."
29. Responsible Use & Accountability: "**Developers should provide agency to physicians**"; "**Hospital systems, insurers, or others who select AI tools... should provide physicians with education about AI tools, access to performance reports of the individual tools, and should design a process for regular review of the efficacy of the tools**"; "**AI tools should be designed in a manner which would provide state medical boards the ability to audit and understand them**, in order to appropriately assess whether a physician who relied upon a tool's output has deviated from standard of care."
30. Equity & Access: "Efforts should be made to ensure equitable access"; "FSMB and state medical boards are committed to the principle that care provided... is equitable and not influenced by bias based on race, ethnicity or other forms of discrimination."
31. Privacy & Data Security: "**Developers of AI tools must implement rigorous safeguards to protect patient data** used in the development and evaluation of AI" (a vendor "must"); "Licensees should generally be informed about how patient data will be used"; "FSMB, along with industry stakeholders, should create policies for the use and dissemination of patient data by AI systems, including **minimum data protection measures**... Where possible both state and federal regulators **should coordinate to ensure any policies are not duplicative**."
32. Oversight & Regulation: "**State medical boards must retain the authority to discipline physicians for the inappropriate application of AI tools** in the delivery of care. This includes considering issues of accountability, **particularly as AI systems become more autonomous**"; "State medical boards **should examine how the 'practice of medicine' is legally defined** in their jurisdiction for purposes of ensuring continued regulatory oversight of those who provide healthcare, human or otherwise"; "FSMB should explore and pilot ways in which AI can aid medical boards in decision-making, with the potential to **shift from a reactive to a proactive system**"; "FSMB should work with state medical boards to help develop policies that address the use of AI systems by licensees, particularly as AI systems become more autonomous."
33. Continual Review: "State medical boards, with the support of the FSMB, should continually review and update guidelines"; "Policy makers should consider the impact of AI on fundamental legal principles such as **the definition of practice of medicine and the impact of AI on the corporate practice of medicine**"; "FSMB should establish a **dedicated team** for the ongoing review and adaptation of AI guidelines."

## (d) Sophistication markers
- Technology: genuine primer (supervised vs unsupervised learning, parameters — brain-neuron analogy with sources, generative AI, LLMs, ambient scribes, chatbots, "black box"). Names **skill degradation, dependence, over-reliance** — the automation-bias cluster — and "as AI systems become more autonomous" (twice) shows foresight of agentic AI without the vocabulary. Cites HBR and CNBC for the 80%-replacement forecast.
- Evidence: light but present (disciplinary statistics 2015–19; footnoted claims). Mostly expert-opinion genre, as stated.
- Mandates: the strongest in the corpus — discipline authority "must retain," physician "duty," developer "must implement," "violation of professional duties," CME "should" with board power behind it. This is the only document whose clauses can end a license.
- Legal sophistication (Gerke's imprint): standard-of-care analysis both directions (following without rationale may breach; deviating requires documented rationale), corporate-practice-of-medicine implications, jurisdiction-definition foresight.

## (e) Distinctives vs sibling frameworks
- Only regulator-authored framework: converts ethics language into disciplinary doctrine. The **rationale/documentation duty (clauses 11–14)** and the **audit-design demand on tools (29)** exist nowhere else in the corpus.
- Principles-not-applications regulatory theory — explicitly anti-specific-rule — is the most sophisticated governance stance in the set and anticipates the field's later consensus.
- Anticipates autonomy ("more autonomous," practice-of-medicine redefinition, corporate practice) two years before agentic AI went mainstream in these documents.
- Anti-overreach + anti-duplication clauses distinguish it from compliance-expansion instincts elsewhere.

## (f) Provisional classification
**pioneer** — the intellectual leader of the clinical-practice strand: earliest comprehensive regulator framework (Apr 2024), deepest legal analysis, real enforcement theory, technology-competent. Its language (enhance-not-replace, tool framing, education/accountability/consent/liability structure) is what other bodies restate.
