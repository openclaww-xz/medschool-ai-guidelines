# GenAI Policies Among ASPPH Member Schools and Programs of Public Health (Frontiers in Public Health, 2026)

## Research question
Which ASPPH member institutions have publicly available AI guidance, which guidance rises to the level of a formal policy, and what are the key components of institution-wide policies?

## Methods
Document analysis of all 153 ASPPH member websites (Colorado SPH = 3 universities → 155 initial documents), Oct 2024–Jan 2025; standardized website search ("artificial intelligence," "AI," "generative AI," "genai," "ChatGPT," "AI policy"); documents PDF-archived. Policy vs guideline classified via University of Wisconsin-Madison Policy Library criteria (4 of 5: states institutional position; mandates/specifies/prohibits behavior; broad applicability; enforceable non-negotiable elements; articulates what and why). Consensus coding by 3 raters; Krippendorff's α=0.514 (82% agreement); descriptive + content + thematic analysis + TF-IDF text mining in R.

## Sample
155 initial documents → 128 publicly available policies/guidelines analyzed descriptively; 18 met policy criteria after screening (108 excluded for missing ≥2 criteria; 2 not public; 23 no AI guidance; 2 external links only).

## Key findings
- Only 18/155 (11.6%) ASPPH-member documents met formal policy criteria — a striking policy gap.
- Content analysis of 18 policies: usage/pedagogical guidance 15 (83.3%); academic integrity/misconduct 14 (77.8%); data privacy/security 9 (50%); software access guidance 7 (38.9%); ethical considerations 5 (27.8%); legal compliance 4 (22.2%); AI detection/disciplinary guidance 4 (22.2%).
- Thematic analysis: 6 themes, 10 subthemes, 21 categories, segmented by audience (faculty: classroom use, misconduct; students: integrity, citation/accountability; staff: sensitive data governance incl. blanket vs tiered data bans; researchers: research integrity/IP/funding-agency rules; all: federal/state law alignment, human-centered ethics).
- Tool mentions: ChatGPT named by 77.8% of schools; DALL-E/Bard ~19.8%; applications dominated by teaching (89.96%) and learning (82.54%). TF-IDF top terms: "use," "student," "tools," "genai," "course."
- Tiered classroom regimes common (prohibited/restricted/unrestricted at instructor discretion); specific legal artifacts cited incl. HIPAA, FERPA, NIH peer-review prohibition, Virginia EO 46 banning DeepSeek on state devices.

## What it says about who leads policy
Institution-level governance dominates; most guidance is instructor-delegated (course-level syllabus statements) rather than school-mandated. Authors attribute sparse formal policy to missing state/federal clarity — i.e., institutions hesitate to mandate without higher-level signals. ASPPH (the association) is exploring network-wide AI norms but had not issued them.

## Limitations (authors')
Public documents only (many schools circulate policy internally); snapshot Oct 2024–Mar 2025; low-moderate inter-rater reliability (Kα=0.514); policy-on-paper ≠ practice/enforcement.

## Relation to corpus findings
The closest methodological sibling to our corpus work in the entire peer-reviewed set — same document-analysis approach, but limited to public health (153 institutions, 18 policies) and a single time window. Corroborates the Clin Teach policy-gap finding and extends it to public health: formal policy is rare (11.6%), content skews to integrity/classroom use, privacy/ethics/legal coverage shallow. Our 378-source corpus (medical schools, health systems, GME, national frameworks) is larger, multi-sector, and longitudinal — able to answer what this study could not: policy prevalence across allopathic medicine, temporal diffusion, and cross-sector convergence.
