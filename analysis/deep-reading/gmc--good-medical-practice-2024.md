# GMC — Good medical practice (2024) [full standards text]

## (a) Authority and authorship
- Publisher: General Medical Council, the UK statutory regulator. Published 22 Aug 2023; **came into effect 30 January 2024**; last updated 13 December 2024. Applies to "all doctors, physician associates ('PAs') and anaesthesia associates ('AAs')" — "medical professionals."
- Nature: the core ethical framework, deliberately NOT a rulebook: "But **it isn't a set of rules. You must use your professional judgement** to apply the standards... to your day-to-day practice." Calibrated language: "'You must' is used for a legal or ethical duty you're expected to meet (or be able to justify why you didn't). 'You should' is used for duties... you may not be able to comply with because of factors outside your control."
- Enforcement linkage: revalidation (annual appraisal, clinical-governance engagement) and fitness-to-practise (risk assessment: seriousness, context incl. "systems factors," insight/remediation).

## (b) Policy philosophy
- Posture: **technology-neutral professional-judgment charter**. AI appears only as an enumerated example inside device reporting — the architecture assumes standards stretch over any technology. Opening duty: "Patients must be able to trust medical professionals with their lives and health. To justify that trust you must **make the care of patients your first concern**, and meet the standards expected of you in all four domains."
- Four domains: Knowledge, skills and development; Patients, partnership and communication; Colleagues, culture and safety; Trust and professionalism — "each domain is equally important."

## (c) Complete clause inventory (all 101 duties, condensed to duty-phrases; AI-relevant clauses flagged)
**Overarching duties**: care of patients first concern; four domains (see (b)).
**Domain 1 — Knowledge, skills and development**: (1) be competent in all aspects incl. leadership/research/teaching; (2) **recognise and work within limits of competence; practise only under appropriate supervision**; (3) **keep up to date with guidelines and developments affecting your work**; (4) follow law, GMC guidance, regulations; (5) necessary English-language knowledge. Clinical care: (6) good standard of practice and care; partner with patients; treatment based on assessment + clinical judgement of effectiveness; (7) in clinical care you must: (a) adequately assess condition incl. symptoms, psychological/spiritual/social/economic/cultural factors, patient views/needs/values; (b) physical examination where necessary; (c) promptly provide/arrange advice, investigation, treatment; (d) prescribe only with adequate knowledge of the patient and satisfaction the treatment meets needs; (e) **effective treatment based on best available evidence**; (f) follow medicines/devices guidance; (g) consult colleagues/supervising clinician where appropriate; (h) refer when it serves needs; (8) cosmetic-interventions guidance if relevant. Remote consultations: (9) **safe and effective care "whether face to face, or through remote consultations via telephone, video link, or other online services"; if safe care impossible in the mode, offer alternative or signpost**. Research: (10) consider research opportunities; tell patients of opportunities. Performance: (11) keep knowledge/skills up to date; (12) engage with structured support (mentoring/coaching) on joining/role change; (13) monitor, maintain, develop, improve performance incl. QA/QI systems — (a) contribute to service-improvement discussions, (b) regular reviews/audits of own and team's work + respond + retrain where necessary, (c) regular training/CPD, (d) regular reflection incl. feedback and self-awareness of how life experience/culture/beliefs influence decisions. Resources: (14) good use of resources; (15) **choose sustainable solutions where able, provided care standards aren't compromised; consider initiatives to reduce environmental impact of healthcare**.
**Domain 2 — Patients, partnership and communication**: (16) recognise dignity and right to privacy; (17) intimate-examinations guidance; (18) respect right to choose/second opinion; (19) treat patients fairly; no discrimination; no refusal/delay based on believed contribution to condition; (20) prioritise by clinical need; if resources/policies/systems prevent this and safety/dignity compromised → para 75; (21) conscientious objection must not block access; (22) **treat patient information as confidential, including after death**; (23) kindness, courtesy, respect — (a) sensitive communication esp. distressing prognoses, (b) listening, recognising patient knowledge, (c) no assumptions about what patient considers significant, (d) explain reasons for options offered and not offered, (e) recognise vulnerability, (f) alleviate pain/distress. Decisions: (24) presume adult capacity; involve patients in decisions; (25) satisfied consent/valid authority before examination/treatment/teaching/research; (26) know consent/capacity law, codes, guidance; (27) end-of-life guidance. Information-sharing: (28) **give patients information they want or need, understandably — condition(s), progression, uncertainties; options incl. no action; benefits, risks of harm, uncertainties, likelihood of success for each**; (29) open dialogue; honest answers; (30) **information clear, accurate, up to date, based on best available evidence**; (31) check understanding; time/support for informed decisions; (32) meet language/communication needs proportionately; (33) consider/respond to disability needs; reasonable adjustments; (34) treat each patient as individual; no assumptions; (35) share information needed for teaching/research decisions; (36) be open about interests affecting treatments. Those close to patient: (37) considerate, compassionate support. Whole patient: (38) support self-care and empowerment; (39) ask about other care incl. OTC; check compatibility; (40) polypharmacy reviews; benefits vs harm. Safeguarding: (41) consider needs/welfare of vulnerable people; (42) **act promptly on abuse/neglect concerns**. Emergencies: (43) offer help in emergency, considering safety/competence/alternatives. Risk-to-others: (44) no denial of care because of risk; minimise risk before treating/arranging. Openness when things go wrong: (45) **duty of candour — open and honest if harm or distress; put matters right; apologise (apology ≠ liability admission); explain fully and promptly short/long-term effects; report incident per organisational policy for review/investigation and learning**; (46) respond promptly, fully, honestly to complaints; complaint must not affect care; (47) end professional relationship only when trust breakdown prevents good care.
**Domain 3 — Colleagues, culture and safety**: (48) treat colleagues with kindness, courtesy, respect ("colleagues includes anyone you work with"); (49) effective teamworking: listen; communicate clearly/politely; respect skills/contributions; lead or follow as required; (50) be accessible when on duty; (51) compassion for colleagues with problems, but patient safety first. Positive environment: (52) role-model respectful, fair, supportive, compassionate culture; (53) awareness of your influence; (54) **be aware of the risk of bias; consider how life experience/culture/beliefs influence interactions and decisions**; (55) respect for others' life experience/cultures/beliefs; (56) never abuse/discriminate/bully/harass on personal characteristics (legally protected characteristics enumerated); (57) no sexual behaviour causing offence/distress; (58) **if you witness such behaviours you should act** — support target, challenge safely, report (with target's awareness where possible); speaking-up responsibility on everyone; (59) leaders/managers witnessing or told of such behaviour **must act**: address, support, escalate promptly. Leadership: (60) follow Leadership and management guidance; (61) ensure appropriate supervision for those whose work you oversee; (62) **accurate, fair, objective references and appraisals incl. locums and students; never omit relevant information on competence/performance/conduct**; (63) offer professional support (mentoring/coaching/teaching) esp. to those new to UK, returning, or without access; (64) fair access to training/development/employment if part of your role. Continuity: (65) share all relevant information promptly within/across teams (incl. reasonable adjustments, communication preferences); share with patients progress, responsibility allocation, lead clinician name; be confident necessary information shared before going off duty/delegating/referring; check handover of responsibility. Delegation: (66) **be confident any person you delegate to has the necessary knowledge, skills and training; give clear instructions; encourage questions/support**; (67) if delegated a task you can't safely do, prioritise patient safety and seek help even if you agreed to act independently; (68) follow Delegation and referral guidance. Records: (69) **records clear, accurate, contemporaneous, legible**; (70) records should usually include: clinical findings; drugs/investigations/treatments; information shared with patient; patient concerns/preferences and whether addressed; reasonable adjustments/communication preferences; decisions and actions incl. no-action and review timing; author and time; (71) keep personal-information records securely per data-protection law; follow Confidentiality guidance. Safety: (72) use clinical governance and risk-management structures; (73) **to keep patients safe you must: (a) contribute to confidential inquiries; (b) contribute to adverse event recognition; (c) report adverse incidents involving medical devices (INCLUDING SOFTWARE, DIAGNOSTIC TESTS, AND DIGITAL TOOLS) that put safety at risk or have the potential to do so; (d) contribute to incident reviews/investigations; (e) report suspected adverse drug reactions; (f) respond to public-health monitoring requests** — [the AI hook: software/digital tools enumerated as medical devices for mandatory reporting]; (74) honour contractual commitments. Safety risks: (75) **act promptly if patient safety or dignity is or may be seriously compromised** — basic-care failure → act immediately; inadequate premises/equipment/resources/policies/systems → protect patients, put right, then raise concern; concerns about a colleague's fitness → seek advice, then report; (76) leaders must create environments where people can talk about errors safely; deal with raised concerns promptly. Health: (77) avoid care from family/close colleagues; (78) care for own health; seek independent advice on fitness; (79) **must consult a suitably qualified professional and follow their advice on practice changes if you have a transmissible serious condition or judgement/performance may be affected — must not rely on own risk assessment**; (80) immunisation against serious communicable diseases.
**Domain 4 — Trust and professionalism**: (81) conduct justifies patient and public trust; (82) honest about experience, qualifications, role; introduce yourself; (83) give registered name/GMC number on request; (84) honest financial/commercial dealings. Research integrity: (85) participants' interests first; honesty and integrity; national research governance. Boundaries: (86) no sexual/improper relationships with patients/those close; (87) no exploitation of vulnerability via personal beliefs. Communication: (88) **honest, trustworthy, maintain confidentiality in ALL professional written, verbal and digital communications**; (89) **information communicated as a medical professional must be accurate, not false or misleading — (a) take reasonable steps to check accuracy, (b) never deliberately omit relevant information, (c) never minimise or trivialise risks of harm, (d) never present opinion as established fact**; (90) public communication (social media, advertising, promotion, endorsement): follow 88-89; declare conflicts; never exploit vulnerability or lack of medical knowledge; align with duty to promote/protect health; (91) follow social-media guidance; (92) witness evidence — state limits of knowledge and expertise. Private communication: (93) **private messages (incl. instant messaging) may become public**. Conflicts: (94) interests must not affect or appear to affect treatment/referral/commissioning; (95) declare conflicts; be prepared to exclude yourself; (96) no incentive payments/gifts/hospitality affecting care, given or accepted; (97) avoid treating self or close relations. Regulatory: (98) cooperate with inquiries, investigations, complaints; (99) tell GMC without delay of cautions, criminal charges/convictions, official-inquiry criticism, professional-body findings; (100) inform organisations and patients of suspension/restrictions; (101) adequate indemnity insurance, reviewed regularly.

## (d) Sophistication markers (AI-relevant)
- Para 73(c) is the AI-bearing clause: "software, diagnostic tests, and digital tools" enumerated inside a **must-report** device-incidents duty — AI tools become reportable without any AI-specific regulation (confirmed by the GMC Q&A, separate note).
- Paras 2-3 (competence limits; keep up to date) are the hook for AI-skills CPD; para 66 (delegation) frames the "confident the person/agent has necessary knowledge, skills and training" logic that GMC's Q&A extends by analogy to technology responsibility allocation.
- Paras 28/30 (information incl. uncertainties/risks; accuracy per best evidence) supply the consent-conversation duty for AI-assisted decisions; para 89(a)-(d) is a general anti-hallucination duty for professional communication.
- Para 15 adds sustainability duties; para 54 adds bias self-awareness; para 9 covers remote-consultation modalities.
- The must/should grammar with justification escape valve is a mature regulatory instrument — duties that anticipate context without enumerating it.

## (e) Distinctives vs sibling frameworks
- The base layer of the UK professional-judgment model: everything AI-specific in the UK corpus (GMC Q&A, joint regulators statement, NHS long read) hangs off these 101 paragraphs. Contrast Singapore's AIiHGLE: MOH writes lifecycle rules for AI products; GMC writes conduct rules for doctors and lets technology arrive.
- Only document in the corpus whose AI relevance is almost entirely implicit — evidence that regulatory density can be near-zero at the conduct layer while remaining complete, because duties are written technology-neutral.

## (f) Provisional classification
**cautious-informed** (as an AI governance artifact, by design): zero AI-specific content, but the enumerated device-reporting extension (73c) and technology-neutral judgement architecture make it fully AI-capable. The anti-naive move is structural: no technology named except inside a reporting duty.
