# Health Professional Students' GenAI Use During Clinical Placements: Cross-Sectional Survey (JMIR Med Ed, 2026)

## Research question
How do health professional students use GenAI during clinical placements — adoption prevalence by discipline, tasks/tools/frequency (incl. patient-data handling), and perceived benefits, risks, and training/governance needs?

## Methods
CHERRIES-compliant anonymous online survey (LimeSurvey, 61 items), Université Grenoble Alpes, France, Jul 17–Sep 30 2025; ethics approval (SIFEM committee, notice 1905-2025). 9-item GenAI maturity index (Cronbach α=0.869); Pearson χ²/Fisher, Cochran-Armitage trend tests, Holm correction; qualitative descriptive content analysis of open responses (213 meaning units; 83.1% initial inter-coder agreement).

## Sample
388 respondents of ~4553 invited (8.5% response; medicine 3.1% → physiotherapy 26%). 79.4% women; 51% aged 20–24; nursing 217 (55.9%), medicine 69, pharmacy 66, midwifery 23, physiotherapy 13. Maturity: 51.3% moderate, 33.5% limited, 7.5% high.

## Key findings
- 52.6% (204/388) used GenAI during clinical placements; lower in midwifery (26.1%, OR 0.30); adoption rose with maturity: 9% (minimal) → 76% (high) (z=6.01, P<.001).
- Nonuse reasons: no need 32.1%, don't know best practices 20.7%, no interest 18.5%.
- Tasks (of users): information extraction 77.9%, bibliographic search 74.5%, translation 71.1%, writing 69.1%, clinical simulation 54.9%; patient-facing tasks lower (drafting patient documents 38.2%, preparing patient communication 38.2%); weekly+ use mostly info tasks (26.0%).
- Confidentiality: 23.5% (48/204) entered patient-identifying data at least once (3 "often"); 47.1% processed real medical data (9.8% "often"); self-data disclosed by 42.6%.
- Benefits: document drafting 81.2%, information accessibility 68.5%; skepticism on prognosis (60.3% disagree) and data collection (54.6% disagree).
- Risks: dependency 90.9%, confidentiality breach 87.4%, long-term skill loss 84.8%; NOT job replacement (>60% disagree).
- Governance perceived absent: only 10% agreed GenAI use is sufficiently regulated; 4.8% agreed staff are trained; 16.3% agreed bias accounted for; 74.9% agreed patients should be informed when AI is used.
- Training demand: best-practice clinical guide 78.3%; ethics/regulation training 77.7%.

## What it says about who leads policy
Documents an "institutional readiness gap": supervisors rarely informed students of legal responsibilities or local rules. Calls for coordinated governance between academic institutions and placement sites (charters, approved-tool lists, escalation procedures); notes French national actors (National Academy of Medicine, HAS guide) as the emerging policy leads. Multi-level: national bodies + school + host clinical sites, with supervisors as the delivery channel.

## Limitations (authors')
Single French university; self-report (recall/social desirability); no supervisor/institutional data; cross-sectional snapshot; interprofessional heterogeneity with small subgroups; low medicine response rate.

## Relation to corpus findings
Directly corroborates the Clin Teach policy-gap study empirically: adoption (52.6%) has outrun governance (10% say use is regulated), and students demand guidance that institutions have not supplied. Our corpus can test whether the demanded artifacts (approved-tool lists, disclosure rules, placement governance charters) actually exist in US medical school and health-system policies — connecting the demand side documented here to the supply side our corpus measures.
