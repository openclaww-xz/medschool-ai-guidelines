# Implementation of Ambient AI Scribes in Hospitals: Leadership, Governance, and Change Management (J Healthcare Leadership, 2026)

## Research question
What leadership, governance, patient-consent, regulatory, and sustainability factors determine whether ambient AI scribe implementation succeeds at hospital/health-system level? (Dentistry/OMFS as specialty case study.)

## Methods
Structured evidence-informed narrative synthesis + framework analysis; PubMed/Embase/Scopus Jan 2020–Mar 2026 + hand-search of society reports, grey literature, standards documents; 59 sources (34 core: 17 empirical, 7 conceptual, 5 policy/legal, 5 industry); Leavitt's Diamond (Structure/Technology/People/Process) as a priori organizing framework; dual-author source review; no formal quality appraisal (deliberate narrative format).

## Sample
Not empirical — 59 cited sources incl. RCTs (NEJM AI, JAMA Netw Open), enterprise deployments (Kaiser Permanente NC: 7,260 physicians, 2.5M+ encounters; 3,442 adopters in first 10 weeks of opt-in), Peterson Health Technology Institute 2025 report, ADA standards, ABA/medico-legal analyses.

## Key findings
- Evidence: Penn ambient scribe −20.4% note-writing time, −30% after-hours "pajama time" (50.6→35.4 min/day); Atrium Health 112 vs 103 clinicians — NO significant productivity gain; PHTI 2025: stress/burnout reduction likely, no clear financial or efficiency ROI; ~21% of US physicians used AI for documentation in 2024.
- Failure modes: hallucination, omission (harder to detect than commission), misattribution, laterality/tooth-numbering errors (3 competing dental notation systems), "contextual blindness" (recency bias compressing history), "note bloat."
- Equity: ASR error higher for AAVE, regional accents, code-switching → self-reinforcing "algorithmic inequity cycle" (data deserts); "diversity tax."
- Liability: vendors disclaim liability, no indemnification for documentation errors; tripartite exposure (clinician-institution-vendor); "liability paradox" of copilot alerts; insurance frameworks unadapted.
- Governance framework (Leavitt's Diamond): executive-sponsored multidisciplinary steering committee (CMO/CIO), credentialing-style competency before use, non-punitive AI-error reporting, vendor contracts with equity-stratified accuracy data, audit rights, exit clauses; trainees need protected independent note-composition (cites U Saskatchewan dental curriculum: competency before AI in year 4); three change-management failure modes — selective non-use, productivity co-optation, feedback atrophy; KPIs across efficiency, well-being, patient experience, note quality, adoption (50% target at 6 months), financials.

## What it says about who leads policy
Health-system leadership (CMO/CIO, steering committees) is positioned as the primary governance actor, operating in a regulatory grey zone national bodies haven't filled; professional societies (AMA, ADA, Australasian Institute of Digital Health) supply guidance; vendors disclaim responsibility — pushing accountability to institutions and signing clinicians. Credentialing-style governance treats AI use as a clinical competency institutions must manage.

## Limitations (authors')
Narrative not systematic review; no pooled estimates; dentistry/OMFS case extrapolated (no dental ambient-scribe evidence base exists); authors' own implementation experience in Singapore/Canada/US introduces perspective; fast-moving field.

## Relation to corpus findings
The governance framework here is exactly what our corpus can audit empirically: do health-system AI policies actually contain steering-committee charters, competency requirements, error-reporting pathways, vendor-contract clauses, trainee-protection rules? The review asserts these are needed; our 378 sources can measure adoption of each element — turning this paper's normative checklist into testable hypotheses across the sector.
