# Karolinska Institutet — Generative AI and Education (staff guidance hub)

## (a) Authority and authorship
- Publisher: Karolinska Institutet (staff pages, staff.ki.se); content from the **Teaching and Learning unit** and **KIB (Karolinska Institutet University Library)** — named producing units, not a central policy office.
- Retrieved via Internet Archive ("ki.se temporarily offline at access time") — provenance noted in front-matter; page itself is guidance ("advice and suggestions"), not binding regulation.

## (b) Policy philosophy
- Posture: **pedagogical adaptation with practical scaffolding**. Framing verbatim: "Generative AI is changing the way we learn, teach and create content. At Karolinska Institutet, we offer guidance, resources and guidelines for using AI tools **responsibly and effectively** in higher education."
- The framing question for educators: "How can KI's teaching and examination practices be **adapted to technology that can generate text or images that are indistinguishable from those created by humans**?" — the indisguishability problem stated as the design constraint.

## (c) Complete clause inventory
1. Hub statement: guidance/resources/guidelines for responsible and effective AI use (verbatim in (b)).
2. Scope of coverage: "opportunities, risks, ethical principles and **legal aspects** of integrating AI."
3. Staff course: "'Working with Generative AI'" — register link; later: "a flexible course primarily for teaching staff... basics of working with generative AI – its possibilities, limitations, legal and ethical aspects"; "currently only available in Swedish, but an English version is under production."
4. "An introduction to Generative AI and teaching" (Teaching and Learning unit): educators find "advice and suggestions" for adapting teaching/examination to human-indistinguishable outputs (verbatim in (b)).
5. "Generative AI and assessment" (Teaching and Learning): names tools — "ChatGPT, Claude, CoPilot and Gemini" — "have both created challenges and opportunities"; page is "a primer on how these technologies work, their strengths and weaknesses, and how to view their use within assessment."
6. "Communicating with students about acceptable AI use": "**annotated KI-specific examples** of how the bounds of acceptable AI use can be communicated with students" — boundary-communication templates.
7. "AI for students" (KIB): "a collection of pages for students who want to use AI effectively and responsibly in their studies... tips, concrete examples and approaches."
8. "Effective AI Prompting": "examples of how to get started with writing prompts that work with the most common tools, as well as some ideas for how to use generative AI in your practice as a teacher or course administrator."
- All clauses are pointers to sub-pages; no binding rules on the hub page itself.

## (d) Sophistication markers
- Naming four specific tools (ChatGPT, Claude, CoPilot, Gemini) and treating "how these technologies work, strengths and weaknesses" as prerequisite knowledge for assessment design — a mechanism-level premise most policy pages skip.
- The pedagogical move is to attack **assessment design** and **boundary communication** (annotated examples) rather than detection — consistent with the strongest international practice.
- Prompting guidance as staff professional development is an unusual, practice-first marker.

## (e) Distinctives vs sibling frameworks
- Most faculty-development-shaped of the international set: course + primer + examples + prompting, a complete teacher-support stack rather than a rulebook.
- The "indistinguishable from those created by humans" framing is the bluntest statement of the assessment-validity problem in the corpus; others gesture at it, KI builds its hub on it.

## (f) Provisional classification
**progressive-structured** — adaptationist, assessment-first, practice-scaffolded; soft authority (guidance + courses) with no enforcement layer, which is characteristic of the Swedish institutional style evident here.
