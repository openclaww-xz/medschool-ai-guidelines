# LLM Use in Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery Training: National Resident Survey (Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, 2026)

## Research question
How are US OMFS residents using LLMs — platforms, frequency, purposes, ethics perceptions — and what guidance do they receive?

## Methods
16-question REDCap survey emailed to all US OMFS program directors for forwarding to residents (2024); descriptive stats, Wilson CIs.

## Sample
81/~1200 residents (~6.8% response); 66.7% male; PGY-heavy toward junior years; only 2 with formal CS background.

## Key findings (with counts)
- 79.0% (64/81) had used an LLM; 96.8% of users used ChatGPT; 51.9% used an LLM at least monthly; 39/81 never during training.
- Top use: self-study/lecture 38.3%; career 9.9%; clinical 7.4%; research 3.7%.
- Guidance: 97.5% (79/81) received NO program guidance on LLM use.
- Ethics: 76.5% find LLM concept-explanation ethical; 79.0% for CVs/letters; manuscript use split 50.6% ethical vs 49.4% not.
- Accuracy belief: 48.1% agree LLM explanations are generally accurate; 43.2% "no experience."
- Comparison: ~50% monthly use ≈ 2x internal medicine residents (26%) in prior study.

## What it says about WHO leads policy
Program-level vacuum: 97.5% no guidance — residency programs provide none; no specialty-society input either. Residents' ethics judgments are entirely self-generated (split on manuscripts), demonstrating what happens when no authority leads.

## Limitations
6.8% response rate; self-report with social desirability; junior-year skew; open-ended not systematically analyzed.

## Corroboration/contradiction with our corpus
Strongest possible corroboration of the governance vacuum at GME level — 97.5% no guidance exceeds every other published figure we've seen. Matches our corpus: OMFS/dental GME has essentially zero AI policy in our collection (our GME policies are IM, radiology, anesthesiology-adjacent). The manuscript-ethics 50/50 split contrasts with our corpus's consistent institutional disclosure requirements — trainees without policy default to ambivalence, supporting the claim that policy content actually shapes norms.
