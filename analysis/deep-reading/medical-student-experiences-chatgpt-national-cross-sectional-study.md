# Medical Student Experiences With ChatGPT: National Cross-Sectional Study (JMIR Form Res, 2026)

## Research question
How do US medical students (allopathic + osteopathic) use ChatGPT across academic, clinical, and research domains, what do they perceive of it, and what responsible-use habits (cross-checking, editing) do they practice?

## Methods
CHERRIES/JARS-compliant anonymous online survey (Qualtrics), Aug 25–Dec 10 2024; 28 items; recruitment via deans of student affairs at 188 US MD/DO schools (14 agreed) + AAMC Organization of Student Representatives; IRB-exempt (U Florida); Pearson χ², dichotomized outcomes.

## Sample
177 completed responses (228 total; conservative response rate ~2.3%). 62.7% female; 57.1% aged 25–30; 48% completed ≥1 clinical block; 14 schools incl. NYU, Penn, Brown, UCF, Wayne State, 3 osteopathic.

## Key findings
- 98.9% heard of ChatGPT; 88.7% ever used; 31.6% had curricular AI exposure; 72.3% moderate-or-higher AI understanding.
- Academic uses (of users): understanding concepts 74.5%, exam prep 61.1%, study materials 55.4%, completing assignments 46.5%.
- Clinical uses: differential diagnoses 42.7%, pharmacology review 35.7%, writing clinical notes 21.0% (57.6% of note-writers were preclinical M1/M2).
- Research: summarizing papers 44.6%, drafting manuscripts 24.8%.
- Perceptions: 75.2% rated ChatGPT "more accurate than not"; 90.4% found it useful; 86.6% believed it would make them more effective physicians; 86.0% will continue using in practice.
- Responsible use: 46.5% cross-check ≤50% of the time; 21.0% edit output ≤50% of the time. Higher baseline AI understanding → more cross-checking (OR 2.31, 95% CI 1.08–4.97) and editing (OR 2.45, 95% CI 1.05–5.71).
- Outlook: 80.2% want AI taught in med school; comfortable with AI for admin (85.3%), notes (75.1%); less for diagnosis/management; 53.7% believe AI will reduce clinical reasoning skills; 35.6% expect physician job reduction.

## What it says about who leads policy
Explicit: "when this survey was sent out, there was little guidance or formal educational policies regarding the ethical use of AI in clinical practice"; cites survey evidence many osteopathic schools lacked formal GenAI policies; calls for institutional policies plus standardization "collaborating with national accrediting bodies and medical education organizations" — schools lead locally, national bodies should harmonize.

## Limitations (authors')
Very low response rate (2.3% conservative); self-report/selection bias (likely overrepresentation of ChatGPT users); closed items only (novel uses missed); US-specific; HIPAA-deidentification degree not measured; survey burnout in med students.

## Relation to corpus findings
The US demand-side counterpart to the policy-gap literature: near-universal awareness, majority use including note-writing and assignment completion, weak verification habits — all unfolding under self-noted institutional silence. Corroborates the Clin Teach study and the osteopathic-pilot file in this corpus. Our 378-source corpus supplies exactly what this study says was absent: the inventory of school-level guidance that did or didn't exist when these students were surveyed — enabling linkage of policy presence to use patterns.
