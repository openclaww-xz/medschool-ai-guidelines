# Medical Students' Attitudes, Perceptions, and Self-Reported Familiarity With AI: Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (JMIR Med Educ, 2026)

## Research question
What are medical students' (MD/MBBS/MBChB/DO-only) AI-related attitudes, perceptions, and self-reported familiarity, quantified with harmonized constructs, prediction intervals, risk of bias, and GRADE certainty?

## Methods
PROSPERO-registered (CRD420251120543) PRISMA 2020 systematic review; 6 databases to Apr 1 2026 + supplementary searches; dual screening/extraction; 9 prespecified proportion domains harmonized via operational framework; random-effects meta-analysis (Freeman-Tukey double-arcsine, HKSJ CIs, prediction intervals); JBI risk of bias; GRADE; participant-independence audit; subgroup/meta-regression (exploratory).

## Sample
96 cross-sectional studies, 37 countries, >45,000 medical students (2019–2026; 85% from 2023+). Eastern Mediterranean 40%, Europe 20%, Western Pacific 13%; 45% high-income. Sample sizes 20–4,492. 62% broad/general AI, 28% ChatGPT/LLM, 10% domain-specific; only 13 used validated MAIRS-MS.

## Key findings
- Pooled proportions: positive attitude 76.9% (PI 42.2–98.3%); career benefit 78.4%; curricular integration support 76.6% (38 studies); replacement concern 39.9% (PI 6.6–80.1%); willingness to learn 71.5%; ethical concerns 62.8%; familiarity 63.3% (PI 7.8–100%; I²=99.5%); prior use 63.2%; trust in AI-assisted decisions 50.6% (only 8 studies).
- ALL 9 domains rated VERY LOW certainty (GRADE) — cross-sectional self-report, nonvalidated instruments, wide prediction intervals, small-study effects in 5/8 testable domains.
- Subgroups: familiarity higher post-2023 (66.4% vs 45.6%, P=.04); ChatGPT-framed studies → higher familiarity (77.4%) but LOWER positive attitude (66.7%) than broad AI (80.1%, P=.005); domain-specific AI → lower replacement concern (27.2%, P=.01).
- Risk of bias: 82% moderate, 18% low; confounding identified in only 18% of studies.
- Key interpretive claim: pooled means are contextual benchmarks, NOT transportable prevalences; schools must run local needs assessments.

## What it says about who leads policy
Recommends schools develop "tiered guidance" separating learning-assistance use (disclosure, integrity) from clinical use (validation, supervision, safety) aligned with ethical/governance frameworks — institution-level policy with alignment to external frameworks; notes policy implications "should be tested" — i.e., no inventory of existing policy exists in this literature.

## Limitations (authors')
All cross-sectional self-report; instrument/AI-referent heterogeneity; mixed-population disaggregation losses; study-level (ecological) meta-regression confounding; trust domain k=8; Google Scholar search records incomplete.

## Relation to corpus findings
The definitive statement that the perception literature cannot guide policy alone — very low certainty throughout. Every number our corpus-based work would compare against (adoption ~63%, favorable attitudes ~77%) comes with prediction intervals spanning most of the range. Corroborates the Clin Teach gap: nothing in these 96 studies measures what schools actually enacted. Our corpus is the missing supply-side variable.
