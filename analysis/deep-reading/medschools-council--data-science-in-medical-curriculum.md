# Medical Schools Council / HDR UK — Data science in the medical curriculum: equipping medical students for the digital age (May 2025)

## (a) Authority and authorship
- Publisher: **Medical Schools Council** (representative body of UK medical schools' heads) with **Health Data Research UK**. May 2025. Authored by Dr Chris Fassnidge (Senior Policy Officer, MSC); named working group of 14 (Chair: Professor Sinéad Langan, LSHTM; members from Imperial, HDR UK/Oxford, Edinburgh, Dundee, Lancaster, UCL, Brighton & Sussex, QMUL, KCL; includes Professor Josip Car, lead of the global DECODE consensus initiative).
- Method disclosed: working group → scope agreement → "**formal consensus voting exercise whereby a threshold of 60% voting for a core competency would result in its inclusion**." Evidence base: 2023 MSC/HDR UK survey (175 students across 19 schools, 173 analyzed; 51 staff across 21 schools). Competencies adapted from the **DECODE** international Delphi consensus (Car J, Ong QC, Erlikh Fox T, et al. JAMA Netw Open. 2025).
- Deliberate scope exclusion: "technical skills such as programming were not considered for inclusion" — deliverable by all schools, not a specialist track.

## (b) Policy philosophy
- Posture: **curriculum-competency expansion** (not conduct regulation). Framing verbatim: "it is **imperative that medical education evolves to equip future doctors with the knowledge and skills necessary to navigate and harness the power of data science** effectively."
- Deliberate technology-agnosticism on LLMs: "it was recognised that the emergence of new technologies, such as **large language models and AI co-pilots, would most probably radically alter the current landscape**... It was determined that the current recommendations **should remain agnostic to these trends until as such time that some form of stability emerges**" — a governance decision about pacing, explicit in the text.

## (c) Complete clause inventory
**Core recommendation**: "The Medical Schools Council recommends that at the point of graduating from medical schools, doctors should be able to adequately perform the competencies listed below. Medical schools may wish to adapt this list to suit their own requirements."
**Domain 1 — Data governance and data management**: describe what constitutes health data; explain the importance of data governance policies; demonstrate understanding of data required for specific EHR functions (e.g., laboratory results, clinical decision support), "its location, accessibility, and record manipulation tracking."
**Domain 2 — Health informatics**: explain the concept; describe the role of health data/informatics in infectious and chronic disease contexts; "**Interpret and explain findings generated from health data to patients and the public**."
**Domain 3 — Professionalism, ethical, legal, and regulatory considerations in digital health**: apply best practices for managing digital patient data; describe protected health information; apply prevailing privacy and security rules; demonstrate compliance with ethical conduct and codes of practice when processing digital patient data; explain how professional, clinical, and research ethics are applied within digital health space.
**Domain 4 — Digital identity, safety, and security**: describe common behaviours of doctors which may compromise data security; demonstrate awareness of rules on sharing PHI via "cross-platform instant messaging services (e.g., WhatsApp, Telegram)"; explain the concepts of medical professional digital identity and digital intelligence.
**Domain 5 — Artificial intelligence in healthcare** (the core AI domain, complete):
- "Describe basic principles of artificial intelligence (AI), natural language processing (NLP), speech recognition, machine learning (ML), automated image interpretation and predictive analytics."
- "**Critique limitations of and barriers to using AI in healthcare**."
- "Explain the role of big data in healthcare data science and delivery."
- "Explain the importance of **rigorous real-world clinical validation of AI-based technologies before implementation in patient care**."
- "**Maintain vigilance and validate advice given by machines to avoid automation bias**."
- "Describe application of automated image interpretation and pattern recognition in radiology, radiotherapy, and retinal photography."
- Discuss integrating big data across sources (EHRs, hospital/medical records, examination results, internet-of-medical-things devices, wearables, biomedical research data) for insights into "healthcare provision, utilization, optimization, and improvement."
**Domain 6 — Clinical Academic Research**: advanced data analysis identifying patterns/trends/risk factors → new research questions; everyday clinical-practice data to assess treatment effectiveness and monitor drug safety; predictive models for "disease progression, treatment effectiveness, or patient risk."
**Domain 7 — Digital diagnostics algorithms**: describe how digital diagnostic algorithms "can enhance, guide, and record aspects of clinical examination"; "**Recognize the limitations of fully automated AI-led diagnostic platforms and traditional methods**."
**Domain 8 — Precision medicine**: describe concept and implications; wearable/mobile-app data supporting precision medicine.
**Domain 9 — Digital health literacy**: define and describe impact on outcomes and access; "higher health literacy leads to reduced healthcare costs, hospitalisation rates, readmissions and emergency department visits."
**Domain 10 — Health inequalities**: "Explain the need for datasets to be **diverse and representative of patient populations, including those from marginalised groups**"; "**Identify and mitigate potential biases in data and algorithms that can perpetuate existing health disparities**"; understand the digital divide; "Recognise the **heightened risk of data breaches and misuse for vulnerable populations**."
**Domain 11 — Personal health records**: positive/negative impacts on patient-centred care; appropriate use of and access to patient health records.
**Domain 12 — Foundations of health information systems**: positive/negative impacts of EHRs on patient care.
**Resources** (all free courses, complete list): King's College London "Introduction to Data Science for Healthcare Professionals" (FutureLearn, 6 weeks); Imperial College London "Digital Health – Design, Development, Evaluation" (Coursera); Strathclyde "The Power of Data in Health and Social Care" (FutureLearn); Groningen "Protecting Health Data in the Modern Age: GDPR" (FutureLearn); NIHR CRN "Introduction to Artificial Intelligence for Clinical Researchers" (NIHR Learn).
**Glossary** (18 terms, complete): AI; Automated Image Interpretation; **Automation Bias** ("the tendency to use automated cues (such as Clinical Decision Support alerts) as a heuristic replacement for vigilant information seeking and processing"); Big Data; Data Science; Digital Diagnostics; Digital Health Literacy; Digital Identity; Digital Intelligence; Health Informatics; Internet of Things; Machine Learning; Natural Language Processing; Pattern Recognition; Personal Health Records; Precision Medicine; Predictive Analytics.
**Appendix 1 — survey results**: 175 students/19 schools (173 analyzed); "most students from across all years of study **did not recognise any teaching as being specifically designated as 'data science'** but respondents recognised the importance"; "Students did not feel that the provision of data science training was sufficiently adequate... particularly by students in the later years"; 51 staff/21 schools confirmed "little to no data science training"; "Of the eight medical schools which offered data science training, **four did not go beyond the teaching of statistics**"; "Less than half of respondents suggested that their courses would increase the level of data science training."

## (d) Sophistication markers
- **Automation bias** appears as both a competency ("maintain vigilance and validate advice given by machines") and a glossary term — the only curriculum document in the corpus that operationalizes deskilling prevention as a graduate outcome.
- Consensus methodology with disclosed voting threshold (60%) and named membership — reproducible standard-setting.
- LLM-agnosticism is an explicit, reasoned deferral ("until some form of stability emerges") — pacing as policy.
- Empirical gap documentation (survey appendix): the document proves the deficit it addresses (half of schools offering "data science" teach only statistics).
- WhatsApp/Telegram clinical-messaging competency — realistic about how junior doctors actually communicate.

## (e) Distinctives vs sibling frameworks
- The UK's **undergraduate-medical-curriculum layer**, slotting between GMC standards (conduct) and HEE capability framework (workforce): MSC defines what a *graduate* must be able to do; HEE defines the whole workforce's ladder.
- DECODE lineage (JAMA Netw Open Delphi) gives it international evidence grounding no other national curriculum document here cites.
- Contrast with AFMC (Canada): MSC builds competencies inward; AFMC lobbies outward (misinformation regulation). Two opposite national-association strategies in one corpus.

## (f) Provisional classification
**progressive-structured** — consensus-derived, evidence-anchored curriculum framework; adoption-assuming yet technically careful (validation, automation bias, bias mitigation). Its conservatism is only about pacing (LLM-agnostic), which is a reasoned choice, not naivety.
