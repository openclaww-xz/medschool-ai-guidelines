# USMLE Program (NBME + FSMB) — "USMLE Program Discusses ChatGPT" (Feb 10, 2023)

## (a) Authority and authorship
- Publisher: the USMLE program, "a joint program of the FSMB and NBME" — i.e., the two bodies that own licensure assessment in the US. Unsigned program statement ("we"), posted to usmle.org. No committee named; institutional voice of the co-sponsors.
- Powers claimed: authority over the USMLE Step exams and their constructs. It binds examinees only through the exams' existing security/fairness rules; this post itself makes no new rules.
- Date matters: Feb 2023 — the **earliest document in this corpus**, three months after ChatGPT's public launch. This is a reactive damage-control/assessment-validity communication.

## (b) Policy philosophy
- Posture: **measured defense of assessment validity, with openness** ("optimistic... mindful of the risks"). Framing quotes:
  - "Although there is **insufficient evidence to support the current claims that AI can pass the USMLE Step exams**, we would not be surprised to see AI models improve their performance dramatically as the technology evolves."
  - "If utilized correctly, these tools can have a positive impact on how assessments are built and how students learn."
  - "While we are optimistic, we remain **mindful of the risks that large language models bring in terms of potential for misinformation and perpetuating harmful biases**."

## (c) Complete clause inventory
All substantive statements (descriptive-analytic; the post contains no must/should directives to examinees):
1. Contextualization: "Several studies have been conducted on the use of AI to answer multiple-choice test questions on medical knowledge. Some conversations about these studies seem to suggest that AI tools are correctly answering USMLE test questions and we wanted to provide some additional context."
2. Provenance check: "A review of the MedQA-USMLE database revealed that the study used test preparation materials from a third party unaffiliated with USMLE. Another study examined the results of ChatGPT using practice questions available at USMLE.org."
3. Construct-defense: "It's not surprising that ChatGPT was successful in answering these questions, as the input material is largely representative of medical knowledge available from online sources."
4. Construct-coverage limit: "the practice questions used by ChatGPT are not representative of the entire depth and breadth of USMLE exam content as experienced by examinees. For example, certain question types were not included in the studies, such as those using pictures, heart sounds, and computer-based clinical skill simulations. This means that other critical test constructs are not being represented in their entirety in the studies."
5. Evidence verdict: "there is insufficient evidence to support the current claims that AI can pass the USMLE Step exams."
6. Trajectory concession: "we would not be surprised to see AI models improve their performance dramatically as the technology evolves."
7. Conditional endorsement: "If utilized correctly, these tools can have a positive impact on how assessments are built and how students learn."
8. Research intent: "The USMLE co-sponsors (NBME and Federation of State Medical Boards) recognize the importance of these studies and their findings. In the future, we would be very interested in examining the questions that ChatGPT answered incorrectly and the implications of these results."
9. Commitment: "As the technology advances, we will continue to look for ways to enhance the assessment of skills and behavior so that we may evolve in tandem with medical education and potential changes to the practice of medicine."
10. Risk acknowledgment: "we remain mindful of the risks that large language models bring in terms of potential for misinformation and perpetuating harmful biases."

## (d) Sophistication markers
- **Assessment-validity threat understanding: first-rate and first-mover.** The post's core move is psychometric: distinguishing third-party prep materials ("MedQA-USMLE... a third party unaffiliated with USMLE") from actual exam content, and identifying untested constructs (pictures, heart sounds, "computer-based clinical skill simulations"). This is the earliest articulation in the corpus of "AI passing the exam ≠ exam compromised" — a construct-representativeness argument.
- Understands LLM failure modes at a general level ("misinformation," "harmful biases") — not the word hallucination, but the concept.
- Evidence-based posture: it audits the studies' materials rather than accepting headlines — "insufficient evidence" is an evidentiary verdict.
- Mandates: none; purely interpretive. No prohibition on examinee use stated here.

## (e) Distinctives vs sibling frameworks
- Earliest (Feb 2023) and uniquely **defensive-of-construct**: everyone else writes ethics; USMLE writes psychometrics. The "questions AI got wrong" research interest anticipates the entire AI-resistant-assessment agenda that NBME's later executive summary develops.
- Calibrated public tone — neither panic ("insufficient evidence") nor complacency ("would not be surprised").

## (f) Provisional classification
**cautious-informed** — genuinely understands the technology's measurement implications, cites the actual studies, makes no rules, and keeps options open. For a 2023 document this was sophisticated restraint; it is not pioneer because it commits to nothing beyond watching and evolving.
