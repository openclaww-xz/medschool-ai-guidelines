# Penn State College of Medicine — MD Student Handbook: Generative AI Tools in Educational Contexts

## Authority / Authorship
- Publisher: Penn State College of Medicine; MD Student Handbook "Policies" accordion (web-captured with plus-icon toggles), each policy cross-linked to a policy-management portal behind ePass login ("View this policy in the policy portal").
- GenAI section sits among MD-program policies (Credit Hour Determination, Forward Feeding Policy, etc.). No approval body, date, or owner named in the captured text itself — governance metadata lives in the linked portal.

## Philosophy (verbatim framing)
- Competency-protection framing: "As future professionals, students are expected to develop the skills necessary for autonomous critical thinking (e.g., clinical reasoning, systems thinking, etc.), patient and/or team communication, ethical professional behavior, and evidence-based decision-making. Overreliance on generative AI during the learning process may hinder the development of these essential competencies."
- The section's architecture is its philosophy: three pre-approved syllabus "models" (permissive / constructive-processes-only / prohibited) from which course directors choose.

## Complete Verbatim Clause Inventory
1. Mandate: "Course Directors must include course rules, outlined in the course syllabus, for use of generative AI tool use. Three possible syllabus statements approved by the College of Medicine are included here. These syllabus statements are suggestions; instructors are responsible for creating course rules concerning AI use that fit the learning objectives of the courses and are approved by program leaders."
2. Model 1 — "When students may use generative AI tools to complete certain assignments": "Students may not use generative AI tools to complete multiple-choice, matching, fill-in the blank, open-ended, or essay exam questions, or for written assignments such as SOAP or procedure notes. Students may use generative AI tools for other assignments but must indicate which generative AI tool they've used and how they've used it. To do that, students must include a statement at the end of the submitted assignment that adequately captures the extent of generative AI tool use. This policy applies to the use of all generative AI tools, including but not limited to Chat GPT, Grammarly, Copilot, and other AI tools, including those provided by the university."
3. Model 2 — "When students may use generative AI tools only for specific constructive processes": "Students may not use generative AI tools to complete multiple-choice, matching, fill-in the blank, open-ended, or essay exam questions, or for written assignments such as SOAP or procedural notes. Students may use generative AI tools to brainstorm ideas, to create an outline or headings for their paper, and to improve their use of grammar. Other uses of generative AI tools are prohibited. This includes the use of AI tools to create content for coursework, including the AI tools embedded in Grammarly. Students must also list the AI tools they've used as a 'Sourced Tool' at the end of the paper or project. This policy applies to the use of all generative AI tools, including but not limited to Chat GPT, Grammarly, Copilot, and other AI tools, including those provided by the university."
4. Model 3 — "When the use of generative AI tools is prohibited": "Students may not use generative AI tools to assist with the completion of any coursework. This policy applies to the use of all generative AI tools, including but not limited to Chat GPT, Grammarly, Copilot, and other AI tools, including those provided by the university."
5. Clinical environments: "Students may not use generative AI tools to assist with completion of patient care or patient write-up assignments, unless specifically endorsed by clerkship program directors or clinical preceptors and in accordance with HIPPA guidance and ethical standards." [sic — "HIPPA"]
6. Accountability and Integrity: "Students are fully responsible for the accuracy, originality, and ethical integrity of all submitted work, regardless of whether AI tools were used in the preparation process. Submitting AI-generated work as one's own without disclosure will constitute an academic integrity violation and misconduct."
7. Clinical/Scholarly/Professional Implications: competency-development passage quoted above.
8. Faculty Review and Enforcement — anti-detector stance: "Faculty are discouraged from using AI text and image detectors due to the imperfection of these tools and the risk of false positives. Emerging research at Penn State shows that faculty and students benefit the most from having explicit AI policies in course syllabi and having conversations about proper use and expectations in the classroom. If you do believe that a student has used AI in a way that violates academic integrity, you are encouraged to communicate with the student and, if necessary, file an official academic integrity violation and misconduct."
9. Ongoing Review: "This policy will be reviewed periodically to reflect advances in AI capabilities and evolving educational standards."
10. Scope of the tool definition (recurring across models): "including but not limited to Chat GPT, Grammarly, Copilot, and other AI tools, including those provided by the university" — note Grammarly counted as a generative AI tool (contrasts with VCU's grammar-tool carve-out).

## Sophistication Markers
- Menu-of-model-statements design (clauses 1–4): three COM-approved syllabus templates spanning permissive → constructive-only → total ban, with a mandate that every syllabus carry one — an implicit three-tier system implemented through syllabus statements rather than labels.
- Constant floor across all three models: no AI on any exam format (multiple-choice through essay) and none on SOAP/procedure notes — assessment and clinical-writing protection cannot be opted out of by choosing the permissive model.
- Grammarly-inclusion rule (2–4): explicitly sweeps grammar tools into the GenAI definition (and flags "the AI tools embedded in Grammarly"), closing the loophole other policies leave open; in tension with peers that exempt grammar checkers.
- Anti-detector position (8): the strongest detection-skepticism statement in cohort B — faculty "discouraged" from detectors citing false positives, backed by "emerging research at Penn State," preferring explicit policies and conversation.
- Disclosure mechanics: end-of-assignment statement of tool + extent (Model 1); "Sourced Tool" listing (Model 2).
- Clinical write-up exception path: clerkship director/preceptor endorsement + HIPAA — per-site opt-in rather than blanket ban.

## Distinctives
- Tiering lives in the syllabus statement, not the policy — an elegant, low-bureaucracy implementation of the tiered model (ECU labels levels; Penn State ships ready-made statements).
- Detection posture is the inverse of Tucson's (which permits plagiarism detectors).
- Only policy in cohort B that names its own institutional research on AI policy effectiveness.
- No PHI clause beyond the HIPAA reference inside the clinical clause; no data-privacy/approved-tools section; no faculty-use rules beyond detector discouragement; "HIPPA" typo.
- Handbook portal architecture (policy text in accordion + canonical version behind login) means the captured text may lag the portal version.

## Provisional Classification: progressive-structured
Justification: implements a de facto three-tier system with mandatory syllabus statements, a non-waivable assessment/clinical-writing floor, disclosure mechanics, an evidence-referenced anti-detector stance, and periodic review — more structural than any pure cautious-informed policy. Falls short of pioneer/Ecu-level completeness because tiers are optional suggestions ("These syllabus statements are suggestions"), there is no equity/access dimension, no faculty-side AI governance, no approved-tool regime, and privacy is handled in one clause. Buffalo and UMass surpass it clinically; ECU and GW surpass it systemically; Penn State leads on detection realism.
