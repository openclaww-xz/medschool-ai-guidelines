# Health CARE-AI Framework: Principles for Responsible AI Across HPE, Research, and Care (JMIR Med Educ, 2026)

## Research question
Can a consensus-validated, practice-level framework of principles for responsible AI use be developed that moves beyond high-level values (UNESCO/OECD/WHO/AMA/AAMC) to actionable expectations for clinicians, educators, learners, and institutions?

## Methods
3-phase modified Delphi (ACCORD-reported), University of Alberta REB-approved: Phase 1 — 2 international meetings (24 participants) + 3 focus groups (n=37: AI 12, HPE 14, ethics 11) drafting 10 principles from social-media professionalism guidelines; Phase 2 — REDCap survey, 5-point importance scale, inclusion ≥70% high ratings/<15% low; Phase 3 — include/exclude/undecided voting ≥70%.

## Sample
303 unique participants across phases. Round 1: 242 initiated/120 completed (49.6%); roles multi-select (HPE educators 64.3%, researchers 50.8%, clinicians 45%, AI specialists 43.7%, patient partners 20.6%); Canada 56.7%, US 32.5%. Round 2: 78 initiated/75 completed; 61 statements rated in R1.

## Key findings
- Round 1: 58/61 statements (95%) met inclusion; 1,887 comments (697 content-rich); 89% agreed draft principles outlined professionalism expectations.
- Round 2: all 10 principles + preamble included; 96% (72/75) final endorsement.
- Framework: preamble + 10 principles in 4 interdependent domains — values → competence → accountability → structural equity — deliberately spanning individual behavior to systemic change.
- Principle evolution example (P1): "personal responsibility" → "professional duty" → "individual AND collective duty" with institutions charged to establish expectations/policies and review tools — participant pressure institutionalized accountability.
- Notable content: equity split into practice-level bias mitigation (P8) and institutional equity-by-design (P9, retained despite redundancy questions); P10 sustainability (energy/carbon footprint + workforce) added from participant environmental concerns.
- Implementation: companion guide + toolkit with 4 scenarios; intended for curricula mapping and institutional governance (policy language, supervision protocols, AI-oversight committees).

## What it says about who leads policy
Explicit multi-level accountability: individuals, teams, AND institutions share responsibility; institutions set policies and review tools; professional/regulatory/accreditation bodies needed for scale. Positions itself as operationalizing AAMC/WHO/UNESCO principles that "remain early consensus statements rather than frameworks validated in practice"; cites AAMC curriculum snapshot documenting rapid uptake with "variability and lack of common standards."

## Limitations (authors')
No formal thematic analysis of qualitative data; English-speaking/high-resource dominance (Canada+US ~89%); role-based bias; Delphi convergence may mask dissent (least-objectionable wording); ~50% round-1 completion (attrition bias); policy lag constrains frontline agency.

## Relation to corpus findings
The most authoritative consensus framework in the corpus — and it explicitly diagnoses the gap our corpus fills: institutional policy variability and lack of common standards. Its 10 principles give our corpus a coding rubric: we can measure which CARE-AI principles (equity-by-design, sustainability, disclosure, oversight committees) actually appear in school and health-system policies — an empirical validation no Delphi study can perform.
