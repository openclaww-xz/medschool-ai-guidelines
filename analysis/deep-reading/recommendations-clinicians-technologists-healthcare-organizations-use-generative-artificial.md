# SGIM Position Statement: Recommendations for Clinicians, Technologists, and Healthcare Organizations on GenAI in Medicine (JGIM, 2024)

## Research question
(Normative position statement) How should clinicians, technologists, and healthcare organizations approach generative AI across clinical decision-making, health systems optimization, the patient-physician relationship, and ethics/equity?

## Methods
Position statement by SGIM committees (Clinical Practice, Health Policy, Ethics, Health Equity, Research); expert writing group consensus; approved by SGIM Council Apr 5, 2024. Domain structure selected by writing-group consensus consistent with physician-identified priorities.

## Sample
Not empirical — internal medicine (largest US physician specialty) expert consensus; supplementary examples of GenAI output.

## Key findings
- Clinical decision-making: GenAI as new CDS class; confabulation/hallucination and omissions the key weaknesses. Central "frameshift" recommendation aimed at technologists: abandon the assumption that clinicians will "supervise" GenAI — tools should meet clinical-workforce performance standards ("physicians should not need to question the accuracy of generative AI when designing a care plan," as pilots don't question GPS).
- Health systems: opportunities in access (automation, APP scope expansion, AI-delivered chronic care), population health visibility, and patient-safety infrastructure; challenges are leadership, workflow, monitoring infrastructure, inertia.
- Patient-physician relationship: documentation burden data (8,000–15,000 inbox messages/yr; 36 min EHR per visit; 26.7 h/day needed for all recommended services); GenAI to "restore humanism" via documentation/inbox/instructions/prior-auth; warning against "backfilling" freed capacity and layering AI on dysfunctional workflows.
- Ethics/equity: bias across the lifecycle (training data, unsupervised learning, implementation — e.g., AI-triage-first models disadvantaging groups); data privacy/monetization pressures; proprietary opacity vs pharma-grade regulation (FDA oversight "does not yet approach" it); scope concerns (insurer-mandated AI narrowing physician autonomy).
- Org-level recommendations: monitor AI performance internally; demand diverse training data, transparency, equitable outcomes; preserve physician agency; avoid rapid workforce displacement; pursue incremental AND transformative change.

## What it says about who leads policy
Tripartite accountability with distinct duties: professional society (SGIM) speaks for clinicians; healthcare organizations hold governance responsibility (monitoring infrastructure, procurement standards, workflow redesign); regulators (FDA) criticized as lagging; industry told to self-regulate to clinical standards. A national framework gap is implicit in the FDA critique.

## Limitations
Normative consensus, no empirical data; pre-dates widespread ambient-scribe deployments; US internal-medicine perspective; conflicts disclosed (several authors with industry ties).

## Relation to corpus findings
Key professional-society voice for the corpus's national-framework layer. Its org-level recommendations (monitoring infrastructure, equity demands, no backfilling) form testable items for our health-system policy documents: does the governance language in actual health-system AI policies match what SGIM asked organizations to do in 2024?
