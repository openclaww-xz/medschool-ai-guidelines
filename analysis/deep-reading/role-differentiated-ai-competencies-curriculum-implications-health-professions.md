# Role-Differentiated AI Competencies and Curriculum Implications for HPE: Qualitative Study (JMIR Med Educ, 2026)

## Research question
How do AI-driven changes in healthcare shape competency requirements across functional roles (users, developers, leaders), and what curriculum framework would cultivate role-specific competencies across the HPE continuum?

## Methods
SRQR-reported qualitative study; 13 subject-matter experts (SMEs) recruited via 4 independent channels (3 professional societies, academic profiling, national media identification, snowball) with 2-of-4 eligibility criteria; semistructured interviews (45–90 min, Oct 2022–Oct 2025, spanning LLM emergence); reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke) with published frameworks as sensitizing concepts; 4 independent coders, adjudication, member checking with all 13 SMEs; Lincoln & Guba trustworthiness; honorarium KRW 300,000 (~US$220).

## Sample
13 SMEs: 5 professors of medicine, 4 professors of dentistry, 2 dental practitioners, 3 healthcare-AI company executives (some dual-role); Korean context, cross-specialty; interviews over 3 years enabling early-vs-late temporal comparison.

## Key findings
- Theme I — AI-driven transformation (4 directions): patient empowerment/horizontal relationships; reconfiguration of medical expertise; workflow redesign via human-machine collaboration; raised "average" care quality (with deskilling risk — cites colonoscopy ADR decline after AI exposure).
- Theme II — role-differentiated framework (layered, shared foundations, asymmetric distribution; most clinicians = users):
  - Users (U1–U4): AI/ML literacy; data literacy; solution selection + human-machine delegation thresholds; AI professionalism (limitations awareness, final-arbiter accountability, empathic patient-centered care, lifelong learning).
  - Developers (D1–D4): advanced foundations; effectiveness evaluation across technical performance/clinical relevance/regulatory compliance; field-demand communication (bridging clinical-technical language gaps, specialty-specific definitions); challenging mindset/problem-solving.
  - Leaders (L1–L3): systemic insight/strategic planning; AI governance and policy design across the FULL lifecycle — selection/procurement, internal validation (vendor evidence reflects controlled conditions), deployment as integration, postdeployment monitoring for drift/bias/off-label use, with pre-defined accountability; resource management and trust-based collaboration.
- Curriculum mapped to modules per role; assessment suggestions (OSCE-style AI-professionalism stations, failure-mode simulations, governance policy briefs); UME core + elective developer/leader pathways (MD-PhD); expert disagreement on how much ML all students need.
- Temporal finding: expert emphasis shifted from foundational (early interviews) to operational/system-level concerns (late), but role categories stable across 3 years.

## What it says about who leads policy
Leader competencies ARE policy competencies: leaders must design policy "roads," govern procurement-to-monitoring lifecycles, and institutionalize internal validation where regulators can't adjudicate — institutional governance fills the regulatory gap. Notes AAMC developing UME→GME→CPD competencies; IACAI recommendations at individual/institutional/system levels.

## Limitations (authors')
Single East-Asian national context (Korea; HIPAA/GDPR/EU-AI-Act contexts differ); dental/imaging-heavy sample; hypothesis-generating; no inter-rater reliability stats; needs Delphi validation, pilot implementations, longitudinal evaluation.

## Relation to corpus findings
Supplies the competency-side logic for our corpus's policy categories: leader-role competencies (approved-tool procurement, internal validation, monitoring, accountability assignment) map directly onto the governance elements we can count in institutional policies. Corroborates Clin Teach: education "trails healthcare innovation," and leaders must build governance regulators haven't — our corpus shows who actually did.
