# Singapore MOH / HSA — Artificial Intelligence in Healthcare Guidelines (AIHGle) Version 2.0, March 2026

## (a) Authority and authorship
- Signed jointly by **Professor Kenneth Mak, Director-General of Health, Ministry of Health** and **Adjunct Professor (Dr) Raymond Chua, Chief Executive Officer, Health Sciences Authority** — regulator + products authority in one instrument. Published March 2026; updates AIHGle 1.0 "drawing on lessons from real-world deployment."
- Co-development disclosed: Academy of Medicine Singapore, College of Family Physicians Singapore, **Synapxe** (national health-tech agency), IMDA, PDPC, professional boards; acknowledgements list 22 organisations (incl. AWS, Google, private hospitals, patient alliances) and 16 named international individuals (Savulescu, Chesterman, Denniston, Atun, Hodges, Kingsland, etc.) plus MDDI/SingEx — global expert review.
- Legal spine: HPA 2007 (medical devices), HCSA 2020 (licensable healthcare services), PDPA 2012, professional Acts (MRA/NWA/AHPA), Civil Law (Amendment) Act 2020 s37 (standard of care), HBRA 2015; HSA GL-04 (software MD lifecycle), GN-13/14/15/17/18, GN-21 (change notification), GN-23 (labelling), IMDRF SaMD framework.

## (b) Policy philosophy
- Posture: **lifecycle-regulatory with mandatory human oversight**. Foreword verbatim: "We are committed to upholding an **agile governance framework that has patient safety at its core, whilst enabling innovation**."
- Core allocation: three stakeholder classes — Developers / Deployers (healthcare organisations, HCSA-licensed) / Users (healthcare professionals) — with responsibilities varying across a seven-stage lifecycle (Plan & Design → Develop → Evaluate → Deploy → Monitor → Review → End of life).
- Human-oversight mandate (4.1.3c): AI functions "much like other medical tools or devices... as aids that **enhance, not replace, professional judgment**... **human oversight is mandatory for all Clinical and Clinical-Ops use of AI**."
- Performance floor (4.1.3b): deployers "must also ensure that the standards of clinical outcomes and welfare of the patients and professionals under their purview are **maintained or improved compared to if AI were not deployed**."

## (c) Complete clause inventory
**Scope:** applies to ML/DL solutions (fixed and continuous-learning; DL incl. GenAI/LLMs, CNNs, GANs, RNNs); three use-case categories — **Clinical** (supports clinical decisions/judgment, direct impact on care outcomes: diagnosis, monitoring, treatment, alleviation), **Clinical-Ops** (part of clinical workflow, no direct impact on decisions: e.g., ambient scribe transcription/summarisation "for doctors' review," medication counselling/education), **Ops** (sector-agnostic operations — out of scope, deferred to IMDA frameworks). Researchers → HBRA + BAC 2025 report.
**Risk table (Figure 1):** Development risks — poor training-data quality/bias; insufficient testing across diverse populations; difficulty validating model reasoning; Deployment risks — integration failure; insufficient monitoring/audit; inadequate staff training; Use risks — misinterpretation/inappropriate use; delayed recognition of systematic errors or performance degradation; unexpected model behaviour "e.g. hallucination or undesirable content"; cross-cutting — data disclosure/breaches, adversarial attacks or prompts, unclear responsibility/liability, IP infringement.
**Ethical principles (Table 2, seven, each operationalized ×3 stakeholders):** (1) Safety (non-maleficence) — developers: rigorous testing, fail-safe features, value hierarchies; deployers: monitoring, periodic evaluation, adverse-event reporting avenues; users: understand limitations, contingency workflows with deployers. (2) Fairness — representative datasets, fair access; context validation, equitable access, monitoring for unintended biases; users apply clinical judgment to mitigate bias. (3) Transparency (autonomy) — disclose datasets/algorithms/evaluation/limitations, front-end AI labels; patient-communication policies; users proficient in the organisation's patient-communication frameworks. (4) Explainability (risk-tiered) — interpretable output; meet user expectations; understand broad logic and limitations to validate output. (5) Robustness — consistent output testing; standardised evaluation, routine accuracy/consistency assessment; users check consistency across time and patient profiles. (6) Security & data protection (secure-by-design: confidentiality, integrity, availability) — cybersecurity across lifecycle incl. Adversarial ML; data-sharing processes per law; user adherence to best practices. (7) AI alignment to human values — ethical frameworks/societal norms in development; deployment guidelines aligned to medical ethics/mission/patient welfare ("deployment of AI should not compromise clinical outcomes and patient welfare"); users "maintain their role as primary decision-makers, using AI as an assistive tool."
**Role formalisation (4.3):** document roles in SLAs (developer↔deployer) and SOPs (deployer↔user); purposes: clear accountability, appropriate tasking, risk/compliance mitigation incl. cross-jurisdiction.
**Ch. 5 Developers:** MD determination per HPA First Schedule (Table 3: 6 MD vs 6 non-MD examples — e.g., LLM giving clinical recommendations = MD; LLM explaining medication side effects or taking consent = non-MD; sleep-apnoea-detecting wearable = MD; wellness wearable = non-MD; fall-risk alerting = non-MD). **TPLC approach** (5.2): risk assessment, software verification/validation, change management, traceability; managed under ISO 13485 QMS; HSA premarket consultation (MD Development Consultation; Pre-submission for higher-risk). Planning/design/development (5.3): multidisciplinary teams; ethics from the start; representative data; **independent training/test sets** ("patients, data acquisition methods, temporal dependencies, feature engineering, and site-specific factors" dependence addressed); PDPA compliance for personal data (consent or valid grounds; security arrangements); **PETs** (data obfuscation, encrypted processing, federated analytics); synthetic data per PDPC guide but "real-world data is essential" for generalisation; secure-by-design per CSA guidelines (incl. Adversarial ML); **IFU** (intended use, indications, population, environment, input/output, limitations, UI interpretation, workflow integration, performance, prominent warnings); full documentation (data characteristics, version history, change control, traceability linking requirements/design/test/validation). Model evaluation (5.4): accuracy/reliability; industry benchmarks; **sensitivity and AUC**, comparison with standard of care. Post-market (5.5): periodic monitoring; feedback loops (built-in or workflow); **HPA-legislated adverse-event/FSCA reporting**; change notification (GN-21) — intended-use change = new registration or MD reclassification. End of life (5.6): exit plan — knowledge/data transfer, archival, disposal, asset recovery, alternative clinical processes; secure disposal (data sanitisation, de-activation, physical destruction). User communications (5.7): disclose datasets/algorithms/evaluation/limitations/risks/operating contexts; in-interface instructions incl. escalation protocols; training support; DTC emphasis.
**Ch. 6 Deployers:** Governance platform (6.1) with four processes: (a) assess/approve — "AI is fit-for-purpose, performs at least as well as current standard processes" + documented deployment decisions + risk/ethics deliberation (approval by Organisational Leadership — Board/Clinical Director/Chairman Medical Board, or owner/clinical lead for solo clinics); (b) **registry of all deployed AI with risk classification**; (c) guidance on safe deployment (integration, adverse-event procedures, patient comms); (d) cybersecurity/data-protection compliance with **permissible data types per solution**. Governance separation (6.1.3): three distinct functions — policy platform / implementation-monitoring-enforcement unit / audit unit. Buy/build/co-develop (6.1.4). Case study NUHS AIGC (policy, registry+monitoring, incident management, post-implementation audit). **Risk assessment framework (6.2)**: harm magnitude × probability; oversight taxonomy (Table 4): **Human-in-the-loop** (human retains full control; Clinical = minor-moderate risk, e.g., imaging flagging; Clinical-Ops = negligible-minor, e.g., scribe with doctor sign-off) / **Human-over-the-loop** (human supervises, can take over on unexpected events; Clinical = moderate-severe, e.g., AI diagnoses with doctors monitoring; Clinical-Ops = moderate, e.g., pharmacist-monitored medication-education AI) / **Human-out-of-the-loop** (no human oversight; Clinical = severe — "**Deployers should not deploy AI solutions that make clinical decisions independently without human making the final decision**"; Clinical-Ops = moderate-severe — same prohibition for e.g. robotic dispensing). Deployment approaches (6.2.3): start with less-critical contexts; "In Clinical or Clinical-Ops settings, **autonomous AI should not be deployed without human oversight**"; robust explainable models; black-box models need developer explainability + extra bias/anomaly detection. MOHT case: escalation-only models that "can never lower the severity of existing alerts." **Pre-deployment testing (6.3)**: organisation-level validation distinct from developer validation; pilot phases in controlled environments, attention to "unusual scenarios or clinic-specific contextual cues... (e.g. certain demographic groups)"; AI Verify framework; **clinical outcomes measurement** — "healthcare professionals and patients benefit from or maintain current care standards"; peer review of validation results (mitigates compounding bias); **AI-MD Exemption Sandbox (Feb 2026)** — low-to-moderately-low-risk AI-MDs across institutions without full licensing. Deployment (6.4): change management; AI champions (SingHealth NoteBuddy case: utilisation metrics — active users, logins, token consumption — plus satisfaction surveys); competency maintenance. Staff training (6.5): roles/responsibilities, professional standards, workflow integration, contingency plans, limitations, output interpretation, patient-communication protocols, data-input restrictions, abnormality detection, incident reporting; re-train on updates; three channels (self-paced, hands-on, seminars). Periodic monitoring (6.6): ad-hoc + regular; performance "minimally meets the clinical practice baseline"; feedback loop to leadership/authorities/developers with roles, timelines, **model-drift indicators**, response matrix. Adverse events (6.7): detect/respond (contingency: shut down AI, switch protocols), report (PDPC data breaches; HSA device adverse events; CSA cybersecurity/Critical Information Infrastructure), investigate (document; involve developer). End of life (6.8). Patient communications (6.9): autonomy/informed consent per SMC Ethical Code C5-C6; **materiality doctrine** — not all AI use is relevant (billing/scheduling); where AI use "has significant impact and require[s] informed decision-making," communicate "benefits... significant limitations, material risks... possible complications as well as alternatives... rather than the technical details of the AI solution"; calibrate extent via governance platform; no per-solution disclosure duty in multi-AI workflows ("not meaningful"); modality flexibility; **DTC duty: "patients are clearly informed that they are interacting with AI applications."**
**Ch. 7 Users (professionals):** 7.1 responsible for quality/safe care regardless of AI; professional judgment over (i) input data accuracy/appropriateness and (ii) output evaluation. **Table 5 (input/output accountability, complete):** Input — accountable: accurate data classification; compliance with privacy/security/handling per classification; appropriate use per legal requirements. Must: review all input incl. non-text files against the organisation's permitted security/sensitivity classification; **anonymise personal data whenever necessary or operationally possible**; **include instructions in prompts for output to include source citations** "if possible"; avoid IP-infringing prompts ("produce an image similar to [X's] works", logos). Output — accountable: accuracy/quality of all AI-assisted work; responsible/appropriate use in context. Must: assess suitability (inappropriate for task; falsehoods/inaccuracies/distortions/bias; confidential/sensitive content; IP infringement or liability/reputational harm); "fact-check, source-check, proof-read, verify, and adapt all output"; ensure compliance with PDPA etc. Training (7.2): understand intended use/limitations/workflow integration; "minimally be able to recognise limitations such as when the AI solution is not performing as expected... various abnormalities... **including hallucinations**"; analogy to robotic-surgery training; must adapt and respond; **"maintain proficiency in contingency and containment measures... understanding fail-safe mechanisms and maintaining skills in non-AI-augmented workflows to ensure continuity of care"** — a deskilling-prevention duty. Monitoring (7.3): proactive feedback to developers/organisations. Adverse events (7.4): report timely; "record... even if these are minor and do not cause adverse outcomes." Patient communications (7.5): informed-consent duty; discretion to share more; DTC → ensure patients clearly informed.
**Ch. 8 Emerging:** Continuous learning (8.1): drift + data-disclosure risks; developers monitor performance within acceptable limits; deployers train users "to identify signs of model drift"; users vigilant/escalate (IMDRF definitions). **GenAI (8.2)**: four amplified risks — hallucination; undesirable content ("mental health chatbot providing dismissive responses to user distress"); data disclosure; adversarial prompts. Mitigations: developers validate via human evaluation studies + use-case-specific qualitative/quantitative methods (ROUGE/BLEU/BERT-score named but flagged as limited for healthcare); **logit-based certainty estimation; aleatoric vs epistemic uncertainty taxonomy ("LLM's hallucinations... is more a form of epistemic uncertainty, and is inherent to LLMs and hard to cure")**; **red teaming** to "break" the model; design for fact-checking (citations, unknown-entity identification); **RAG to reduce hallucination**. Deployers: benchmark + red-team testing, component testing, redlines training, front-end AI labels. Users: critical evaluation, "clinical judgment as the primary decision-making tool," prompt-for-citations, scrub sensitive/identifiable output. MOHT mental-health-helpline case (AI supports empathetic listening; human decides). **DTC (8.3)**: all DTC AI-MDs/SaMDs must be HSA-registered; safety layers reduced outside care settings; interfaces in layperson language; "**DTC applications should not generate outputs requiring clinical expertise to interpret safely, even when accompanied by disclaimers**"; deployers communicate intended use/limitations/escalation; professionals recommending DTC must educate patients.
**Ch. 9** maps 24 instruments (see (a)); **Ch. 10** acknowledgements (above).

## (d) Sophistication markers
- Deepest technical content in the corpus: model drift, train/test independence sources, PETs, RAG, red teaming, logit-certainty, aleatoric/epistemic uncertainty, Adversarial ML, ISO 13485, IMDRF SaMD, AI Verify — none of it decorative; each is wired to a stakeholder duty.
- The oversight × risk matrix (Table 4) is a genuine decision procedure: prohibition bands for autonomous Clinical AI; escalation-only architecture (MOHT) as a design pattern for safety floors.
- Registry + risk classification + governance three-way separation (policy/operations/audit) is hospital-accreditation-grade governance.
- Materiality-based patient communication (significant-impact AI only; no per-tool duty in multi-AI workflows) solves the disclosure-burden problem pragmatically.
- Deskilling duty ("maintain skills in non-AI-augmented workflows") — explicit, named, operationalized for professionals; MSC UK makes it a competency, Singapore makes it a standing contingency duty.
- Sandbox instrument (AI-MD Exemption, Feb 2026) — regulatory experimentation embedded in the guideline.

## (e) Distinctives vs sibling frameworks
- The archetype of the **Singapore lifecycle-regulatory model**: product-lifecycle rule-making (TPLC), statutory hooks everywhere, mandatory human oversight, performance floors. Contrast GMC (conduct-only, near-zero AI text) and Australia (assessment design, no product regulation) — Singapore regulates the *tool* through its lifecycle while the UK regulates the *user* through judgment.
- Precision on the MD/non-MD boundary for ambient scribes and LLMs (Table 3) is unmatched: the same scribe technology is MD or non-MD by function, and the FAQ-grade examples make it usable.
- Education is absent as a domain — this is practice/deployment governance that a medical school would consume via its health system, not write.

## (f) Provisional classification
**progressive-structured** (governance architecture) with the strongest **cautious-informed** technical spine in the corpus — enabling innovation by statute-gated stages while prohibiting autonomous clinical decision-making outright. For a medical school's purposes it is the reference model for clinical-environment AI governance, not curricular AI.
