# System-Level AI Governance in Nursing: A Practical Framework (Nurs Adm Q, 2026)

## Research question
(Normative framework) How should nurse executives build system-level AI governance — organizational decision rights, oversight structures, risk management, lifecycle monitoring — for safe, scalable adoption?

## Methods
Conceptual synthesis of trustworthy-AI frameworks (WHO ethics & LMM guidance, NIST AI RMF govern/map/measure/manage, Joint Commission + CHAI guidance) combined with nursing professional governance principles; translation of trust domains into operational governance questions, decision processes, and monitoring expectations across the AI lifecycle.

## Sample
Not empirical. Anchored in US federal policy signals: White House March 2026 National AI Legislative Framework; HHS AI Strategy; Joint Commission/CHAI responsible-use guidance.

## Key findings
- System-level vs tool-level governance distinction: system-level = decision rights, oversight structures, risk processes, lifecycle monitoring; tool-level = validation/usability/local performance — the framework ensures tool-level work happens consistently with nursing decisional responsibility.
- 5 trust domains operationalized: clinical usefulness; safety & reliability (local validation, drift monitoring, escalation pathways, automation-bias safeguards, incident reporting, dashboards); fairness & equity (bias assessment pre-deployment, subgroup monitoring by race/ethnicity/language/SES/disability/geography, equity in vendor contracts); transparency & accountability (explainable outputs, audit trails, defined executive ownership); privacy & security (vendor due diligence, breach surveillance, data stewardship).
- Lifecycle workflow checklist (9 steps): intake & risk tiering → clinical-usefulness/workflow validation → safety/human-oversight design → fairness assessment → transparency/documentation readiness → privacy/security/procurement review → go/no-go approval → postdeployment monitoring & incident reporting → iteration or retirement/decommissioning.
- Operationalization: interdisciplinary AI governance committee (nurse executives, informatics, frontline nurses, physicians, quality, IT, data science, compliance); standardized intake and risk tiering; pilot-before-scale; predefined KPIs (adoption, outcomes, workflow, safety events, fairness metrics, user feedback); companion membership linking AI governance to professional nursing governance structures.
- Workforce: interprofessional training plans, competency review; agentic-AI automation-bias risk flagged; patient trust data (more trust when clinicians review AI output); American Academy of Nursing advocacy position cited.

## What it says about who leads policy
Explicit multi-level: federal (WH AI framework, HHS strategy) sets direction; Joint Commission/CHAI set governance expectations; WHO/NIST set principles; health-system governance committees (with mandatory nursing leadership) operationalize. Nurse executives positioned as system-level governors — the health system is the accountable unit.

## Limitations
Conceptual, no empirical evaluation; US health-system context; nursing-leadership vantage (physician/other perspectives thin); adaptation to organizational maturity asserted, not tested.

## Relation to corpus findings
The health-system governance blueprint our corpus can audit: 9-step lifecycle, 5 trust domains, committee composition, KPIs. Corroborates the policy-gap literature from the operations side (adoption "outpaced organizational structures"); our health-system policy documents can be scored against exactly this checklist — measuring governance-structure adoption across systems, which no single framework paper does.
