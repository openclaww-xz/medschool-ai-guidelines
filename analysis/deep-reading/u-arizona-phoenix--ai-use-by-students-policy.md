# University of Arizona College of Medicine – Phoenix — Artificial Intelligence (AI) Use by Students Policy

## Authority / Authorship
- Publisher: COM–Phoenix; policy page with metadata: applies to "All Students"; "Current Effective Date 08/11/2026"; "Original Approval Date 08/11/2026"; "Approving Committee: Curriculum Committee" (approval/reaffirmed 08/11/2026).
- Lineage: text is a revised sibling of COM–Tucson Policy 4-108 (Nov 2024) — same four-section skeleton (Definitions / Principles / Guidelines / Compliance), with 2026-dated edits and material divergences (below).

## Philosophy (verbatim framing)
- "It is the policy of the University of Arizona College of Medicine - Phoenix that students understand and adhere to established principles and guidelines for the appropriate use of artificial intelligence (AI) tools during their education. These principles and guidelines for AI use include ethical considerations, responsible usage, proper citation of AI tools, and HIPAA compliance in clinical documentation. Site specific policies and guidelines must also be followed when appropriate."
- Core principle (shared with Tucson): "AI tools should supplement learning and enhance educational outcomes, supporting— not replacing—the critical thinking and clinical reasoning skills essential to medical practice."

## Complete Verbatim Clause Inventory
Definitions
1. "AI, AI Tools: Products of the branch of computer science dealing with the reproduction or mimicking of human-level intelligence, self-awareness, knowledge, conscience, thought, and creativity in computer programs. Examples include, but are not limited to, ChatGPT, Claude, and OpenEvidence." (expanded named list incl. a clinical-Evidence tool; philosophically loaded definition)
2. "HIPAA: Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act" / "PHI: Protected Health Information"

Principles
3. Educational Enhancement: "AI tools should supplement learning and enhance educational outcomes, supporting— not replacing—the critical thinking and clinical reasoning skills essential to medical practice."
4. Course-level variance: "Individual policies around AI use may vary by course*. Students are responsible for verifying with course faculty whether the use of AI tools is permissible for specific assignments or tasks."
5. Transparency and Accountability: "Students are responsible for any AI-generated content they submit, even if flawed or biased. Verification of information and judgement of quality is expected."
6. "Students must be transparent about AI usage and accountable for their work. Any assistance from AI must be acknowledged and properly cited according to institutional guidelines."
7. Patient Confidentiality and Data Security: "The use of AI must comply with HIPAA and other relevant laws… AI tools used for clinical documentation must be certified as HIPAA-compliant."
8. "Students must ensure that PHI is only used with approved AI systems and shared in a secure manner. Refer to the following: University of Arizona AI Tools and Data Use Guide."
9. Ethical Standards: "Students are expected to adhere to the highest ethical standards in their use of AI. This includes avoiding plagiarism, ensuring the accuracy of information, and maintaining professional integrity."

Guidelines — Permitted Uses
10. "Learning and Research: AI may be used for educational purposes, such as studying, researching medical literature, and understanding complex topics."
11. "Administrative Tasks: AI may assist with non-clinical administrative tasks, such as scheduling and organization."
12. Clinical Use: "AI tools may be used for clinical knowledge acquisition or when researching clinical questions, and must adhere to the policies and guidelines of the clinical site and/or College of Medicine - Phoenix. The user is responsible for ensuring clinical use of AI is compliant with HIPAA regulations to ensure confidentiality and security of PHI."
13. AI Training Set Opt-Out (softened from Tucson's mandate): "Students are strongly encouraged to only use AI tools that allow users to opt out of having the data they submit to generative AI be used for the system's own training. This step is essential to protect data confidentiality and intellectual property."

Guidelines — Prohibited Uses
14. "Assessments and Assignments: All student contributions to any assessment or assignment must represent the student's own, original work. Unless explicitly permitted by a course* director, any use of generative AI for an assessment or assignment is prohibited."
15. "Non-HIPAA-Compliant Tools: AI tools that do not meet HIPAA standards must not be used for processing or storing any form of PHI."
16. H&P/A&P ban (broadened from Tucson's course-specific carve-out): "Histories, physical exams, assessments and plans: AI tools may not be used to write histories, perform physical exams, or create assessments and plans for patient care in the foundational and clinical phases of the curriculum."
17. "Unauthorized Patient Interaction: AI should not be used in direct patient interactions unless authorized by supervising faculty and in compliance with institutional policies."
18. "Intellectual Property and Compliance with Terms: When using AI tools, students must ensure that any content they input does not violate intellectual property rights or the terms and conditions governing the AI tool… Also refer to the following: University of Arizona AI Tools and Data Use Guide. Use of University-Sponsored Educational Material Policy."

Citation
19. "Any use of AI tools in assignments, research, clinical documentation, or other academic work must be clearly cited according to institutional guidelines."
20. "Students must describe the nature and extent of AI assistance, ensuring transparency and ethical responsibility."

Compliance and Review
21. "Any suspected or evident violations of AI use can be considered a potential professionalism report or honor code violation. Refer to the following policies: Professionalism Assessment Policy; Honor Code Committee and Procedures and Process for Dismissal."
22. Footnote: "Course* = any component of the curriculum where a grade is earned."

## Sophistication Markers
- Divergence management across a two-campus system: Phoenix keeps Tucson's skeleton but recalibrates three norms — (a) opt-out mandate softened to "strongly encouraged" (13), (b) Tucson's Doctor & Patient-course H&P exception generalized to a curriculum-wide ban on AI for histories, physical exams, and assessments/plans in BOTH foundational and clinical phases (16), (c) "Clinical Use" reframed as a permitted activity (knowledge acquisition, clinical-question research) with user-side HIPAA responsibility (12).
- Accountability clause is the bluntest in cohort B: "Students are responsible for any AI-generated content they submit, even if flawed or biased" (5).
- Links institutional infrastructure by name (UA AI Tools and Data Use Guide; Professionalism Assessment Policy; Honor Code dismissal process).
- Definition lists OpenEvidence alongside ChatGPT/Claude — acknowledges clinical-Evidence AI as a student tool category.

## Distinctives
- "perform physical exams" in the prohibited list (16) is a category error as literal text (no AI performs a physical exam) — presumably means documenting/interpretation, but as written it is the corpus's only exam-performance clause; signals drafting-by-analogy across campuses.
- Definition invoking "self-awareness, knowledge, conscience, thought, and creativity" — the most philosophical (and least operational) AI definition in the corpus.
- No procedures, no faculty obligations, no disclosure format, no approved-tool list, no detection stance (unlike Tucson's detector acknowledgment).
- Violations route to professionalism reporting + honor code with an explicit dismissal-process link.

## Provisional Classification: cautious-informed
Justification: same band as its Tucson sibling — formal policy with metadata, real HIPAA and IP clauses, and a defensible default-deny-on-assessments posture, softened by a permissive study/clinical-knowledge channel. Its broader clinical-documentation ban and accountability language show informed calibration; lack of structure (no tiers, disclosure mechanics, faculty duties) keeps it out of progressive-structured. Marginally more restrictive clinically than Tucson, marginally less strict on data-training hygiene.
