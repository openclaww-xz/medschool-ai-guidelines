# University of Melbourne — AI at Melbourne: For students

## (a) Authority and authorship
- Publisher: University of Melbourne (GAIT-adjacent student-facing guidance; part of the "AI at Melbourne" site).
- Audience: all coursework students (undergraduate + graduate); medical/clinical specificity: none — this is general university guidance, included here as the student-facing layer of Melbourne's model.

## (b) Policy philosophy
- Posture: **value-preservation with mandatory disclosure**. Signature framing verbatim: "GenAI tools can often mimic human outputs. It's important that you know **what value you add** in comparison to these tools and consider **what might be lost in outsourcing** to these tools. University educators are invested in supporting you to **graduate with the capabilities your degree claims you have**."
- On good use: "The use of GenAI tools should **add to your capabilities rather than providing a means of allowing you to offload or avoid challenging tasks**."
- Assessment authority is devolved: "Specific rules on whether GenAI is permitted for use are **set by the subject coordinator**."

## (c) Complete clause inventory
1. Value clause: know what value you add vs AI tools; consider what is lost in outsourcing (verbatim in (b)).
2. Degree-integrity clause: educators support you "to graduate with the capabilities your degree claims you have."
3. Training clause: a "short, **non-compulsory 'Introduction to GenAI' module**" covering what GenAI is, ethical questions, implications for learning and employment; commencing undergraduates see it in "Joining Melbourne" suite; **commencing graduate coursework students automatically enrolled**.
4. Permission clause: per-subject rules set by subject coordinator; "Some uses **may be prohibited** based on the nature of the subject and intended learning outcomes"; "Make sure you know the rules for each assessment."
5. Mandatory acknowledgment clause: "**In all cases**, the use of GenAI tools in the preparation of work for assessment **must be acknowledged**. Unacknowledged use of GenAI tools **constitutes academic misconduct** and is subject to the usual academic misconduct procedures of the University." — the only absolute rule on the page.
6. Anti-offload clause: GenAI should "add to your capabilities rather than... offload or avoid challenging tasks."
7. Detection stance: a section heading "About AI writing detection" exists (content not captured) — detection is addressed but not relied upon.
8. ancillary supports: translation/editing-tools guidance; "Acknowledging AI use"; "Referencing AI outputs"; student club ("Responsible Artificial Intelligence Development"); Melbourne Student Forum where "students can help shape University policy."
9. Provisioning clause: "Students have access to **Microsoft Copilot (Web)** as part of the MS365 licenses provided by the University." — institutional tool provision, equity-by-default.

## (d) Sophistication markers
- "What value you add / what might be lost in outsourcing" is a learning-theory framing (desirable difficulty) rare in policy prose — more sophisticated than generic "academic integrity" language.
- Named sections on referencing AI outputs and translation/editing tools show operational depth (citation format, ESL fairness) beyond most student pages.
- Institutional Copilot provision marks an equity move: equal access via licensing rather than exhortation.

## (e) Distinctives vs sibling frameworks
- The degree-as-claim framing ("capabilities your degree claims you have") is unique in the corpus — consumer-protection logic applied to credentials.
- Automatic enrollment of graduate students in the GenAI module (while "non-compulsory") is a nudge-architecture distinctive.
- Contrast: Sydney (same country) builds a two-lane assessment regime; Melbourne keeps coordinator-level discretion but adds the universal acknowledgment rule — lighter machinery, one bright line.

## (f) Provisional classification
**progressive-structured** — adoption-permissive, disclosure-mandatory, coordinator-devolved; one bright-line rule (acknowledgment) plus strong learning-value framing. Not naive: it names offloading as the core risk.
