# Use and Perceptions of GenAI Tools Among First-Year Medical Students: Cross-Sectional Survey (Adv Med Educ Pract, 2026)

## Research question
How do first-year medical students (undergraduate vs graduate entry) use AI tools, perceive their benefits/risks/trust, and does prior exposure to structured AI-literacy activities associate with differences in reported encounters with incorrect AI-generated content?

## Methods
Cross-sectional survey (SurveyMonkey), University of Nicosia, Cyprus; 3 first-year cohorts (MD2030 n=44, MD2031 n=46, GEMD2030 n=12); Cyprus National Bioethics approval; expert-reviewed (not psychometrically validated) instrument; ANOVA/χ² plus NLP pipeline on free text (VADER sentiment; TF-IDF + NMF topic modelling with negation tagging); MD2031 had completed a structured activity critiquing AI text with intentionally embedded errors before the survey.

## Sample
102 students (response rates 21–26%); MD cohorts mostly 17–33 yrs (mean ~19), female-majority; GEMD all degree-holders (mean 22.5 yrs), slight male majority; high English proficiency.

## Key findings
- Awareness 93–100%; ChatGPT use 87–100%; weekly+ use: MD2030 70.5%, MD2031 73.9%, GEMD 100% (daily 41.7%); 64–100% used AI before med school.
- Usefulness high (4.07–4.22/5); trust moderate (3.11–3.33/5) — consistent usefulness–trust gap; ~66–75% routinely cross-check AI outputs.
- Incorrect-AI-content encounters differed by cohort: MD2030 84.1% vs MD2031 52.2% (χ²=10.73, p=.0047) — the AI-literacy-exposed cohort reported FEWER encounters (interpretation cautious: training may alter recognition/reporting, not error rates).
- Attitudes: 70–75% say AI enhances (not replaces) learning; only ~50% support formal curricular integration; 72–75% would recommend AI tools.
- NLP: Q17 (errors) themes — incorrect factual info, misleading explanations, calculation errors, inappropriate responses; Q24 (improvements) — accuracy, explanation quality, curriculum integration, misinformation safeguards, UI, clearer sources; error-topics differed between cohorts (p=.0092), improvement-topics did not (p=.40); no sentiment difference between cohorts; error-sentiment more negative than improvement-sentiment (p<.001); errors reported did not predict improvements suggested (ρ≈0.11).

## What it says about who leads policy
Institution-level: "students engage with AI tools independently, often without structured institutional guidance"; authors urge schools to move beyond discouragement policies toward structured responsible-engagement approaches and AI-literacy curricula. No national-level claim.

## Limitations (authors')
Single institution; small GEMD cohort; self-report (recall/response bias); cross-sectional (no causality for the literacy-exposure association); no objective learning outcomes; topic model not externally validated (no inter-rater reliability); low response rates.

## Relation to corpus findings
Adds the "AI-literacy exposure" moderator the survey literature usually lacks (echoing the US national study's finding that AI understanding predicts conscientious use, and the physiotherapy study's literacy gains from structured teaching). Confirms the pattern behind the Clin Teach gap: universal adoption, moderate trust, self-taught norms — under absent institutional guidance. Our corpus measures whether structured guidance/curricula exist at scale.
