# WHO Regional Office for Europe — Artificial intelligence is reshaping health systems: state of readiness across the WHO European Region (2025 survey report)

## (a) Authority and authorship
- Issued by the WHO Regional Office for Europe (UN City, Copenhagen; publication code WHO/EURO:2025-12707-52481-81028). A intergovernmental-secretariat report built on a 2024–2025 survey of the 50 responding Member States of the European Region (53 members; 50 responded), conducted under the regional digital health action plan 2023–2030 (RC72/R2 resolution).
- Not a guideline with normative force: it is a readiness assessment plus a "way forward" action menu. It binds no one; its authority is convening and comparative-evidence authority. It sits downstream of WHO HQ's "Ethics and governance of artificial intelligence for health" (2021) and "Regulatory considerations on artificial intelligence for health" (2023), which it cites repeatedly as its normative anchors.
- Audience: ministries of health, national AI-strategy and data-governance bodies, regulatory agencies.

## (b) Policy philosophy
- Posture: governance-enablement at system level — neither physician-facing embrace rhetoric nor prohibition. The framing is "promise balanced by significant challenges": "AI has begun to shift how care is planned, delivered and governed, promising more efficient services, improved patient outcomes and reduced pressures on overburdened health workforces. However … this promise is balanced by significant challenges, gaps and uncertainties that must be carefully navigated."
- Distinctive statist framing: the unit of action is the Member State, not the clinician or the school. Emphasis on strategy, funding, liability law, data hubs, regulatory agencies, cross-border harmonization (EU AI Act, EHDS, Product Liability Directive, Council of Europe Framework Convention on AI).
- On regulation: "Regulation not only mitigates risk but also fosters responsible innovation, public trust and equitable access. Without safeguards, AI systems may perpetuate bias, harm patients or undermine rights. Yet overly strict or fragmented rules can hinder innovation and access."
- On workforce: literacy-enablement — "Health care professionals need training that extends beyond technical skills, fostering critical thinking, ethical judgement and a strong understanding of AI's practical risks and benefits."

## (c) Complete clause inventory (normative statements, quoted verbatim, by theme)
The normative content is concentrated in Section 4 "The way forward" action bullets (imperative mood, addressee = Member States). All quoted verbatim:

### Strategy and oversight ("The navigators")
- "Develop and/or update national strategies, whether health-specific or cross-sectoral, that set a clear vision aligned with health priorities and integrate with broader development plans."
- "Set time-bound objectives with robust monitoring and evaluation frameworks to track progress and ensure accountability."
- "Ensure strategies involve stakeholders across sectors and provide mechanisms for sustainability and adaptation to technological advancements."
- "Assign management, oversight and implementation of AI strategies to a well-established government agency or multiple agencies rather than temporary structures, to ensure continuity, accountability and sustained execution."

### Stakeholder engagement and workforce ("The change-makers")
- "Involve end users, the public and industry in codesign and coregulation processes to identify ethical concerns, enhance accountability and build trust."
- "Create platforms and dialogues that improve transparency around data sharing and promote culturally acceptable AI applications."
- "Integrate AI-related content into preservice curricula, in-service training and continuing professional development to equip the health workforce with a solid understanding of AI benefits, risks and ethical considerations." ← the medical-education clause; preservice curricula = UME/GME
- "Ensure ongoing training for relevant stakeholders to stay informed on evolving ethical, legal and regulatory requirements, embedding these considerations throughout the AI design lifecycle."

### Law/regulation ("The guardrails")
- "Establish clear responsibilities for developers, clinicians, data providers and institutions, with mechanisms for timely redress and accountability when AI systems cause harm."
- "Ensure stakeholders understand key AI components, such as data sources, algorithms, decision-making processes and limitations, while respecting proprietary rights; validate safety, reliability and real-world effectiveness through prospective trials before deployment to clinical practice and broader health system use."
- "Integrate ethical guidelines and incentivize responsible design by embedding ethical, legal and technical standards into precertification programmes. Encourage developers to adopt safety- and human-rights-by-design approaches from the outset…"
- "Expand postmarket surveillance and establish mechanisms for ongoing monitoring of AI products in health to ensure safety, effectiveness and adherence to standards."
- "Invest in regulatory agencies tasked with both approving and monitoring AI in health and promote cross-country knowledge-sharing to improve governance practices."

### Data governance ("The backbone")
- "Align health-data governance with international standards to protect individual rights, including informed consent, transparency and independent oversight."
- "Ensure special protections for marginalized groups and promote public participation in data-sharing decisions."
- "Set high standards for health data hubs by requiring precise consent procedures, demonstrable public benefit in data-sharing agreements and good-practice networks…"
- "Define clear rules for data access, consent and benefit-sharing, including collaborations with the private sector, while ensuring public benefit, transparency and protection of individual rights."
- "Develop guidance for the secondary use of health data in public-interest research and establish clear rules to enable secure and ethical cross-border data sharing."

### Deployment ("The catalysts")
- "Align AI applications with patient interests and national health goals, communicating capabilities, conditions and limitations transparently."
- "Strengthen funding mechanisms, create implementation roadmaps and ensure integration of AI tools into existing health system workflows."
- "Implement standards for developers and mandate independent pre- and postdeployment impact assessments."
- "Monitor AI systems continuously to detect bias, performance drift and potential harms." ← explicit drift clause
- "Perform pre- and postdeployment assessments guided by international standards, independently audited, with results publicly available."

### Adoption barriers ("The gatekeepers")
- "Leverage regulatory sandboxes to enable regulators, developers and health institutions to collaborate in real-world but lower-risk settings…"
- "Evaluate AI solutions against non-AI alternatives (e.g. established decision-support systems or other digital health tools) and ensure alignment with ethical and human rights standards prior to adoption."
- "Ensure that public–private partnerships operate transparently with public disclosure of agreements, uphold individual and community rights by securing ownership or access to AI technologies, and clearly define which health care responsibilities remain public and which are delegated to private actors."

### Descriptive risk statements with normative undertone (findings text)
- "potential risks must also be addressed, including biased or low-quality outputs, automation bias, erosion of clinician skills, reduced clinician–patient interaction and inequitable outcomes for marginalized populations."
- "gaps in workforce training can lead to overreliance on AI, erosion of clinical judgement and challenges in critically evaluating outputs."
- Environmental: LMMs' "significant energy consumption and resource demands … contribute substantially to carbon emissions and water usage, raising sustainability concerns"; only 20% of Member States impose legal requirements on developers to address this.

## (d) Sophistication markers
- Technically literate and evidence-citing: 41 numbered references including WHO 2021/2023 AI guidance, EU AI Act (Reg. 2024/1689), AI Liability Directive, Bender et al. "stochastic parrots," Wynants BMJ COVID-model appraisal, Vayena PLOS Med, OECD AI Principles, NICE DG57 stroke guidance, HunCRC pathology dataset paper. Heavy empirical base: ~30 figures/tables of survey percentages by subregion.
- Names the specific failure modes: "automation bias, erosion of clinician skills," "performance drift," generative-AI/LMM-specific legal gaps (only 3/50 Member States have health-specific GenAI legal requirements), environmental footprint. Case studies with outcome data (Brainomix 360: thrombectomies +280%, independence 34%→55%; Hungary colorectal CNN AUC 0.73–0.98; Türkiye mammography screening; UK AI Airlock sandbox).
- What it does NOT do: no hallucination-specific discussion, no agentic AI, no ambient scribes (closest: "dictation tools to support speech to text," "summarizing EHRs" listed as established applications), no assessment-validity discussion — education is treated as workforce training, not as an assessment-integrity problem.
- Mandates nothing (it cannot); it gestures to Member States with imperative recommendations and quantifies how few currently comply (e.g., 60% have issued no practical legal/ethical risk guidance; 64% no AI health policies at all).

## (e) Distinctives vs sibling frameworks
- Only document in the corpus operating at intergovernmental/system level: its unit of analysis is the state, its instruments are treaties, statutes, data hubs, and regulatory agencies — vs. professional societies' clinician-facing ethics and schools' curricula.
- Unique empirical contribution: readiness percentages across 50 countries (e.g., 46% assessed legal gaps; 54% have approval agencies; only 8% liability standards; 66% health data strategies; 68% health data authority; 64% using AI-assisted diagnostics; 98% prioritize patient outcomes; top barriers legal uncertainty 86% and affordability 78%; top enablers liability rules 92% and transparency guidance 90%).
- Northern/western Europe vs central Asia gradient documented throughout — an equity-of-governance finding no US body makes.
- Subregional tables (Tables 2–6) enable benchmarking; no US framework offers comparable multinational data.

## (f) Provisional classification: progressive-structured
Justification: maximally evidence-based and structurally specific (percentages, subregional tables, named legal instruments, case studies with outcome metrics), and its recommendations are imperative and operational (mandate impact assessments, expand postmarket surveillance, integrate AI into preservice curricula). Falls short of "pioneer" only because it is descriptive-comparative rather than prescriptive-first: it invents little new normative language, leaning on WHO HQ 2021/2023 guidance and EU law, and it has no education-specific depth (no assessment validity, no student use policy).
