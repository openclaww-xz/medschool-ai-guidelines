# Buffalo Jacobs School of Medicine — GenAI Use Policy for Medical Students

**Classification: progressive-structured (near-pioneer) — best clinical-environment policy in cohort B** (run-28 rank #3; `analysis/deep-reading/28-cohortB-synthesis.md`; included per task context as the named Buffalo pioneer)

## Who drove it

The **Office of Medical Education**, as a dated web policy in the OME policies list ("Revised: March 31, 2026"), with a defined feedback loop: "The Office of Medical Education will track student comments and criticisms of this policy and inform the Curriculum Committee" (`raw/us-med-schools/buffalo-jacobs--genai-use-policy-medical-students.md`; `analysis/deep-reading/buffalo-jacobs--genai-use-policy-medical-students.md`). OME also commits to "provide suggested language to support consistent messaging across courses and rotations" — the policy comes with a faculty-support unit attached, which run 28 identifies as the precondition that makes default-permit models work.

## Institutional context

Two enabling layers: (1) **UBIT (university IT) tool approval** — "the only generative AI tools approved by UBIT is the version of Microsoft Copilot that requires a UBIT login and the Semantic Clinical Artificial Intelligence (SCAI) chatbot," tying permissions to contractual data protection; (2) an existing clinical-education supervision culture the policy can regulate through — faculty/residents as role models, clerkship structures, Promotions Committee escalation.

## What they built and why it is pioneer-grade

1. **The four-verb clinical supervision framework**, operationalized from published literature (Sedhom & Torious [sic], NEJM 2024;390(6):503-505): faculty are responsible for "**Detecting** when AI has been used in student work or clinical tasks, **Evaluating** whether that use is appropriate in the given context, **Framing** AI as a tool that can complement, but never replace, clinical reasoning, and **Teaching** students to integrate AI outputs with evidence, patient values, and sound judgment." This is the corpus's only policy that regulates the *supervision relationship* around AI rather than the tool — treating AI as inevitable in clinical environments and governing how attendings teach with it.
2. **The BAA requirement**: "Faculty are responsible for ensuring that clinical site-deployed tools that they approve for student use are covered by a Business Associate Agreement between the clinical site and the vendor" — the only cohort-B policy to reach vendor-contract level. This legitimates Epic-embedded AI and ambient documentation assistants *under faculty approval + BAA* instead of banning them.
3. **Anti-ban safeguard**: "Faculty may not implement blanket bans on the use of generative AI without consultation with and approval from the appropriate Phase leadership" (one of two in cohort B, with GW).
4. **Enumerated HIPAA consequences** — clerkship failure, Promotions Committee referral, dismissal — and an "err on the side of caution" consultation duty for uncertain cases.
5. **Deliberately conservative clinical-documentation line**: H&P/note generation prohibited "regardless of whether patient data is included" — a defensible training-stage position taken explicitly, not by omission.

## What they explicitly chose NOT to do

- No detection regime; integrity runs through disclosure and the supervision framework.
- No blanket clinical ban: site-deployed tools are permitted under supervision + BAA — the policy declines the easy absolutism of "no AI in the clinic."
- No tier labels or assessment taxonomy — permitted/prohibited menus at fine granularity instead.
- Notably broad citation duty (even editing/idea-gen "should be cited") — they chose over-disclosure over under-disclosure, accepting noise for transparency.

## Borrowed vs original

Borrowed: the NEJM supervision framework (cited with DOI — though the deep-read flags the DOI as malformed, a citation-hygiene flaw). Original: the BAA clause, the anti-ban safeguard, the enumerated HIPAA consequence ladder, and the OME comment-tracking loop. Per run 28, the anti-ban safeguard appears at exactly two schools (Buffalo, GW) — a small borrowing pool, likely coincidental convergence.

## Weaknesses / what's missing

- **No equity-of-access provisions and no tier labels** — the two reasons it sits below ECU/GW in run 28's ranking.
- No secure internal deployment beyond UBIT Copilot/SCAI; no assessment redesign.
- The supervision duty is assigned to faculty without any described faculty development or time support — a four-verb duty with no resourcing plan.
- Citation demand covering even grammar-level help is likely unenforceable and breeds disclosure noise (run 28's default-permit weakness).
- Citation-hygiene flaw (malformed DOI) in its single scholarly anchor.
- No governance metadata beyond the revision date: no approving committee is named for the policy itself, and the review cadence is "frequently... as needed" rather than dated — weaker document control than ECU or UAMS.

## What to steal concretely

1. The **Detect/Evaluate/Frame/Teach supervision framework** — the correct template for any clerkship director: it converts AI oversight into teachable supervisory behaviors. Assign it by name in clerkship orientation.
2. The **BAA requirement for site-deployed clinical AI** — one sentence that legally gates every ambient-scribe and Epic-embedded tool a student might touch.
3. The **anti-ban clause with Phase-leadership approval** — prevents individual faculty opt-out anarchy while preserving course-level calibration.
4. **OME-owns-messaging**: suggested syllabus language, Q&A, and tracked student feedback — the administrative spine that keeps a permissive policy from drifting.
5. The **enumerated HIPAA consequence ladder** — students should be able to read exactly what a PHI breach costs before they make one.
