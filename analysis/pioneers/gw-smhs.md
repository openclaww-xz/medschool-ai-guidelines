# GW School of Medicine & Health Sciences — Guidelines for the Use of AI (MD Program)

**Classification: pioneer — best disclosure architecture in the corpus** (run-28 cohort B rank #2; `analysis/deep-reading/28-cohortB-synthesis.md`)

## Who drove it

"Created by a multidisciplinary task force of MD program faculty, staff, and students, chaired by Ioannis Koutroulis, MD, PhD, MBA" (April 2026), effective "upon approval by the GW SMHS Committee on Undergraduate Medical Education (CUMEC)" (`raw/us-med-schools/gw-smhs--md-student-ai-use-guidelines.md`; `analysis/deep-reading/gw-smhs--md-student-ai-use-guidelines.md`). Three authorship features stand out: a **named chair** (rare — most policies are anonymous); **student membership** on the drafting body; and a closing footnote disclosing the task force's own AI use — "the task force used Microsoft Copilot to (1) brainstorm and generate initial ideas, (2) help structure an outline... all conceptualization, interpretation, and argumentation were developed by the taskforce... They take full responsibility for the final content." The policy models its own disclosure norms.

## Institutional context

A 606-line apparatus-rich document (the longest in cohort B) built on GW's existing data-classification regime and professionalism infrastructure: it links the MD Program Code of Conduct in the Learning Environment policy, FERPA/HIPAA frameworks, and university IT security policy. The genre choice is itself context: these are "guidelines" deliberately — softer than policy — because the task force opted for cultural intervention (faculty development, destigmatized disclosure) over rule enforcement.

## What they built and why it is pioneer-grade

1. **A disclosure culture, engineered.** Mandatory disclosure elements (tool name and version; purpose and nature of use; the user's intellectual role), a fill-in-the-blank eight-field template adapted from **AMEE Guide No. 192** (Cleland, Driessen, Masters, Lingard, Maggio, *Medical Teacher* 2025), model syllabus language in three flavors (permissive/pitfall/citation), and prompt-log retention expectations ("Course directors/faculty may request in advance that AI users save a log of relevant prompts and output").
2. **The no-punishment-for-disclosure guarantee**: "disclosure of permitted AI use, by itself, should not result in disciplinary or professionalism actions" — paired with an explicit cultural program: students "are not asked to 'confess' AI use, but simply to document it in the same way they might document other learning supports," and faculty development will teach that disclosed use "should not be interpreted as laziness." This is the only policy in the corpus that designs the *incentives* for honesty rather than just demanding it.
3. **Data classification taught, not asserted**: Appendix 5 walks Regulated/Restricted/Public with six worked scenarios — including the anti-HIPAA-washing hard line: PHI entry into public tools "is never permitted... even if the AI tool developers claim that it is HIPAA compliant."
4. **Professionalism scenario tables**: six student scenarios and a faculty poor-modeling table (undisclosed AI teaching materials; uploading last year's exam+key; entering grades into public AI; unapproved AI scribe in patient care) each mapped to typical response and educational emphasis — policy-to-UVME-professionalism bridging.
5. **Anti-ban safeguard**: faculty "should not issue blanket bans on AI use without permission by the Office of Medical Education" (one of only two in cohort B, with Buffalo).

## What they explicitly chose NOT to do

- No tier labels: "permitted, limited, prohibited" distinctions exist but "are not systematized into levels as at ECU" — consistency is left to faculty uptake (the stated trade-off).
- No detection regime; no punitive framing.
- GME, admissions, and clinical operations are explicitly excluded — "governed by separate institutional, hospital, or accrediting-body policies."
- Fully automated grading banned outright for faculty ("Fully automated grading or summative assessment by AI is not appropriate").

## Borrowed vs original

Borrowed with attribution: AMEE Guide 192's disclosure framework (international medical-education literature, not US national bodies — consistent with run 32's finding that disclosure frameworks flow through AMEE; `analysis/deep-reading/32-international-synthesis.md`). Original: the disclosure-safety guarantee, the "confess → document" reframing, the professionalism mapping tables, the vendor-HIPAA-claim hard line, and the task-force self-disclosure footnote.

## Weaknesses / what's missing

- **No systematized tiers** — "held below ECU only because tiers are described, not systematized, so consistency rests on faculty uptake" (run 28).
- Longest document in the cohort: apparatus richness risks faculty non-readership; the syllabus mandate is "encouraged," not required.
- No named provisioned tools (GW-approved tools referenced generically; "Future GW local tools" still aspirational in Appendix 5).
- No assessment redesign — disclosure-heavy, architecture-light.

## What to steal concretely

1. The **no-punishment-for-disclosure guarantee** plus the neutral-language principle — without this, every disclosure regime underperforms because disclosure is rational only when safe.
2. The **AMEE-192-derived eight-field disclosure template** — borrow the source directly (it is published medical-education literature).
3. The **data-classification appendix with worked PHI/FERPA/restricted scenarios**, including the anti-HIPAA-washing sentence — the sharpest vendor-claim clause in the corpus.
4. **Faculty poor-modeling table** — governing faculty AI misconduct with named examples is rarer than it should be.
5. The **anti-ban clause** (blanket bans need OME approval) — the strongest structural signal that a school treats AI as a competency rather than a threat (`analysis/deep-reading/28-cohortB-synthesis.md`, cross-cohort observations).
6. **Task-force AI-use self-disclosure footnote** — adopt for any policy drafted after 2024.
