# OHSU School of Medicine — GME Policy 48: AI Use in Graduate Medical Education

**Classification: pioneer (GME niche)** (run-29 cohort C; `analysis/deep-reading/29-cohortC-synthesis.md`)

## Who drove it

School of Medicine Policy **GME 48**, effective 2/12/2026, owned by the **GME office**, amended by the **GMEC** (Graduate Medical Education Committee) — with a version table showing v1 (GMEC, 12/18/2025) and v2 (2/12/2026, "Addition of language as recommended by AI Governance Committee") (`raw/us-med-schools/ohsu-som--gme-48-ai-use-in-gme.md`; `analysis/deep-reading/ohsu-som--gme-48-ai-use-in-gme.md`). Institutional actors: the **OHSU AI Governance Committee** (tool registry via IT Security portal, pilot approvals), the Office of Research Integrity, and ISP-25 (OHSU's AI Information Security Policy). Unusually, the policy **credits its source**: "UT Health San Antonio GME AI Policy — GME AI policy used as resource in development of OHSU GME AI policy" — documented cross-institutional lineage.

## Institutional context

OHSU's AI Governance Committee and information-security policy stack (ISP-23 sanctions, ISP-25) predate and absorb GME-48: the policy doesn't create governance, it routes trainees into it. Oregon's single-state AHSC structure means one committee can govern clinical AI across the enterprise the residents work in. The 2025→2026 versioning shows a live amendment cycle driven by the governance committee's recommendations.

## What they built and why it is pioneer-grade

GME-48 covers the resident-specific risk surface no UME policy reaches (run 29, §4.1):

1. **Milestone-gated AI access**: "Individual programs may restrict or prohibit the use of certain OHSU-approved AI tools by trainees until the program has determined the trainee has reached pre-determined educational milestones" — educational-sequence thinking: AI privileges are earned as competencies develop, exactly mirroring how procedural privileges work.
2. **AI-scribe consent with documentation**: "Patient consent and disclosure are required prior to use of AI scribes or documentation tools including documentation of the consent" — consent is not a one-time notice; it is per-use and charted.
3. **Applicant-screening control**: "Using AI to evaluate, rank or score residency or fellowship applicants unless the process is approved by institutional leadership and uses OHSU-approved methods" is prohibited — governing the era's GME flashpoint (contrast UKY GME's blanket never-independently-screen rule; run 29, §4.1).
4. **CCC/evaluation-data protection**: evaluations, milestones, and CCC data may never enter non-approved tools — learner-data protection extended to the resident context.
5. **Cognitive-bias-aware definitions**: automation bias, confabulation, and algorithmic bias defined in the policy itself, with a documentation duty that includes "examination for confabulations, hallucinations" of AI-assisted notes under mandatory faculty oversight.
6. **Three-tier use structure**: Prohibited (disciplinary) / Permitted with Attribution / **Encouraged** ("Using institutionally approved AI for education, workflow efficiency, or patient communication enhancement") — a permission gradient ending in active endorsement, not grudging tolerance.
7. **Verbal-attribution exemplar** for clinical teams: "(e.g., 'the differential I am presenting was generated by AI')" — norm-setting at the point of communication.

## What they explicitly chose NOT to do

- No blanket tool bans: approved tools are usable, programs may only restrict pending milestones.
- No detection regime for coursework integrity — attribution and disclosure duties instead.
- No enterprise overreach: at non-OHSU sites, "that institution's policy governs" — site deference.
- Not a UME instrument: preclinical/coursework AI is left to other policies (OHSU's PA program policy is separate).

## Borrowed vs original

Borrowed and credited: the **UT Health San Antonio GME policy** as the structural template — run 29's key diffusion finding: "OHSU GME-48 credits UT Health San Antonio — a state school — as its source. The diffusion network is state-school-to-state-school; 'elite origin' is not the dominant propagation pattern" (`analysis/deep-reading/29-cohortC-synthesis.md`, pattern 2). Original: milestone gating, charted per-use scribe consent, the confabulation-examination duty, and the encouraged tier.

## Weaknesses / what's missing

- Narrow scope by design: GME only; UME (the school's own MD/PA programs) is governed elsewhere.
- The applicant-screening rule allows institutionally approved AI screening rather than banning it — defensible, but weaker protection than UKY's.
- No equity provisions; no faculty-development obligations beyond modeling.
- Depends on the AI Governance Committee's tool registry staying current — governance bottleneck risk shared with UAMS.

## What to steal concretely

1. **Milestone-gated AI privileges** — the single most exportable GME idea: AI tools join procedures as competentially-gated privileges, with the program (not the institution) setting the milestones.
2. **Per-use, charted scribe consent** — one clause; makes consent auditable in the EHR.
3. **Applicant-screening control clause** — if AI touches selection, it needs institutional approval and approved methods; write the rule before the vendor demo arrives.
4. **CCC/evaluation-data as a protected class** — most schools protect PHI and forget learner data.
5. **Verbal-attribution exemplar** — give trainees the literal sentence to say on rounds.
6. **Credited policy lineage** — name your source policy in the resources table; it is how the state-school diffusion network functions and how your school's improvements become findable by the next one.
