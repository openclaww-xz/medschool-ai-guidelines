# University of Sydney — Two-Lane AI Assessment Policy (+ implementation FAQ)

**Classification: pioneer — the corpus's most intellectually coherent assessment regime** (run-32 international; `analysis/deep-reading/32-international-synthesis.md`)

## Who drove it

Named institutional leadership: **Professor Joanne Wright, Deputy Vice-Chancellor (Education)** and **Professor Adam Bridgeman, Pro Vice-Chancellor (Educational Innovation)** (Nov 2024 announcement), with the implementation FAQ authored by **Adam Bridgeman and Danny Liu** (Educational Innovation) as a living document fed by an all-staff webinar (`raw/international/u-sydney--ai-assessment-policy-two-lane.md`; `raw/international/u-sydney--two-lane-assessment-faq.md`; `analysis/deep-reading/u-sydney--ai-assessment-policy-two-lane.md`, `analysis/deep-reading/u-sydney--two-lane-assessment-faq.md`). An **AI in Education Working Group** (staff and students) co-designed the public guidance. Crucially, the policy was developed **with the national regulator at the table**: "Two academics from the University of Sydney were recently asked by TEQSA... to show the risks and opportunities of AI in assessment," and TEQSA's Nov 2024 toolkit features the two-lane approach "as a key example."

## Institutional context

Australia's regulatory environment supplies the scaffolding: TEQSA's two assessment principles, the Higher Education Standards Framework (Threshold Standards) 2021, and Group of Eight principles. Sydney's Coursework Policy (Principles 5 and 6) already required program-level integrated assessment — the two-lane model leverages an existing curricular architecture. Infrastructure: a Microsoft partnership providing **free Copilot for Web to all students**, and **Cogniti** — Sydney's own platform for "customised AI agents that can be steered with instructions and subject-specific information," with **over 1000 Cogniti agents** developed and adopted by other institutions. Named "AI University of the Year" (2024 Future Campus Awards).

## What they built and why it is pioneer-grade

One axiom, rigorously pursued: **"Any unenforceable restriction damages assessment validity."** Everything follows:

1. **The two-lane structure**: Lane 1 (secure, in-person, mainly program-level — interactive orals, vivas, contemporaneous in-class tasks, exams; "AI restricted or scaffolded as relevant") and Lane 2 (open, unit-level — "AI as a 'menu' where students can pick anything and it is our role as educators to guide them"). The **no-amber rule**: "In Open (Lane 2) assessments, there is only a 'green' light... AI use can only be restricted when we can do so securely and reliably." Traffic-light and scale systems are rejected as structurally untenable, not merely imperfect.
2. **The default flip** (S1 2025): from prohibited-unless-allowed to "allowed unless expressly prohibited," with exams/in-semester tests keeping the prohibition; "By January 2025... coordinators will not be able to prohibit its use" in open assessment — removing unenforceable discretion from individual coordinators entirely.
3. **Detection rejected on evidence AND equity grounds**: "Research has consistently shown (e.g. Scarfe et al (2024) and Fleckenstein et al (2024)) that humans cannot accurately detect good AI use"; detection is "effectively an 'arms race' in which those who can afford the latest models will remain undetected" and "likely only to 'catch' those who use the tools poorly or who cannot afford the latest AI models."
4. **A principled ban boundary**: collusion and contract cheating remain banned because they "are not graduate qualities we wish to develop" — AI use is. The distinction is educational philosophy, not enforceability pretense.
5. **Operational depth**: hurdle architecture ("a lane 1 hurdle task at the end of each year, progressing from an oral discussion to a final exam"), paired assessments (open essay + secure oral; slides/script AI-assisted + secure Q&A), grading migration ("grading only should only apply to lane 1 assessments, which may become 'hurdle' (must pass) tasks, whilst lane 2 assessments are pass/fail"), wearables foresight ("Generative AI is already being added to 'wearables' such as glasses, pins and hearing assistance aids"), remote-proctoring risk assessments, special-consideration redevelopment.
6. **The FAQ itself as an artifact**: ~50 answered staff objections — a worked, objection-by-objection implementation manual that "a US school can adapt almost directly" (run 32, §2).

## What they explicitly chose NOT to do

- No detection-based enforcement, no suspicion-driven vivas: "it is only poor use that is reliably detected" — securing the *task*, not suspect-testing the student, is the answer.
- No coordinator-level prohibition power in open assessment — discretion is removed, not guided.
- No attempt to restrict *which* AI tools students use in Lane 2 ("There is no way to reliably restrict or select which... generative AI tools students should use... except in Secure (Lane 1) environments").
- They decline to treat AI use as misconduct per se — the misconduct categories (collusion, contract cheating) are re-derived from graduate qualities, not copied from the pre-AI code.

## Borrowed vs original

Original: the two-lane construct, the validity axiom, the no-amber rule, the menu doctrine, Cogniti. Borrowed/borrowed-from: TEQSA's two principles supply the normative anchor; the FAQ notes "programmatic assessment... is associated with... medical education in particular" and suits "dentistry, medicine and veterinary science" — Sydney explicitly acknowledges importing medical education's programmatic-assessment logic into general higher education. Run 32's verdict: the primary import recommendation for US medical schools, because they already run the lane-1 toolkit (OSCEs, shelf exams, clerkship observership, Step-style secure exams) and program-level outcomes machinery (`analysis/deep-reading/32-international-synthesis.md`, §2).

## Weaknesses / what's missing

- **Integrity now rests wholly on lane-1 quality and resourcing** — exam capacity, invigilation of wearables, remote-proctoring risk; "expensive to run" (run 32, §1D). Under-resourced secure lanes produce under-validated credentials.
- Assumes regulator partnership (TEQSA); a US school lacks the equivalent national body — LCME/NBME would need to play that role.
- Not clinical-environment governance: two-lane governs assessment only; ambient scribes and clinical AI need a different instrument (run 32 recommends importing Singapore's structures for that layer).
- The FAQ candidly documents unresolved items (weighting, special-consideration matrix) — an honest but real work-in-progress state.

## What to steal concretely

1. **The validity axiom as policy preamble**: "any unenforceable restriction damages assessment validity" — one sentence that reframes every AI-integrity debate from conduct to psychometrics.
2. **Lane 1 / Lane 2 portfolio mapping** — medical schools already own the lane-1 toolkit; the work is designating which outcomes are secured where, with **hurdle tasks distributed across years** ("progressing from an oral discussion to a final exam").
3. **Paired assessments** (open product + secure oral Q&A) — the single most importable lane architecture for courses with papers or presentations.
4. **The graduate-quality test for bans**: permit what you want graduates to do; ban only what "is not a graduate quality we wish to develop."
5. **Free institutional tool provision** (Copilot floor, Cogniti-style steerable agents) as the equity complement to open assessment — you cannot require skilled AI use of students who cannot afford the tools.
6. **The living FAQ format** — publish your staff's objections and your answers; adoption is an argument, not an announcement.
