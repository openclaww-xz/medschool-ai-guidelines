## INTRODUCTION

Generative artificial intelligence (AI) has rapidly evolved from an academic concept to a tool widely tested in clinical settings.[1,2] Large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT have enabled novel applications in clinical documentation, decision support, and educational content generation, with demonstrated potential to improve workflow efficiency and clinician productivity.[3,4] However, significant questions remain regarding the reliability, safety, bias, and responsible clinical integration of these systems.[5,6,7,8,9]

In emergency medicine, a specialty characterized by high-acuity, time-sensitive decisions, high patient volumes, and substantial documentation burden, AI has been explored for its potential in data synthesis, workflow optimization, and clinical decision support.[10,11] Despite this promise, concerns persist regarding model hallucinations (i.e., the fabrication of plausible but inaccurate content), bias in training data, interpretability, and clear accountability frameworks for real-world deployment.

Survey data on clinician adoption and perceptions of generative AI are growing but remain concentrated in high-income settings. A survey of primary care physicians in the United Kingdom found that while generative AI was perceived as potentially workload-reducing, limited training remained a central concern.[12,13] A 2024 survey of UK general practitioners reported that approximately one in five had used generative AI tools in clinical practice, with a majority expecting positive productivity impacts.[13] In the United States, the American Medical Association reported that physician AI use more than doubled between 2023 and 2024, with 66% of physicians using AI in at least one professional context by 2024.[14] A national convenience-sample survey of American College of Emergency Physicians (ACEP) members found that emergency physicians were cautiously optimistic about AI, reporting adoption for noninstitutional tasks in approximately one-third of cases, with concerns about data privacy, bias, and inconsistent institutional policies reflecting themes similar to those identified in our dataset.[15] These findings highlight a global pattern of accelerating informal adoption in advance of formal governance and training, a pattern that appears particularly pronounced in lower-resource settings.

The adoption of AI in emergency medicine within Latin America reflects a complex landscape shaped by high patient volumes, limited specialist availability, substantial administrative burdens, variable digital infrastructure, fragmented regulatory environments, and uneven access to continuing professional education. In this context, generative AI tools may function as pragmatic compensatory tools, enabling clinicians to access information, synthesize evidence, and manage documentation more efficiently. However, informal adoption may be occurring ahead of institutional policies, training frameworks, or ethical oversight.

Understanding how emergency medicine professionals in Latin America are engaging with generative AI, including their use patterns, perceived value, recognized risks, and training needs, is essential for designing responsible implementation strategies aligned with both clinical realities and governance requirements. This study aims to describe current patterns of use, perceived benefits and risks, institutional readiness, and educational needs related to generative AI among emergency medicine professionals in Latin America, providing an empirical foundation for policy development and educational intervention.

## METHODS

### Study design and setting

We conducted a voluntary, anonymous, cross-sectional online survey targeting physicians and healthcare professionals practicing emergency medicine in Latin America. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) (determination date: 9 January 2025) of Universidad Nacional Pedro Henríquez Ureña (UNPHU), Santo Domingo, Dominican Republic, with a determination of minimal-to-no risk and exemption from full review. The survey was administered from April 2025 to October 2025, with data analysis conducted from October to December 2025.

### Sampling and survey dissemination

Participants were recruited through a convenience sampling strategy utilizing multiple distribution channels: (1) professional emergency medicine societies and associations in Latin America; (2) institutional academic and hospital networks; (3) professional social media platforms and specialty-specific online groups; and (4) peer-to-peer referral via participating clinicians. No incentives were offered. Participation was entirely voluntary. The survey was administered in Spanish, the primary professional language across the target region, to maximize accessibility and response validity.

The instrument included Likert-scale items (1–5), multiple-choice questions, and open-text fields.

Before full deployment, the instrument underwent pilot testing with a convenience sample of five emergency medicine clinicians not included in the final study population. Pilot testing assessed item clarity, response burden, and face validity. Minor wording adjustments were made based on pilot feedback before final deployment. No formal psychometric validation was conducted, which is acknowledged as a limitation.

### Statistical analysis

All data were exported from the online survey platform and analyzed using descriptive and inferential statistical methods. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages, calculated based on total valid responses per item. Continuous and ordinal variables (including Likert scales) are described using frequencies and proportions across response categories.

Associations between categorical variables, specifically, between country of origin and AI familiarity, frequency of use, platform preference, and institutional policy status, were assessed using Pearson Chi-square tests. Due to the distribution of responses across country categories, countries with fewer than five respondents were grouped into a residual category for these analyses. Statistical significance was set at P &lt; 0.05. Qualitative open-text responses were reviewed independently by two authors and grouped into thematic categories using an inductive approach to identify recurring patterns of use and perception. All analyses were performed using standard statistical software.

### Ethical considerations

All participants provided voluntary electronic informed consent before completing the survey. No personally identifiable information was collected. The study was conducted in full accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

## RESULTS

### Participant characteristics

A total of 123 responses were analyzed, representing healthcare professionals from 16 countries across Latin America. The largest proportions of respondents practiced in the Dominican Republic (24.8%), Mexico (approximately 19.5%), Colombia (14.2%), and Bolivia (6.2%). Most participants worked in public (42.6%) or mixed public–private (28.7%) healthcare settings. Professional experience was substantial: 61.3% reported more than 10 years of experience in emergency medicine, 26.1% reported 5–10 years, and 12.6% fewer than 5 years. Access to the Internet and digital devices for professional use was nearly universal (97.5%). Full participant characteristics are presented in Table 2.

### Generative artificial intelligence familiarity and use patterns

Most respondents reported moderate-to-high familiarity with generative AI tools [Table 3 and Figure 1]. Specifically, 76.9% reported a familiarity level of 3 or higher on a 5-point scale. More than three-quarters of respondents (78.3%) had used generative AI tools in professional practice. ChatGPT was the most commonly used platform (81.0%), followed by Gemini (31.9%), Copilot (24.1%), and Perplexity AI (11.2%). Country of origin was not significantly associated with AI familiarity (P = 0.41), frequency of use (P = 0.38), or platform preference (P = 0.52), suggesting relatively homogeneous adoption patterns across the region.

Governance of AI in healthcare appeared markedly underdeveloped in the surveyed sample. Only 7.4% of respondents reported that their institution had clear policies governing AI use, while 77.0% indicated no institutional guidance existed, and 15.6% were uncertain. ### Training interest and preferences

Interest in formal AI training was very high (88.4%), with a strong preference for virtual (69.7%) or hybrid (26.2%) formats. Importantly, 65.6% of respondents supported integration of generative AI into clinical protocols or official guidelines, indicating readiness for structured, regulated adoption once appropriate safeguards are in place.

## DISCUSSION

This study provides a regional snapshot of how emergency medicine clinicians across 16 Latin American countries are engaging with generative AI. The central finding is that rapid, largely informal adoption is occurring across diverse professional and institutional contexts, driven by perceived gains in efficiency, education, and cognitive support, but in the near-total absence of institutional governance, formal training, or standardized oversight frameworks.

The adoption rate observed in this study (78.3%) is notably high by international standards. In the United States, the ACEP national convenience-sample survey found emergency physicians reporting noninstitutional AI use in approximately one-third of cases, with attitudes generally optimistic but governance concerns similarly prevalent.[15] In the United Kingdom, a 2025 longitudinal survey of general practitioners found that approximately 25% reported using generative AI tools in clinical practice, up from 20% in 2024, with documentation and differential diagnosis generation as primary use cases, and near-universal agreement (79%) that increased training was needed.[13,16,17] The considerably higher adoption rate observed in our Latin American sample may reflect the acute information and documentation pressures facing emergency clinicians in resource-constrained settings, where generative AI may serve a compensatory function for limited access to subspecialists, current medical literature, or administrative support. Alternatively, response bias toward more digitally engaged clinicians may partly explain this difference, a limitation acknowledged below.

Consistent with findings from the United States and Europe, the most prevalent applications of generative AI in this dataset were research and literature synthesis, educational content generation, and clinical documentation, areas where LLMs demonstrably reduce time burden without requiring highly specialized medical judgment. Diagnostic reasoning support, reported by 38.3% of respondents, represents a higher-stakes application that warrants closer scrutiny and validates the concern, expressed by over half of respondents, about erosion of clinical judgment. This coexistence of enthusiasm and caution reflects a mature pragmatic understanding of the technology’s capabilities and limitations.

The near-universal absence of institutional governance (92.6% of respondents reporting no clear AI policy) is perhaps the most policy-relevant finding of this study and aligns with patterns observed globally. The ACEP survey similarly found inconsistent institutional policies as a key barrier in U.S. emergency departments.[15] In the UK, despite a more developed national regulatory environment, governance gaps in clinical AI use persist at the institutional level. In the Latin American context, this vacuum places legal, ethical, and professional responsibility entirely on individual clinicians, a particularly precarious position given the documented risks of AI misinformation and hallucination in high-acuity settings.[10]

The strong demand for formal training (88.4%) represents a clear and actionable signal for professional societies, academic institutions, and ministries of health. Training needs extend beyond basic tool literacy and should address AI model limitations, prompt engineering, bias recognition, data governance, documentation accountability, and ethical frameworks for human-in-the-loop decision-making. The strong preference for virtual (69.7%) and hybrid (26.2%) formats reflects the geographic distribution and time constraints of emergency clinicians across the region and should inform curriculum delivery strategies.

From a policy perspective, the current landscape presents a classic tension between bottom-up innovation and top-down regulation. Rather than prohibition, which risks driving use underground while depriving clinicians of potentially valuable tools, regulatory strategies should focus on enabling safe, transparent, and accountable use. A staged implementation model could guide this transition: (1) a recognition phase that formally acknowledges existing informal use and defines its scope; (2) a capacity-building phase providing structured education and competency frameworks; (3) a governance phase developing institutional and national policy; (4) a pilot implementation phase with monitored, auditable use cases; and (5) an evaluation and scaling phase with continuous assessment of safety, quality, and equity outcomes.

Latin America’s heterogeneous digital infrastructure and regulatory landscape mean that national and regional approaches will need to be context-sensitive. Regional professional bodies could play a convening role by issuing consensus guidance, minimum standards, and ethical principles tailored to emergency care. Such guidance should emphasize human-in-the-loop oversight, advisory – not prescriptive – status of AI outputs, transparent documentation of AI-assisted reasoning, and mechanisms for error reporting and learning. Equity considerations are also essential: as generative AI becomes embedded in clinical workflows, its benefits must not accrue only to well-resourced institutions. ### Comparison with international evidence

The patterns identified in this study align substantively with evidence from higher-income settings, while revealing important contextual differences. The primary use cases (documentation, research and education), the dominant platform (ChatGPT), the coexistence of enthusiasm and risk awareness, the demand for training, and the governance vacuum are consistent across the ACEP U.S. survey, the UK GP longitudinal surveys, and this Latin American dataset.[13,15,16,17] The key distinction is scale: adoption rates appear higher in our sample, governance infrastructure is more absent, and the resource constraints motivating adoption are more acute. This suggests that the transition from informal to structured AI use may need to occur more urgently, and with more contextually tailored support, in Latin America than in settings with more developed digital health infrastructure.

### Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the survey relied on self-reported data and is subject to social desirability and recall bias. Second, the convenience sampling strategy, utilizing professional networks and social media, likely overrepresents digitally engaged, urban, and institutionally affiliated clinicians, potentially inflating adoption and familiarity estimates relative to the broader population of emergency medicine professionals in Latin America. Third, the sample, while geographically diverse across 16 countries, is not statistically representative of any individual country or the region as a whole, and the small per-country sample sizes limited country-level subgroup analysis. Fourth, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and longitudinal assessment of adoption trends. Fifth, while the instrument underwent pilot testing, formal psychometric validation was not performed, and the survey was not adapted from a previously validated instrument. Finally, the study did not assess objective clinical outcomes, actual AI output quality, or patient safety metrics associated with AI use.

## CONCLUSIONS

Generative AI tools are already being widely adopted by emergency medicine professionals in Latin America, primarily for education, research, clinical documentation, and diagnostic reasoning support. Clinicians perceive substantial benefits but also recognize significant ethical, legal, and safety risks. This adoption is occurring almost entirely in the absence of institutional governance, structured training, or formal oversight, a pattern consistent with, but more pronounced than, trends observed in the United States and Europe.

These findings underscore an urgent need for regionally relevant frameworks that promote responsible, equitable, and safe integration of generative AI into emergency care. Investment in workforce education, institutional governance, and policy development, delivered through professional societies, academic institutions, and ministries of health, will be essential to realizing the benefits of generative AI while safeguarding clinical judgment, patient safety, and professional accountability. This study provides an empirical foundation for those efforts.
