# Current Landscape of Curriculum Development and Implementation in Medical Artificial Intelligence: A Scoping Review.

*Journal of multidisciplinary healthcare*, 2026-09-02. DOI: 10.2147/jmdh.s628152

## Abstract

#### Purpose

To explore the current status of medical AI educational programs and to review how these programs were developed, implemented, and improved.

#### Methods

Following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines, this study searched English articles from 2021 to April 2026 published in PubMed Central, Web of Science, Scopus, and MEDLINE databases. All curricula included in this review were implemented programs, not theoretical frameworks.

#### Results

Among the 7652 screened documents, 36 were identified. Data extraction focused on programs’ status, construction methods, and course frameworks. The number of programs has increased rapidly but remains small, with most in the pilot stage. Learners were predominantly medical students, with small class sizes. Curriculum development mainly relied on expert experience. Learning objectives mainly focused on the understanding level, but also covered advanced cognitive skills, and the content, covering AI principles, clinical applications, and ethics, demanded high cognitive engagement. Most programs were offered as modular elective courses in online or face-to-face formats, employing diverse teaching methods and activities. The learning outcomes and course evaluations mainly relied on student feedback. This review identified persistent gaps: the lack of standardized curriculum frameworks, affective learning objectives, and objective assessment tools; limited content on AI development, applications, collaboration, communication; weak integration with existing curricula; and insufficient involvement of learning designers.

#### Conclusion

Medical AI education programs are still in the initial stage. There is a need to accelerate curriculum development pathways, enhance practical skill trainings and affective competency developments, and establish effective instructional design and evaluation systems.

## Introduction

The rapid growth and widespread adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) are transforming how healthcare is delivered. As AI technologies are increasingly integrated into clinical specialties such as radiology, pathology, and dermatology,1 there is an emerging demand for AI-related competencies among healthcare professionals. Clinicians need to understand the core principles of AI in medicine and gain the knowledge and skills to critically appraise and ethically incorporate AI tools into their practice.2 However, existing research suggests that the medical industry currently faces an AI talent gap, underscoring the urgent need to cultivate professionals capable of developing, implementing, managing, and evolving AI medical technologies to fully realize AI’s potential.3 Medical AI education offers a critical pathway for building AI literacy among healthcare trainees.4 As a result, the design, development, and implementation of AI-focused educational programs have become key priorities in medical education.

In recent years, many organizations have issued policies or guidelines for medical AI education, such as the Principles for the Responsible Use of Artificial Intelligence in and for Medical Education5 proposed by the American College of Physicians, Artificial Intelligence in Health Professions Education assessment6 released by the Association for Medical Education in Europe, and the requirements and guidance for medical education in the EU Artificial Intelligence Act (2024).7 These policies or guidelines highlight the importance of medical AI education and provide broad strategic direction, but they lack specific guidance for designing and developing medical AI courses.

At the moment, healthcare organizations advocate for AI-driven medical training,8,9 and many medical schools are actively developing or refining AI curricula.10 Medical professionals, educators, and students also hold positive attitudes toward medical AI courses.11 Despite the great enthusiasm for AI medical education, the lack of a standardized framework for medical AI education,12 as well as educators’ insufficient understanding and confidence in AI,13 hinders the integration of AI content with medical education.

Miller’s Pyramid and other models provide a theoretical basis for medical education institutions to develop courses. Institutions can divide AI capabilities into different levels and integrate them more effectively into the existing curriculum system. For example, beginners need to enhance their AI literacy and understand and master AI-related conceptual knowledge; competent users need to possess AI capabilities, including the ability to apply AI systems in medical environments and improve medical treatment quality; experts need to possess greater professional skills, including the development and systematic improvement of AI systems, thereby leading medical AI innovation.14 Additionally, some studies have explored how to integrate AI content into undergraduate, graduate, and continuing medical education.15 For instance, Crotty et al16 suggested a modular approach to teaching, while Ma et al17 advocated conducting basic, practical, experimental, and ethical teaching across the preclinical, clinical, and clinical research stages. These efforts provide references for integrating AI into existing medical education. However, the heterogeneity of medical education systems in different countries18 further affects the design of medical AI courses. Differences in academic duration, governance methods, and clinical practice will all influence AI learning objectives, teaching methods, and integration approaches. For medical schools, key challenges include the contextual development of interdisciplinary AI curricula, the improvement of instructional resources, and the enhancement of AI competencies among healthcare professionals.

Although previous studies have explored medical AI education, most have focused on the theoretical construction of courses or on improving medical students’ knowledge, skills, and attitudes, while rarely addressing programs design and practical processes. Moreover, the course samples selected for research on program design and practical processes were limited. They often included theoretical frameworks that had not been implemented, thus failing to reflect the actual programs situation. Therefore, in order to comprehensively understand the current development and implementation status and problems of medical AI education programs, this study chose the scoping review method, widely including literature targeting different learning objects and different training levels,19 based on one of the most recognized frameworks for designing, implementing, and evaluating courses in the medical education field, Kern’s Six-Step Approach to Curriculum Development,20 and conducted a structured analysis of the included literature, with a focus on the key areas consistent with this framework: learning objectives, scope and organization of teaching content, teaching implementation (teaching environment, teaching strategies), learning outcome assessment method, and curriculum evaluation and feedback. This study aims to review the current status of medical AI education programs’ construction and provide corresponding support and guidance for medical schools to design and develop AI courses.

## Materials and Methods

### Study Design

This study was conducted based on the five-stage process proposed by Levac et al21 The steps in the review process included defining the research questions, identifying the relevant literature, selecting the eligible studies, extracting and charting the data, and synthesizing the results. Results were reported in accordance with the PRISMA-ScR. The research questions guiding this study primarily include: (1) how existing medical AI courses and training programs are developed, (2) which learner populations they target, (3) what learning objectives are commonly defined (4) what content is included and how curriculum contents are structured, (5) how programs are delivered—including teaching environment, teaching strategies, and learning outcome assessment methods, (6) and what approaches are used to evaluate and improve the course.

### Literature Search

The search was conducted between March and April 2026. The search strategy used terms such as “artificial intelligence”, “machine learning”, “deep learning”, “medical curriculum”, “medical competency”, and “medical literacy”. Publications were retrieved from 2021 to April 2026 in four bibliographic databases: PubMed Central, Web of Science, Scopus, and MEDLINE.

### Study Selection

All the retrieved references were first imported into Rayyan for duplicate removal. Two reviewers then screened titles and abstracts preliminarily through Rayyan. References with consistent screening results were retained, and discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer. After the initial screening, the two reviewers evaluated the full texts of the references based on the research question and finally determined which references would undergo systematic analysis.

### Eligibility Criteria

The search only included English journal articles available in the aforementioned databases. The articles must describe structured medical AI programs for medical professionals, including undergraduate, graduate, postgraduate, and continuing medical education. Pre-medical programs were excluded. The medical AI education programs must have been implemented in practice; those developed solely through theoretical methods—such as the Delphi technique, literature review, survey—without actual delivery were excluded. The articles must at least cover all or part of the learning objectives, content selection, teaching implementation, or evaluation feedback. Those without detailed descriptions were excluded.

### Quality Appraisal of Individual Studies

We did not conduct a formal quality assessment or risk-of-bias analysis for the included studies. The scoping review method aims to summarize and aggregate various types of studies, including qualitative, quantitative, or mixed-methods studies, whether published or unpublished, to explore the breadth of existing research areas and identify gaps, providing a basis for future research directions and prioritization. Usually, quality or risk of bias is not used as an exclusion criterion.22 This review focuses on the current process of developing and implementing medical AI courses rather than evaluating intervention effects. Therefore, not conducting a quality assessment is the methodological choice of this review.

### Data Extraction

For the 36 studies that met the inclusion criteria, two reviewers analyzed and coded the literature based on Kern’s Six-Step Approach to Curriculum Development and the framework proposed by Obadeji.23 They conducted a systematic analysis focusing on the learning objectives of the courses, the course content, the organization of the content, teaching strategies, the implementation methods of the courses (teaching environment, teaching strategies, and evaluation methods of learning outcomes), as well as course assessment and improvement, to ensure the reliability and accuracy of the results, and disagreements were judged by a third reviewer.

## Results

A total of 7652 articles were found. After duplicate removal using Rayyan software and manual verification, 2336 duplicate articles were removed, leaving 5316 articles eligible for title and abstract screening. A total of 5168 irrelevant articles were excluded after title and abstract review, with 148 articles retained for full-text assessment. Sixteen articles were excluded due to inaccessibility, 57 for lacking relevance to curriculum development, and another 39 for designing curricula without practical teaching implementation. Ultimately, 36 studies met all eligibility criteria for final analysis (Figure 1).

### Characteristics of Included Studies

A total of 14 studies were published in the United States;24–37 4 in Germany;38–41 2 in Canada;42,43 2 in Turkey;44,45 2 in the United Kingdom;46,47 and 1 each in Australia,48 Belgium,49 Egypt,50 Ghana,51 Iran,52 Japan,53 New Zealand,54 Romania,55 Saudi Arabia,56 the Netherlands,57 Oman,58 and Jordan.59 By publication year, 2 articles were published in 2021,31,39 2 in 2022,40,43 6 in 2023,25,26,34,42,44,49 7 in 2024,28,32,33,36,37,47,57 13 in 2025,27,29,35,38,41,46,50–52,55,56,58,59 and 6 in 2026.24,30,45,48,53,54 Reference 24 described both a beginner-level AI course and an advanced data analysis course; only the beginner program was retained for analysis. References 29 and 36 covered both introductory and advanced curricula, which were integrated into a single coding unit during data extraction. The curriculum series in Reference 30 incorporated elective modules reported in Reference 26. Reference 34 represented a revised iteration of the curriculum originally presented in Reference 31. Although Reference 41 had not been implemented at the time of publication, its launch was scheduled for January 2025, and it was included in the final analysis after team discussion.

### Course Delivery Information

23 studies explicitly documented the year of first program delivery: 2 programs launched in 2019,26,43 3 in 2021,33,36,40 7 in 2022,25,27,32,42,44,47,50 2 in 2023,28,52 6 in 2024,29,30,45,48,56,59 and 2 in 2025,41,53 1 during the 2022–2023 academic year.49 Five programs confirmed that their programs had been offered in multiple rounds.26,33,43,48,50 Twenty‑eight programs specified the number of enrollments: 17 programs had fewer than 60 participants per iteration,25,26,28,31–34,36,38,40,42,47–49,55,57,59 5 had 90–150 participants,29,39,45,51,53 and 6 enrolled more than 150 learners.27,35,43,44,50,56 Four studies specified withdrawal rates,25,27,39,40 with the lowest being 9.38%40 and the highest 73.33%.25 It should be noted that the enrollment numbers for programs offered in multiple rounds were calculated based on the total number of students.

### Curriculum Development

26 of the included studies explicitly documented their curriculum development methodologies. Seven employed a narrative literature review approach, primarily to identify performance gaps, define learning objectives, select teaching content, and establish assessment methods.24,25,28,35,50,52,56 Six studies adopted questionnaire surveys, which were used to determine learning needs and curriculum scope through input from educators, students, domain experts, and professional organizations.24,25,27,36,52,55 Thirteen programs were developed by interdisciplinary teams consisting of clinical specialists and AI experts.26,29,35,38,41–43,46,51,52,54,57,59 Twelve programs were designed solely by clinical domain experts.27,28,31,32,34,36,40,47,48,50,55,56 Among these 12, 4 were developed by clinical experts with established AI literacy;28,32,47,48 3 had their content reviewed by external AI specialists or authoritative organizations;31,34,50 and 2 were developed under the supervision of professional bodies or interdisciplinary working groups.27,36 One program was independently designed and developed by AI experts alone.24

### Target Audiences

Twenty-seven studies focused on university-based medical education.25–28,30,32,33,35,37–41,43–47,49–55,58,59 Of these, 11 targeted undergraduate medical students,39,43–47,49–51,54,59 one involved master’s-level medical trainees,47 and 8 were designed for doctoral medical students.25,26,28,30,32,33,37,39 The remaining 9 could not be clearly categorized due to vague reporting or cross-national differences in educational systems. Nine programs belonged to postgraduate medical education,25,27,31,34,36,39,42,43,57 mainly serving resident physicians. Seven fell within continuing medical education,24,27,39,41,47,48,56 targeting practicing clinicians, nursing staff, and medical researchers.

Sixteen studies specified clear disciplinary orientations: 6 in nursing,28,35,44,45,56,59 5 in radiology,36,42,46,54,57 and 2 in neurosurgery.25,47 Single studies were identified in pediatrics,48 medical imaging,51 and physical therapy.35 Six programs explicitly stated prerequisite prior knowledge for enrollment.25,26,29,31–33 Among these, References 26 and 32 required learners to complete pre-course self-study via assigned academic papers, instructional videos, or academic lectures.

### Learning Objectives

In terms of curriculum learning objectives, the focus was primarily on the knowledge and skill domains. Fifteen studies focused on the knowledge domain,27,31,33–35,38,40,41,44,46,51,53,55,57,59 while another 21 covered both knowledge and skill domains.24–26,28–30,32,36,37,39,42,43,45,47–50,52,54,56,58 20 studies explicitly formulated their learning objectives in accordance with Bloom’s taxonomy.25,26,28–35,38,39,42,43,51–54,57,58

To assess the consistency of the two coders’ evaluations of the cognitive levels of the learning objectives, we employed the Kappa consistency test. The results yielded a Kappa coefficient of 0.93 (95% CI: 0.900–0.967), indicating an extremely high level of inter-rater agreement. The results showed that only 4 of these remained at the understanding level or below;32,51,54,57 the rest targeted higher-order cognitive skills, including applying, analyzing, evaluating, or creating. ## Discussion

### Key Findings

Structured programs and formal training are a critical approach to improving AI literacy of healthcare professionals. Currently, 17 countries, led by the United States, have developed medical AI curricula that are actively being taught, with significant growth in the last 5 years. More than 70% of the studies included in this review were published during the last 3 years. However, the overall extent of medical AI curriculum development and implementation remains limited. In terms of target audiences, most programs address medical students, while AI programs specifically designed for residents and practicing physicians are less common. Nursing and radiology have taken the forefront in developing medical AI courses, with other specialties making progress on this front.

While many organizations and institutions have investigated medical AI education for medical professionals at different training levels and specialties, most programs remain in pilot, to our knowledge, with not many offered repeatedly. The enrollment number is relatively small, and some courses have high withdrawal rates, indicating that medical AI education is still in an early exploratory stage.

In terms of curriculum development methods, the current medical education AI courses mainly rely on expert knowledge and experience for construction; the learning objectives mainly focus on knowledge and skills and involve high-level cognition, but the affective learning objectives are missing; the teaching content focuses on core content, clinical applications, ethics, and law, with limited focus on AI development or application skills, communication and collaboration, and quality improvement, which are mainly organized in a modular way and carried out in the form of elective courses, and strong content arrangement and integration are still required; various teaching methods are adopted to conduct online and in-person teaching activities for groups, teams, or individuals, but they lack solid theoretical foundations, and the instructional design team is insufficiently involved, and objective assessment of learning outcomes is also absent; course evaluation and improvement rely on subjective learner feedback, with limited use of instructor input and learning analytics.

#### Program Development Methods

This review systematically analyzed the approaches used to design educational programs. The results indicated that most curricula were developed by interdisciplinary teams that include AI specialists or by teachers who have some knowledge of AI. Designs are heavily influenced by individual expertise and experience, which is due to inconsistent competency standards for medical AI education, limited established curricula, and a lack of implementation. Most institutions and instructors set learning objectives independently, without formal assessments of future workforce skill needs or gaps between intended goals and learners’ current abilities.

Literature reviews and surveys are the most common development strategies. These methods are widely adopted by other researchers. Tolentino et al12 performed a literature review to examine frameworks for medical AI education. Ki et al developed a South Korean curriculum based on survey data.60 The Association of American Medical Colleges surveyed AI courses offered in US and Canadian medical schools.61 Besides, the Delphi method is also an effective approach: Singla et al applied it to create a Canadian medical AI curriculum;62 Kern’s Six-Step Approach provides another structured path for the design and development of medical AI courses, such as Cai et al63 and Ahsan et al15, who used the framework to analyze and construct the curriculum framework. Curriculum teams may adopt these methods to design medical AI courses.

#### Learning Objectives

Learning objectives describe anticipated cognitive, psychomotor, and affective domains for learners. They serve as the primary basis for selecting course content, designing teaching strategies, and formulating evaluation methods for learning outcomes. Research results show that due to the different target audiences, the learning objectives of different courses or training also vary. However, all educational programs cover knowledge acquisition, with more than half of the courses involving skill teaching, and almost no program involves affective teaching, which indicates that the existing AI programs lack learning objectives for the emotional domain.

In terms of the way of clarifying learning objectives, most programs frame objectives using Bloom’s Taxonomy, outlining the cognitive abilities for medical students needed to achieve the expected learning outcomes. Overall, the learning objectives are concentrated mainly at the understanding level, but most curricula also cover higher-order cognitive skills such as analyzing, evaluating, or creating. Medical learners need not only to understand the fundamentals of AI, but also to build, implement, and evaluate AI tools ethically. They must also adapt to evolving team dynamics and doctor-patient communication patterns shaped by AI integration.17,64–66 Miller’s Pyramid provides an alternative description of progressive medical competencies. Curriculum teams can use the model to plot expected proficiency levels, as Cai et al67 and others did to investigate healthcare AI competencies.

#### Selection and Organization of Teaching Content

Curriculum teams select and organize curriculum content that will help learners achieve learning outcomes. Existing programs always align curriculum contents with learning outcomes and assessment strategies, covering foundational AI knowledge, clinical applications, and ethical-legal principles. Fewer programs cover basic AI development and applied skills, and even fewer address collaboration, communication, or quality improvement. Most are standalone courses; few incorporate AI material into existing courses. They are offered mainly as electives during preclinical, clinical, or postgraduate programs for less than a month. Shorter formats reveal profound medical school workloads and busy clinical schedules—mandatory or long formats risk overburdening healthcare professionals.4 Most curricula describe a modular structure, while spiral progression remains rare. Alignment across learning stages and integration with existing medical content remains to be addressed.

#### Program Implementation

During delivery, course teams must consider instructional environments, resources, and teaching strategies. Current programs often combine multiple approaches. Self-directed learning promotes individualized study through online activities. Face-to-face sessions deliver traditional lectures, explain AI concepts, and demonstrate hands-on skills. Some courses use flipped learning: students complete independent study before engaging in guided discussions with instructors. Many programs also offer both in-person and live/recorded online sessions to accommodate diverse learning needs.

Few programs explicitly base design on learning theories;26,30,50,53,54 most resorted to different methods of teaching didactic and practical design. Lectures remain the most common format of presentation. Case-based learning, discussions, hands-on activities, and project-based learning are other common themes. Such approaches promote interaction between learners, instructors, and peers, enhancing knowledge retention and skill development. Most programs require instructors to have AI experience, but instructional designers or learning science specialists are rarely involved Reference 28, 35. The use of dedicated design teams informed by learning theory has the potential to enhance the quality of online courses.

#### Learning Outcome Assessment and Course Evaluation

Learner self-report via survey dominates summative assessment. Although self-evaluations indicate improved knowledge, skills, and attitudes, limited formal and objective outcome measures have been demonstrated. Future work should develop reliable and valid assessment tools that will more accurately demonstrate learner competencies and measure objective progress.

Existing medical AI curricula rely heavily on subjective student feedback to assess overall program quality, course content, and delivery implementation. Based on such input, common revisions include expanded faculty guidance and additional practical training components. Even so, insufficient attention is paid to instructor and institutional feedback, as well as empirical analysis of learners’ actual performance. The scalability and sustained implementation of medical AI education hinge directly on its instructional effectiveness. Curriculum developers should conduct ongoing evaluation and continuous course optimization while documenting tangible educational benefits. Such evidence allows institutional administrators to assess the merit and value of a course objectively and facilitates the formal incorporation of pilot programs into a medical training system.

## Strengths and Limitations

This review adopted a systematic search strategy across major medical databases. It builds from broad literature coverage and methodologically reproducible procedures. Two independent researchers conducted study screening, data analysis, and qualitative coding to minimize potential research bias. There are several limitations to be acknowledged. The primary studies offered incomplete descriptions of course details, leaving important program descriptions unstated. As a result, qualitative coding and interpretive analysis were inevitably influenced by subjective judgment, which may result in minor discrepancies relative to the original training initiatives. The total pool of retrieved literature was small. Study methodological quality and publication quality were therefore not assessed during the screening process.

## Conclusion

The continuous shift toward AI-enabled smart healthcare has brought forward new competency requirements for medical professionals. In recent years, AI-based clinical tools have advanced considerably, such as the nested ensemble deep learning model for predicting gynecological cancer risk,68 and the multimodal framework for the early diagnosis of Parkinson’s disease.69 These developments require medical staff not only to understand and apply AI systems, but also to interpret and evaluate the results they generate critically, and to communicate AI-informed decisions to patients. Targeted AI education is urgently needed to build AI proficiency of medical students, resident physicians, and practicing clinicians. This study systematically examined the design, development, and practice of existing medical AI education programs, providing references and inspirations for future curriculum construction and implementation of medical AI programs. All courses included in this study have been implemented in practice, rather than those still at the stage of theoretical construction. They thus provide a more accurate reflection of the current status and problems of existing programs and offer more operational guidance for the construction of medical AI courses. In addition, this study conducted a more in-depth analysis of these programs based on Bloom’s taxonomy. This systematic review indicates that medical AI education programs are still in the exploration stage, mainly consisting of small-scale pilot programs, and the design, implementation, and evaluation methods still need to be improved: affective learning objectives are missing; content related to AI development, application, communication, and collaboration remains insufficient; course scheduling, organization, and integration with existing courses need strengthening; teaching design teams are not adequately involved; and the learning results and course evaluation methods need to be perfected.

To enhance the quality of the medical AI courses, we suggest that the relevant course teams give priority to the following areas. Firstly, AI applications, communication, and cooperation should be integrated as core teaching content into the course content. Medical students should not only understand the basic knowledge of AI, but also master the relevant skills for applying AI systems in clinical practice, thereby achieving intelligent healthcare. Secondly, the courses should introduce formal and verified learning outcomes and course evaluation tools, so as to more objectively measure the extent to which the medical students have achieved their learning goals and the quality of the courses, avoiding being influenced by subjective factors such as novelty effects. Finally, a longitudinal integrated AI course system should also be explored, integrating AI throughout the entire process of medical knowledge learning and clinical practice learning, so that medical students can progress from being beginners who understand AI knowledge to competent users who master the methods of using AI, and ultimately become experts leading the innovation of medical AI.

We suggest that future research be conducted in the following three aspects. First, establish a standardized capability framework. Currently, the courses mainly rely on expert experience and lack a systematic framework for guidance. It is recommended to conduct surveys of interdisciplinary teams, including institutional administrators, medical experts, and AI experts, to construct a medical AI capability standard framework that reaches consensus among interdisciplinary experts. This will help institutions design and develop localized medical AI courses. Second, explore the effectiveness of different teaching models. The current teaching models are diverse but lack comparative effectiveness evidence. It is recommended to conduct comparative studies on learning outcomes of different teaching models and use subgroup interaction tests or moderator variable analysis to examine differences between different majors or training levels, and explore effective teaching strategies in different contexts. This will provide an effective theoretical basis for course teams to design teaching environments, select teaching strategies, and design teaching activities, thereby improving learning quality. Third, introduce data-driven assessment and improvement methods. Currently, the courses mainly rely on learners’ subjective feedback and lack objective indicators. It is recommended to introduce objective structured clinical examinations and other objective evaluation methods to measure learning outcomes and report effect sizes to enhance the comparability of the results; as the evidence base accumulates, it can be used to determine the best teaching strategies through network meta-analysis, determine the optimal teaching intensity through dose–response modeling, explore the impact of course characteristics on medical students’ completion through survival analysis, and monitor the learning process and measure learning outcomes through machine learning and other methods, promoting continuous improvement of evidence-based courses; employ decision analysis modeling methods such as Markov models and agent-based modeling methods to conduct cost–benefit analysis and barrier prediction for course implementation.
