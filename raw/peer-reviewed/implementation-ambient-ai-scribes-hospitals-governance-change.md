## Introduction

### The Administrative Burden of Modern Healthcare

Healthcare professionals face a growing and unsustainable documentation burden. In the era of electronic health records (EHRs), physicians routinely spend substantial time outside clinic hours inputting and retrieving data. In a 2019 national survey in the United States (US), office-based physicians spent an average of 1.77 hours per day completing documentation outside of work hours, amounting to an estimated 125 million hours of after-hours charting annually; 58.1% considered the time spent on documentation inappropriate, and approximately 57% reported that documentation reduced the time available for patients.1 Cumulative clerical load and poorly designed EHR interfaces have been identified as significant contributors to physician burnout,2 and the digital transformation of healthcare, while enabling data access and billing compliance, has inadvertently created a documentation crisis that erodes professional satisfaction and harms patient–provider interaction.

In response, attention has turned to ambient AI scribes (also known as ambient listening documentation tools). These systems use AI and NLP to passively capture clinician–patient conversations and automatically generate clinical notes from the dialogue.3,4 Unlike traditional human scribes5 or conventional speech-to-text dictation tools, ambient AI scribes operate in the background through microphones and software, requiring no active dictation by the clinician. They leverage advanced NLP and generative AI (GenAI), including large language models (LLMs) trained on medical data, to interpret context, filter extraneous conversation, and produce coherent, structured notes in real time.3,4 Early studies, including randomized controlled trials (RCTs), suggest these tools can save documentation time and integrate directly with EHR systems such as Epic to insert AI-generated notes into the patient record for clinician review, rather than requiring manual transcription.6,7

Newer iterations are evolving from passive scribes into AI clinical copilots, that is, active assistants that go beyond transcription to synthesize a patient’s medical history for pre-charting, surface relevant imaging or laboratory trends, flag medication interactions and allergy conflicts, and detect missing guideline-recommended tests.8 In theory, this represents a shift from reducing typing time to reducing the total cognitive and administrative load of patient care. In a 2024 survey, approximately 21% of physicians reported using AI in routine practice for documentation-related tasks, including billing codes, medical charts, and visit notes,9 and numerous vendors as well as major EHR companies now compete in this space. This rapid uptake reflects both the urgency of the burnout problem and broader advances in GenAI and LLM capabilities that have improved the quality and usability of AI-generated clinical documentation.

### Purpose of This Review

This manuscript goes beyond a traditional narrative review by proposing a leadership and governance framework for the implementation of ambient AI scribes in hospital settings, with hospital-based dentistry and oral and maxillofacial surgery (OMFS) serving as an illustrative case study of specialty-specific challenges. Existing reviews of ambient AI scribes are predominantly technology-centric or confined to primary care and a small number of medical specialties;3,4,9 relatively few address the leadership, governance, patient-consent, regulatory, and long-term sustainability dimensions that determine whether implementation succeeds at the level of a hospital or health system, and fewer still examine procedural and surgical specialties where documentation is anatomically precise and acoustically demanding.8 Hospital-based dentistry and OMFS were chosen as the primary illustrative case precisely because they sit at the interface of medicine and dentistry and concentrate these harder problems: multilingual encounters, workflow complexity, tooth- and surface-level granularity, fragmented EHR ecosystems, and high-noise operative environments. Throughout, we foreground patient perspectives, consent expectations, and regulatory uncertainty as first-order implementation concerns rather than afterthoughts. This review critically examines the implementation of ambient AI scribe technology within secondary and tertiary hospital settings from a healthcare leadership and strategy perspective. We review the current evidence base, analyze the benefits and risks that healthcare leaders must navigate, and propose a leadership-focused framework for implementation covering governance, change management, and long-term sustainability. Informed by our own experience, our goal is to equip healthcare leaders with a balanced understanding of how to harness ambient AI scribes in a manner aligned with the core mission of healthcare, that is, enabling clinicians to provide safe, humane, and high-quality care. The analysis is organized using Leavitt’s Diamond framework, a foundational model of organizational change, to make explicit that technology adoption succeeds only when Structure, Technology, People, and Process/Task are addressed in concert.10 This framework was chosen as it is particularly apt for studying the impact of new technology on an organization by considering the inter-relationships between the four aforementioned components.11

## Methods

This article presents a structured, evidence-informed narrative synthesis and framework-based leadership analysis of ambient AI scribe implementation in hospital settings. We searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and Scopus from January 2020 to March 2026 using combinations of the terms “ambient artificial intelligence”, “ambient scribe”, “AI scribe”, “clinical documentation”, “large language model”, “electronic health record”, “clinician burnout”, and “clinical copilot”. Reference lists of relevant articles, professional society reports (eg, American Medical Association, American Dental Association, Australasian Institute of Digital Health), grey literature and relevant industry analyses (eg, Peterson Health Technology Institute) were hand-searched to capture implementation experience that has not yet entered the indexed literature. Given the rapid pace of development in this field, we prioritized peer-reviewed primary studies, randomized trials, and large enterprise deployments where available, and supplemented these with conceptual, ethical, legal, and policy sources to inform the governance discussion. For the dentistry case study, we additionally searched the Journal of the American Dental Association, the British Dental Journal, and standards documents from the American Dental Association (ADA) Standards Committee on Dental Informatics. Sources were selected by author consensus on the basis of relevance to hospital implementation, methodological quality, and timeliness; where competing evidence existed, both perspectives were reported. Consistent with a narrative-synthesis methodology, no formal quality-appraisal instrument was applied. The practical discussion and recommendations were also informed by the authors’ direct clinical and operational experience implementing or evaluating ambient AI tools in hospital, dental, and OMFS settings in Singapore, Canada, and the US. Leavitt’s Diamond framework was selected a priori as the organizing structure for the leadership analysis because it explicitly models the interdependence of Structure, Technology, People, and Process/Task during organizational change.10 In this review, we use the term Process/Task to emphasize both what work is performed and how that work flows operationally.

To make the basis for source selection explicit, we applied the following inclusion and exclusion criteria. We included English-language, peer-reviewed primary studies (randomized and observational), large enterprise deployments, systematic and narrative reviews, and authoritative conceptual, ethical, legal, regulatory, and policy sources published between January 2020 and March 2026 that addressed ambient AI documentation, its clinical implementation, or its governance. We excluded non-English publications, promotional or marketing materials lacking verifiable data, and records unrelated to clinical documentation. Titles and abstracts were screened against these criteria and potentially relevant full texts were assessed for eligibility. Older foundational and contextual sources identified through citation searching were retained where necessary to support the organizational, medico-legal, speech-recognition, and dental-informatics discussion. The 59 sources cited here comprise empirical clinical studies and trials, systematic and narrative reviews, conceptual and ethical analyses, medico-legal and regulatory sources, professional-society and standards documents, relevant industry reports, and grey literature. Of these, the principal sources that directly evaluate or analyze ambient AI scribes or AI-assisted clinical documentation are summarized by source type in Table 1. To limit selection and interpretive bias, each candidate source was reviewed by at least two of three authors (K.X.Z., Q.X.N., and E.Y.W.E.), and disagreements were resolved by discussion. We deliberately adopted a narrative-review and leadership-commentary format rather than a systematic or scoping review because our aim was not to estimate a pooled effect or comprehensively catalog every primary study. Rather, we sought to integrate heterogeneous empirical, operational, legal, and ethical evidence into an actionable governance framework for healthcare leaders. This broader, framework-oriented objective was better suited to an evidence-informed narrative synthesis than to a conventional systematic or scoping review. We acknowledge the corresponding limitation: in the absence of a formal quality-appraisal instrument, source selection necessarily reflects author judgment, and the synthesis should be read as an evidence-informed leadership perspective rather than an exhaustive or quantitatively pooled appraisal.

## Current Evidence Base: Efficiency, Well-Being, and Operational Experience

### Early Evidence of Benefit

The first systematic studies of ambient AI scribes are now emerging, and results are cautiously optimistic. A 2025 prospective study at an academic health system (University of Pennsylvania) evaluated an ambient scribe tool in outpatient practice, finding a 20.4% reduction in time spent writing notes per patient (from 10.3 to 8.2 minutes on average), a 9.3% increase in same-day appointment closure, and approximately 30% less time per day on after-hours EHR work (reducing “pajama time” from 50.6 to 35.4 minutes per workday).12 These objective gains were accompanied by significantly lower perceived documentation burden and greater clinician engagement with patients during visits.12

A year-long rollout at Kaiser Permanente Northern California, involving 7,260 physicians across more than 2.5 million patient encounters, further demonstrated enterprise-wide scalability.35 The greatest time savings accrued among heavy users from various specialties, who achieved more than double the time savings per note compared with occasional users. Notably, Kaiser’s approach of enabling the tool for approximately 10,000 physicians and staff on a voluntary opt-in basis, with 3,442 physicians adopting it within the first 10 weeks, offers a replicable model for large-scale implementation with self-selected early adoption.35 Complementing these North American data, a 2026 prospective observational time-motion study at a large academic medical center in Singapore similarly found that experienced users of an in-house ambient scribe achieved reduced documentation time and improved patient engagement (notably more eye contact during consultations) without altering consultation duration or total time per patient, supporting the technology’s use in multilingual Asian healthcare settings.13

These large-scale deployments typically begin with a focused pilot in primary care or high-documentation specialties, with workflows refined iteratively based on user feedback before phased expansion. Adoption is greatest when AI-generated notes integrate seamlessly into existing EHR templates without adding extra steps for clinicians.9

Take-home: Early prospective and enterprise-scale data show consistent reductions in documentation time, after-hours charting, and clinician-reported burden, but realized benefit is concentrated among heavy users and depends on phased, EHR-integrated rollout rather than the technology alone.

### Technology Overview: Scribes, Copilots, and What Differentiates Them

Ambient AI scribe systems use automatic speech recognition (ASR) to convert spoken dialogue between clinician and patient into text, while NLP layers interpret meaning and context. Current platforms incorporate LLMs trained on medical data to organize the raw transcript into a polished clinical note formatted into standard sections (History of Present Illness, Assessment and Plan, etc), with structured elements such as medication lists drawn from the EHR.9

Compared with traditional human scribes, ambient AI alternatives offer greater scalability and consistency without the logistical complexity of recruiting in-person or remote personnel. Compared with conventional speech-recognition dictation, ambient AI scribes passively capture natural conversation without requiring active dictation. Advances in ASR and LLM-based post-processing have improved transcription accuracy, although performance remains heterogeneous across systems and may deteriorate with specialized terminology, accents, background noise, and multi-speaker conversations.3,9 By drawing directly from the clinical encounter, AI-generated notes may also capture patient quotes and contextual detail that clinicians might abbreviate or skip in busy settings.

Distinguishing ambient scribes from AI clinical copilots is important because these tools differ in function, risk profile, and governance requirements. Ambient scribes primarily automate documentation; copilots incorporate EHR-derived synthesis and decision-adjacent prompts (pre-charting, guideline-based prompting, and automatic safety flagging), raising additional accountability and oversight considerations.8 The clinician burden offloaded by a copilot extends beyond keystrokes to cognitive and administrative load, enabling a more empathetic, patient-centric interaction.14 As systems evolve toward active copilots, governance frameworks must evolve in parallel, with particular attention to accountability, validation requirements, and safe-use boundaries.

Take-home: Ambient scribes and AI clinical copilots differ qualitatively in function and risk profile; healthcare leaders should evaluate, govern, and contract for them as distinct categories of technology rather than treating “ambient AI” as a single homogeneous tool.

## A Leadership and Systems Perspective: Risks and Challenges

While early evidence is promising, ambient AI scribes introduce new categories of error and operational challenges. These range from technical issues of accuracy and safety to concerns about professional practice, human–machine interaction, equity, legal liability, and economic value.8,36

### Accuracy, Hallucination, and Contextual Blindness

The most immediate concern with AI-generated clinical notes is accuracy. Even small documentation errors can carry serious clinical consequences. Traditional speech recognition errors are well-documented; earlier dictation systems have caused patient harm through inadvertent transcription mistakes, such as recording “no vascular flow” instead of “normal vascular flow” in an imaging report.9,40,41 Although some ambient AI scribes report word error rates under 3%, these tools introduce additional failure modes arising from the generation and summarization of clinical content.3,9

Chief among these is AI hallucination, that is, the insertion of plausible-sounding medical content that was never stated. An AI scribe might document a cardiac exam as normal when no exam was performed, or list a diagnosis that was not discussed, purely because the language model “filled in” expected text.9 Omissions are equally concerning: the AI may fail to capture a symptom or instruction, particularly when speakers overlap or use indirect language. Misattribution, or the assignment of a statement to the wrong person or context, is a further risk. In procedural specialties such as surgery or dentistry, laterality and numbering errors are especially grave; an AI may not reliably distinguish tooth #14 from #15, or left from right in a complex operative discussion, potentially documenting procedures on the wrong site.8 There is a paucity of evidence demonstrating that current ambient AI systems can consistently handle all specialty-specific nomenclature or ontology.

A related and under-recognized failure mode is contextual blindness. While these systems excel at synthesizing recent dialogue, they may suffer from recency bias, compressing a complex, decades-long medical history into a superficial summary that omits subtle but potentially life-saving nuance. This creates a significant validation burden: auditing a summarization algorithm is more cognitively demanding than reviewing a literal transcript, because the clinician must evaluate not only what is present but what is missing.15 If the time required for manual post-editing exceeds the time saved by the AI, the tool adds to the very cognitive load it was designed to reduce.42 Further complicating this, several studies have demonstrated that while AI tools reduce documentation time and manual input, they are associated with increases in note length, including progress notes specifically, and in total documentation volume.6,12 This “note bloat” may inadvertently add to clinicians’ overall cognitive burden.

Hallucinations and omissions have also been documented in real-world pilots and can be exacerbated by challenging acoustic conditions, eg, high background noise, simultaneous speakers, or strong accents.16 Such gaps are especially concerning when clinicians divide attention between patient interaction and monitoring AI performance; a missing detail discovered hours later may be impossible to reconstruct accurately, introducing medico-legal risk.25 While errors of commission (ie, incorrect or fabricated content) may be more readily identified because they are overtly inconsistent, errors of omission are inherently more difficult to detect, requiring clinicians to recall absent details from prior encounters.15 As systems evolve toward active copilots, the potential impact of these errors expands, making robust safety checks and human-in-the-loop review indispensable. Healthcare leaders must treat the AI scribe as a tool requiring consistent human oversight, not an infallible expert.

Take-home: Hallucinations, omissions, and contextual blindness create failure modes that may be difficult for clinicians to detect reliably during routine review; safe deployment therefore requires structured human-in-the-loop verification, prospective error surveillance, and an institutional posture that the AI-generated output is a draft, not a final clinical record.

### Human–Machine Interaction: Editorial Oversight, Automation Bias, and Interface Design

The “clinician as editor” framing captures the efficiency promise of ambient scribes but also signals a new competency requirement.25 Clinicians transitioning from documentation authors to AI editors must develop AI literacy: the ability to review drafts critically, identify subtle inaccuracies, and resist automation bias, that is, the tendency to trust an automated system uncritically.43 In this context, a busy physician may accept an AI-generated note even when something seems slightly off, leading to propagation of errors. The opposite, that is, automation resentment, occurs when the AI produces notes that require substantial post-editing, generating frustration and potential abandonment of the tool. Some physicians in early pilots reported that editing AI output added work rather than reducing it.23

Over time, the shift from author to editor introduces a more subtle professional risk: the potential erosion of documentation skills and clinical reasoning, particularly among trainees who may have fewer opportunities to construct notes from first principles. The process of writing itself supports clinical reasoning and reflective practice, enabling clinicians to organize information, synthesize findings, and articulate diagnostic uncertainty.44 AI-generated documentation, while efficient, may produce records that prioritize completeness over clinical narrative, with reduced visibility of the clinician’s reasoning and decision-making process. As such, training programs should continue to emphasize independent note composition, and institutions should ensure that clinical assessment and medical decision-making are actively verified and expressed in the clinician’s own words. For example, the University of Saskatchewan’s undergraduate dental curriculum sequences AI integration developmentally: students first establish independent competency in year three before AI tools are introduced in year four, where the emphasis shifts to evaluating efficiency.

If the AI scribe interface adds alerts or prompts (for example, flagging unaddressed symptoms or suggesting tests), it may recreate the alert fatigue that clinicians already experience from conventional EHRs. As systems evolve toward active assistance, a liability paradox emerges: if an AI flags a drug interaction or suggests screening for a condition and the clinician does not act, could that constitute a missed finding?30 Conversely, overexposure to AI prompts may breed automation complacency, causing clinicians to reflexively dismiss alerts, potentially masking high-risk signals.45 There is unfortunately a dearth of medico-legal frameworks and a lack of legal certainty, and leaders should state clearly that the AI scribe is not a clinical decision-maker, and any evolution toward combined documentation and decision support must be accompanied by explicit governance policies on how AI-generated suggestions should be handled.26,30

Take-home: The clinician’s role shifts from author to editor, but only deliberate AI-literacy training, calibrated alert design, protected opportunities for trainees to compose notes independently, and explicit policies on how AI-generated prompts are handled can prevent the twin risks of automation bias and automation resentment.

### Performance Heterogeneity, Specialty Gaps, and Algorithmic Inequity

Ambient AI scribes do exhibit performance variation across specialties, languages, and care settings, creating both operational and equity risks.8,27 Early deployments have been concentrated in primary care, internal medicine, and medical specialties, which involve relatively standard conversational exchanges. In contrast, procedural and surgical specialties involve workflows and documentation styles that AI may struggle to handle.17 In an oral surgery clinic, a surgeon’s brief conversation interspersed with long silences or staff-directed speech may confuse speaker identification.8 Similarly, in mental health settings, there is a risk that ambient AI systems may misinterpret elements of the clinical interview, as patients often express symptoms in nuanced, culturally shaped, and context-dependent ways that require expert clinical interpretation.28

A further, under-recognized source of heterogeneity is the baseline duration of clinical consultations. In many Asian healthcare systems, hospital outpatient clinics operate on a high-throughput model in which a single clinician may see upwards of 40 patients during a 3–4-hour session, with each individual encounter lasting only a few minutes. In a recent prospective time-motion study at a large academic medical center in Singapore, the absolute documentation-time savings attributable to ambient scribe use were proportionally smaller for shorter consultations, and the marginal efficiency benefit may not justify the implementation overhead in such settings.13 Healthcare leaders should therefore expect that the magnitude of efficiency gains will vary not only by specialty, language, and acoustic environment, but also by baseline consultation duration and clinic operating model; pilot site selection should account for this expected gradient when projecting ROI.

Accordingly, healthcare leaders should demand specialty-specific piloting and validation before system-wide rollout. Environmental and acoustic conditions compound these limitations. As noted above, AI transcription accuracy may degrade significantly in noisy settings such as emergency departments (EDs) or multi-bed wards, where background noise and multiple simultaneous speakers impair capture quality.3,16 In such environments, the tool can shift from a time-saver to a liability generator, forcing clinicians to function as unwilling editors of flawed documentation. ROI is therefore both geographically and acoustically dependent; a one-size-fits-all hardware strategy will fail in many subspecialty environments.

Most commercially available ambient AI scribes have also been optimized on training data dominated by standard American English, making language and accent diversity a significant and under-recognized equity challenge.18,19 Clinicians or patients who speak with regional accents or dialects (such as Appalachian English or African American Vernacular English), or who code-switch between languages during clinical encounters may experience higher transcription and interpretation error rates. These errors translate into less accurate or incomplete documentation, imposing a “diversity tax” on both patients and clinicians who must spend additional time correcting flawed records.

The aforementioned challenges set up a self-reinforcing cycle of inequity (Figure 1). When AI systems are trained predominantly on data from English-speaking majority populations, under-represented communities become data deserts.19 Poor performance discourages adoption, resulting in fewer high-quality AI-generated notes from minority populations, further limiting the representative training data needed to improve model performance. Healthcare leaders must recognize algorithmic inequity as a systems issue rather than a technical flaw. Mitigation strategies include requiring vendors to demonstrate multilingual and accent robustness, routinely monitoring error rates stratified by speaker demographics, and expanding training datasets to better reflect linguistic diversity. Staged or specialty-specific deployment, beginning with settings where language is more standardized and workflows are predictable, may also be appropriate, while parallel investments are made to adapt the technology for more complex environments. Hardware and acoustic improvements (higher-quality microphones, noise cancellation) can also help ameliorate performance gaps in noisy settings.31 Finally, prospective validation studies of AI scribes must include adequate linguistic diversity to support external validity and equitable performance.

Take-home: Performance is not uniformly distributed across specialties, languages, accents, or acoustic environments. Equitable deployment requires stratified pre-deployment validation, ongoing demographic monitoring of error rates, and the willingness to delay rollout where infrastructure or evidence is inadequate.

### Hospital-Based Dentistry and Oral Surgery: A Case Study in Specialty-Specific Complexity

Dentistry represents one of the largest clinical specialties absent from the ambient AI scribe evidence base.8 With over 200,000 practicing dentists in the US alone46,47 and hundreds of millions of dental encounters globally, it constitutes a substantial segment of healthcare delivery, yet, at the time of writing, no rigorous evaluation of the performance of ambient AI scribes in dental or oral surgical settings exists. This omission is particularly notable given that hospital-based oral surgery or OMFS departments operate at the intersection of medicine and dentistry, managing patients with complex craniofacial trauma, head and neck oncology, obstructive sleep apnea, and congenital anomalies whose documentation spans both medical and dental domains. The challenges these environments pose for ambient AI systems are illustrative of the broader difficulties faced by procedural and surgical specialties and merit specific examination.

The most consequential challenge is the domain-specific precision that dental documentation demands. Three competing tooth identification systems coexist internationally: the Universal Numbering System (teeth numbered 1–32), the FDI/ISO 3950 two-digit notation, and the Palmer notation.48,49 These systems are non-interoperable: tooth “14” in the Universal system (upper left first molar) refers to an entirely different tooth from “14” in the FDI/ISO system (upper right first premolar), and it does not exist within the Palmer system. Within a single hospital, oral surgery, orthodontics, pediatric dentistry, and general dentistry departments may each default to different conventions. An AI scribe transcribing a surgeon’s verbal instruction to “extract the upper right four” must disambiguate the intended numbering system from context alone, a task that has proven error-prone even for human providers. The MedPro Group has documented malpractice cases arising from precisely this form of numbering system miscommunication, resulting in wrong-tooth extractions.50,51 For AI-generated documentation, this risk is amplified. The system may not flag its own interpretive uncertainty, and downstream clinicians reading the note may have no reason to question what appears to be an unambiguous record.

Apart from tooth identification, dental documentation routinely specifies individual tooth surfaces (mesial, distal, buccal, lingual, occlusal) and multi-surface restorations, for example, “MOD composite on #14”. This degree of anatomical granularity represents a domain where hallucination or omission can directly precipitate wrong-site procedures. The clinical equivalent in general surgery—a wrong-site or wrong-side error—is recognized as a “never event” that has prompted extensive safety protocols including surgical site marking and procedural time-outs. In dentistry, the analogous risk is a wrong-tooth procedure, and an audit of 1,128 dental patient records in the United Kingdom found that 44% of dental charts already contained inaccuracies, including tooth count errors, restorations documented on the wrong tooth, or restorations entirely missing from the chart.52 AI systems trained predominantly on general medical corpora, which lack exposure to dental charting conventions, may inherit and potentially compound these baseline documentation vulnerabilities. Pre-populated templates and copy-and-paste practices can also introduce or perpetuate inaccuracies when required edits are omitted.53

Hospital-based OMFS departments also present additional challenges related to mixed clinical workflows and acoustic complexity. It is not uncommon for a single oral surgeon to conduct a conventional ambulatory consultation resembling a primary care visit, followed immediately by a minor surgical procedure under local anesthesia with the patient unable to speak clearly, and later dictate a multi-phase treatment plan. Current ambient AI scribes are typically optimized for a single encounter archetype and cannot dynamically adapt note structure, terminology expectations, or template selection across this operational spectrum.8 The acoustic environment further complicates capture as dental encounters frequently involve simultaneous speech from the surgeon, resident, assistant, and patient amid high-speed handpiece, suction, and ultrasonic noise, and the physical obstruction of the oral cavity by specialized instruments compounds the speaker-diarization challenges already noted for ambient AI in multi-speaker settings.3,8

A further structural barrier to ambient AI deployment in hospital-based dentistry is the fragmented EHR landscape. Unlike medicine, where a small number of enterprise EHR platforms dominate (eg, Epic, Oracle Health), dental practices and hospital dental departments operate on a diverse ecosystem of specialty practice management systems, including Dentrix, Eaglesoft, Open Dental, and axiUm in academic settings, that lack support for the Health Level Seven (HL7) and Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR) standards now common in medical EHRs.54,55 Hospital-based dental departments frequently operate on dual systems, utilizing the institution’s medical EHR for inpatient records and cross-service referrals while maintaining a separate dental practice management system for clinic-specific charting and billing. Ambient AI scribes designed for seamless Epic or Oracle Health integration may therefore lack the interoperability pathways required to write into these dental-specific systems, creating an implementation barrier that is invisible to healthcare leaders evaluating these tools from a general medical perspective.

Recognizing these gaps, the ambient AI documentation market has begun to differentiate between general-purpose medical platforms and emerging specialty-specific tools designed explicitly for dental and surgical workflows.8 This trajectory mirrors the broader pattern observed in EHR development, where general-purpose platforms eventually required specialty-specific modules to accommodate the documentation conventions of fields such as ophthalmology, dermatology, and dentistry. Healthcare leaders evaluating ambient AI for hospital dental departments should not extrapolate performance data from primary care deployments; these data do not account for the unique nomenclature, anatomical specificity, or workflow complexity that define dental documentation. The ADA’s Standards Committee on Dental Informatics has recognized the growing role of AI in dental practice but has not yet addressed ambient documentation systems specifically.32 Prospective, multi-institutional validation of ambient AI scribe accuracy, safety, and usability in surgical and dental specialties is urgently needed. Until such specialty-specific evidence exists, deployment in these settings should proceed with greater caution, more intensive quality monitoring, and a lower threshold for identifying and reporting documentation errors than would be considered sufficient in primary care contexts.

Take-home: Hospital-based dentistry and OMFS exemplify why ambient AI deployment in procedural specialties cannot rely on extrapolation from primary care evidence; specialty-specific nomenclature, anatomical precision, acoustic complexity, and EHR fragmentation demand dedicated validation, specialty-specific integration pathways, and heightened safety surveillance before scaled adoption.

### Clinician Variability in Adoption

Admittedly, not every provider will want or use an AI scribe even when one is available.20 Leadership faces the challenge of how aggressively to drive adoption. Mandating use risks breeding resentment, while a purely optional approach may yield low uptake among hesitant clinicians. Skewed and uneven adoption is itself a structural problem; an expensive technology yields limited system-wide benefit if a large proportion of clinicians remain non-users.37 Fragmented adoption also potentially creates data silos, with certain specialties generating high-quality AI-documented datasets while others remain in traditional formats, limiting the availability of high-integrity data for analytics and future AI development.

One effective strategy is to identify clinical champions—physicians who are enthusiastic about technology and can act as peer mentors and demonstrate effective use cases to hesitant colleagues. Trusted peers with positive firsthand experiences can increase uptake more effectively than a top-down mandate.56

Take-home: Sustainable adoption depends on peer-led champions and aligned incentives rather than a mandate; leaders should monitor adoption gradients explicitly, since uneven uptake creates hidden data silos that themselves erode the long-term value of the technology.

### Ethical, Legal, and Economic Considerations

#### Vendor Data Stewardship, Consent, and the Regulatory Grey Zone

As health systems move from passive scribes to active AI copilots, governance gaps are increasingly recognized. These tools rely on third-party vendors that process sensitive patient data, raising questions about data stewardship, consent, and whether health systems retain adequate oversight once information leaves the enterprise firewall.26 As noted above, liability remains unresolved. If a copilot flags a drug interaction or red-flag symptom, the clinician does not act on the alert, and an adverse outcome subsequently occurs, it is currently unclear whether responsibility lies with the clinician, the deploying institution, or the vendor.30 The regulatory landscape has not kept pace; unlike traditional medical devices, AI copilots operate in a grey zone with evolving standards for auditability, explainability, and clinical validation.

#### Liability Allocation, Contracting, and Consent Stewardship

While it may be instinctive for healthcare leaders to seek vendor indemnification for errors resulting from algorithmic hallucinations or failures to meet stated accuracy benchmarks, this approach is neither practical nor prudent. Publicly available terms from major ambient AI vendors do not presently provide indemnification for clinical documentation errors and generally disclaim or substantially limit vendor liability for clinical outputs, while requiring human review of AI-generated documentation. More fundamentally, indemnification conflates two distinct legal frameworks: product liability, under which manufacturers are accountable for defective products, and professional liability, under which clinicians bear responsibility for clinical judgment. Ambient AI scribes do not assume a standard of care; the clinician who signs the note does. Attempting to transfer that obligation contractually to a software vendor introduces a legally untested allocation of responsibility. It may also create a moral hazard: if vendors assume liability for documentation errors, clinicians’ incentive to critically review AI-generated output is diminished, directly undermining the “clinician as editor” model upon which the safe use of these systems depends.25,29,30 Rather than pursuing blanket indemnification, healthcare leaders should negotiate contracts that require transparency, including mandatory disclosure of known error patterns, accuracy benchmarks stratified by specialty and language, defined incident-response timelines, and the right to independent audit. The objective is not to shift blame but to ensure that accountability is clearly allocated and that each party (clinician, institution, and vendor) bears responsibility commensurate with its actual role in the documentation chain. On the data governance side, long-term policy must prevent “consent decay”; institutions should establish technically auditable boundaries on secondary data use, ensuring that clinical encounter data remain under institutional stewardship and are not applied to commercial model training without explicit patient authorization.33

#### Direct Malpractice Exposure and Insurance Gaps

Beyond governance-level liability, healthcare leaders must recognize the direct malpractice exposure that documentation errors create. Traditional medical malpractice claims have centered on clinical decision-making; ambient AI scribes introduce a legally untested category in which the record itself—not the clinical judgment it documents—may be the proximate cause of harm.9,40 An AI-generated omission of a drug allergy, a misattributed laterality, or a hallucinated finding that a downstream clinician acts upon may constitute a failure to meet the documentation standard of care, with institutional and individual liability potentially shared across clinician, organization, and vendor. Current insurance frameworks have not adapted to this tripartite exposure. As this is a matter of concern to clinicians,30,33,34 leaders should proactively engage risk management and legal counsel before deployment to clarify how errors originating from AI outputs are viewed in existing case law, determine whether existing malpractice coverage extends to AI-assisted documentation, and ensure that incident reporting systems are configured to capture and categorize events in which AI forms part of the causal chain.

#### Financial Return on Investment and the Strategic Value Proposition

While ambient AI scribes may reduce documentation time, their financial ROI remains uncertain. Licensing costs can be substantial, often amounting to several thousand US dollars per clinician annually, depending on the vendor, contract structure, deployment scale, and usage volume, and early analyses have not consistently shown reductions in staffing costs or increases in attributable revenue. A multicenter study at Atrium Health comparing 112 primary care clinicians using an ambient AI scribe with 103 controls found no significant improvement in overall productivity.21 The Peterson Health Technology Institute’s 2025 report similarly concluded that while AI scribes likely reduce clinician stress, there was no clear evidence of financial improvement or efficiency gains in terms of more patients seen or faster workflows.38 Health systems typically justify adoption through non-financial value propositions such as improved clinician well-being, reduced after-hours documentation, workforce retention, and strategic digital positioning, rather than immediate cost savings.38

Leaders must therefore make an explicit, prospective determination about whether this investment is warranted as a strategic workforce intervention, analogous to other systemic commitments to clinician retention and organizational resilience. Absent that clarity, institutions risk implicitly expecting productivity gains that directly undermine the burnout-reduction outcomes the technology is intended to deliver. Articulating the primary value proposition, whether efficiency, workforce sustainability, or future AI capability, is also essential to set coherent expectations, guide implementation design, and preserve clinician trust.

Take-home: Ethical, legal, and economic risks cannot be assumed to be fully transferable to vendors through indemnification clauses; they must be governed through transparent contracting, deliberate consent stewardship, malpractice-aware risk planning, and an explicit articulation of the strategic value proposition that justifies the investment.

### Structure: Governance, Accountability, and Institutional Oversight

Senior leadership must actively champion ambient AI scribe implementation, articulating a vision that moves beyond burnout reduction toward a fundamental shift in clinical practice: restoring human-centric care through cognitive load reduction. Ambient AI should be framed as a tool to eliminate administrative friction, not simply as a documentation shortcut or productivity accelerator.

When leaders such as the Chief Medical Officer (CMO) or Chief Information Officer (CIO) vocally support the initiative, it signals an organizational priority rather than a technology experiment. Leadership should form a multidisciplinary steering committee early, including clinical leaders from multiple specialties; representatives from IT, informatics, compliance, legal, and nursing; and patient representatives where feasible. Physician champions, who are ideally well-regarded departmental leaders with an interest in informatics, are critical to peer credibility. Leadership must commit resources: technology budget, protected time for training, and clear communication that this is a sustained organizational investment. As Dr Brian Hoberman (CIO of The Permanente Federation) noted, it is important to consider ethical implications and ensure AI is “purpose-built to do the job that we need to do and that we understand what the risks are” before full deployment.39

Critically, leadership must maintain an “eyes-open” culture that proactively addresses the liability paradox: explicitly communicating that while AI is the copilot, the clinician remains responsible for reviewing, correcting, and approving the final clinical note. Long-term sustainability also requires leadership commitment that documentation time recovered through automation is channeled toward clinician well-being and more purposeful patient interaction (eg, greater eye contact, more attentive listening, and time for the social and rapport-building dimensions of the encounter), rather than being automatically absorbed into higher patient volumes. Some pressure to recoup the substantial implementation costs of these tools is realistic and should be acknowledged openly; however, increased throughput should not be the default assumption nor the primary justification for deployment, and any adjustments to scheduling templates should be discussed transparently with front-line clinicians.

A dedicated governance structure, potentially a subcommittee of the broader steering group, should hold explicit responsibility for quality and safety oversight. Core functions include defining appropriate and inappropriate encounter types for AI scribe use; standardizing patient consent processes across clinics; ensuring full compliance with privacy and data-protection regulations; and establishing a shared risk framework that clarifies the attribution of responsibility among clinician, institution, and vendor.

A continuous quality-assurance (QA) loop is essential. During the initial 3–6 months, a multidisciplinary team should periodically audit samples of AI-generated notes against clinical records, using a standardized note quality rubric that specifically screens for contextual blindness, where the AI omits vital long-term medical history in favor of superficial summaries. Findings should then be fed back to vendors for technical refinement and to clinicians to guide editing practices. Internal dashboards tracking documentation time, after-hours charting, and AI scribe utilization by department allow early identification of outliers who may require additional support.

An instructive governance parallel exists in established credentialing and privileging frameworks. Before granting surgical privileges, hospitals require demonstrated competency, peer review of outcomes, and ongoing performance monitoring. A similar logic should apply to ambient AI scribes, particularly as they evolve into clinical copilots that influence documentation, prompting, and decision adjacency. Leaders should consider whether clinicians using these tools require baseline AI literacy training before deployment, whether initial use should be supervised or limited to lower-complexity encounters, and how competency in critical review of AI output will be assessed. This framing, that is, AI tool use as a clinical competency requiring governance, not merely a software subscription, shifts institutional responsibility appropriately and creates a mechanism for managing scope creep as capabilities evolve. At the same time, leaders should be mindful that overly restrictive prerequisites for adoption can themselves become impediments to uptake, particularly among clinicians with competing demands on their time. Competency requirements should therefore be calibrated to actual risk, with lighter-touch requirements for low-stakes documentation use and more rigorous requirements for evolving copilot functions, and should be rolled out in a way that supports, rather than impedes, clinician adoption.

A formal, non-punitive error-reporting mechanism should be established, similar to existing EHR-related safety reporting processes, enabling categorization of error types (transcription error, hallucination, omission) to prioritize remediation. Service-level agreements with vendors should include commitments on accuracy, uptime, cybersecurity controls, and responsiveness to safety concerns.

Beyond contractual oversight, healthcare leaders should recognize that the most productive vendor relationships are collaborative rather than purely transactional. Institutions that engage vendors as co-development partners and contribute clinical expertise, specialty-specific workflow knowledge, and structured feedback on failure modes are better positioned to shape products that reflect the realities of their practice environments. This is particularly relevant for procedural and surgical specialties where vendor training data may be sparse; institutional clinicians possess domain knowledge that no external development team can replicate independently, and the exchange of that expertise accelerates product maturation in ways that benefit both parties and, ultimately, patients.

Take-home: Governance must combine senior sponsorship, multidisciplinary oversight, credentialing-style accountability, and a continuous QA loop so that AI scribe use is treated as a managed clinical competency and a co-development partnership, not a software subscription.

### Technology: Platform Selection, Infrastructure, and Vendor Stewardship

From an infrastructure standpoint, IT environments must support the technology: sufficient wireless bandwidth for audio streaming, microphone hardware in clinic spaces, and seamless EHR integration (ideally single sign-on with in-workflow note delivery). Special attention is needed for fast-paced and high-noise settings such as EDs and surgical clinics, and downtime protocols should be established so clinicians can revert to typing or dictation without disruption.

When procuring ambient scribes, contracts should require vendors to provide pre-deployment validation data demonstrating accuracy stratified by specialty, language, accent group, and clinical complexity, not merely aggregate error rates, which can mask significant performance disparities in the populations that matter most. Performance benchmarks, such as maximum acceptable error rates per note type and minimum accuracy thresholds for interpreter-mediated encounters, should be codified in service-level agreements with defined remediation timelines. Organizations should also negotiate for periodic independent equity audits assessing whether documented benefits are distributed equitably across the patient populations they serve. Data portability and exit provisions should be included to prevent vendor lock-in as the market consolidates, preserving institutional flexibility to migrate to better-performing platforms as the technology matures.

Vendor relationships should be structured as ongoing quality-improvement partnerships, with contractual provisions for regular performance review, transparency around error patterns, and responsiveness to safety concerns. As platforms evolve, introducing model updates, expanded language support, and new functionalities that blur the boundary between documentation assistance and clinical decision support, healthcare organizations should establish formal mechanisms through governance committees to evaluate, approve, and safely integrate new features prior to deployment.

Take-home: Procurement should privilege transparency, equity-stratified validation data, and durable vendor partnerships over point-in-time performance, recognizing that ambient AI platforms will continue to evolve after the contract is signed and that downtime, exit, and decision-support governance must be planned for in advance.

### People: Clinician Engagement, Training, and Cultural Readiness

Before large-scale deployment, leaders should assess readiness across culture, infrastructure, and workforce dimensions. If clinician skepticism or fear exists—about surveillance, job replacement, or data privacy—it must be proactively addressed through town halls, demonstrations, and early clinician involvement. Significant inter-user heterogeneity has been noted in prior studies,20,22 and identifying early-adopter physician champions who can advocate among peers may help alleviate initial apprehension and support future iterations and broader adoption within the organization.23

Introducing ambient AI scribes represents a substantive workflow change and should be guided by established change management principles. The framing matters: leaders must consistently position these tools as mechanisms to reduce administrative burden, restore professional identity, and improve the patient experience by enabling more attentive, less screen-mediated consultations, not as surveillance instruments or precursors to increased productivity demands. Meaningful staff engagement should begin early, with front-line clinicians involved in pilot feedback sessions, surveys, and governance representation. Crucially, feedback must be visibly acted upon; when clinicians report that AI-generated notes are excessively verbose or poorly structured, leadership should work with vendors to refine outputs and communicate anticipated improvements, closing the feedback loop and signaling that clinician input is valued.

Training should be treated as an ongoing process rather than a one-time event. Many usability questions emerge only after real-world use.23 Resources such as concise tip sheets, peer-to-peer forums, and refresher sessions help normalize the learning curve. During the initial weeks of deployment, dedicated “elbow support”, eg, on-site personnel, typically super-users, informatics staff, or vendor representatives, available in the clinic to provide just-in-time troubleshooting, can ameliorate early frustration, accelerate proficient use, and prevent the small workflow snags that otherwise compound into abandonment of the tool. Clinicians should be taught practical voice habits that improve AI performance (for example, explicitly verbalizing key findings and plans, since silent reasoning will not be captured) and efficient techniques for reviewing and editing AI drafts.

Early technology deployments should also be carefully orchestrated, as visible failures erode clinician trust and generate organizational resistance that can be difficult to reverse. A principle of strategic containment, deploying only where success is probable, protects the program’s long-term legitimacy. Establishing departmental super-users to support local adoption and create a community of practice enhances uptake, troubleshooting capacity, and system resilience as deployment scales.

Non-financial incentives can support engagement throughout: recognizing early adopters, providing appropriate hardware, and acknowledging clinician contributions to innovation. Equally important is explicit reassurance that efficiency gains are intended to reduce after-hours work and cognitive burden, not to automatically increase patient volumes. Clinicians may reasonably fear that working faster will simply yield higher workload expectations,24 negating the well-being benefit. Any future changes to productivity expectations should be discussed transparently and collaboratively to preserve trust.

Experience across early-adopter organizations has revealed three recurring change management failure modes that leaders should anticipate. The first is selective non-use: clinicians who agree to use the tool but quietly disable or circumvent it for complex, sensitive, or emotionally charged encounters without institutional awareness. Appropriate non-use in unsuitable encounters is not itself an implementation failure; the concern arises when such non-use is unmonitored or falls outside agreed criteria. This can create a hidden data gap in which the cases most relevant to safety evaluation are systematically absent from quality monitoring. The second is productivity co-optation: institutional expectations creep upward following documented time savings, with scheduling templates expanded or throughput targets revised, effectively transferring the efficiency dividend from clinician well-being to organizational output. This predictably erodes trust, reduces adoption, and undermines the burnout-reduction rationale that justified investment. The third is feedback atrophy: governance structures established at launch are not sustained, QA audit cycles lapse, and the program loses its self-correcting mechanism precisely as the technology scales and risk accumulates. Each failure mode is preventable through deliberate design, eg, aggregated, privacy-preserving monitoring of use patterns, explicit contractual commitments that recovered time belongs to clinicians, and governance charters with mandated review cycles, but only if leaders anticipate them before deployment rather than after they manifest.

Take-home: Sustainable adoption is a sociotechnical achievement, requiring cultural readiness, ongoing training, peer-led champions, and explicit protections that the time recovered through automation is reinvested in clinician well-being rather than absorbed into productivity targets.

### Process/Task: Workflows, Metrics, and Sustainable Operations

Scalability inherently demands an incremental, specialty-sensitive approach. Orthopedic surgeons may need custom musculoskeletal terminology, pediatric services may require well-child documentation templates, and inpatient environments may require speaker identification and contextual segmentation to handle multi-speaker ward rounds. In diverse communities, multilingual support and dialect robustness must be prioritized as a deliberate equity measure.

Leadership should define from the outset what success looks like, using multi-dimensional key performance indicators (KPIs) that capture both the promises and perils of the technology, with some examples listed below:

#### Efficiency Metrics

Average documentation time per encounter (from EHR logs), same-day note completion rates, and after-hours charting per provider per week. A locally adapted target might be a ≥20% reduction in documentation time or ≥50% reduction in after-hours charting for active users.

#### Clinician Well-Being

Standardized burnout scores, job satisfaction, and retention rates among users versus baseline, with a goal of measurable improvement in provider satisfaction with documentation within 6–12 months.

#### Patient Experience

Patient experience could be assessed using a setting-appropriate validated measure, such as the Consumer Assessment of Healthcare Providers and Systems Clinician and Group Survey (CG-CAHPS) for ambulatory care,57 including relevant items on provider concern and time spent with the patient, together with direct patient feedback on comfort with the ambient recording process.

#### Note Quality and Safety

Note completeness and accuracy, addendum/error-correction rates, incidence of patient harm, near-miss events attributable to documentation error, and periodic QA audit scores.

#### Adoption and Utilization

Percentage of eligible clinicians actively using the scribe, with a target of 50% active usage in deployed departments by 6 months. Stalling below this threshold warrants investigation.

#### Financial Metrics

Changes in billing accuracy, transcription cost savings, and any changes in patient throughput should be monitored carefully to ensure productivity pressure does not offset well-being gains.

Leadership should share selected metrics transparently with stakeholders; for example, communicating the number of hours of charting collectively saved in the previous quarter can reinforce the tool’s value. Alternatively, if metrics such as error rates are concerning, these must be addressed openly and improvements communicated.

Implementation represents only the initial phase of ambient AI scribe adoption; long-term value depends on sustained oversight, continuous evaluation, and iterative improvement. Healthcare leaders should plan for ongoing assessment beyond the initial post-deployment period, when novelty effects may temporarily inflate perceived benefits. KPIs should be reviewed at regular intervals to determine whether early gains are maintained. Any deterioration in outcomes, whether through reversion to previous documentation habits, expanded documentation requirements, or increased productivity expectations, warrants systematic investigation and policy recalibration.

Financial evaluation must extend beyond license fees to include the total cost of ownership: hardware, ongoing IT and governance support, change-management costs, and the opportunity cost of clinician time spent reviewing and editing AI-generated notes. Leaders should assess whether broad-based deployment of relatively simple ambient scribes across all specialties, or deeply customized products for selected high-complexity specialties, better serves their organization, recognizing that both financial ROI and adoption rates will vary considerably by specialty and setting.

Finally, ambient AI scribe initiatives should be explicitly aligned with broader organizational strategies, eg, value-based care, digital health transformation, and workforce sustainability. Where quality reporting or care-gap documentation is central to contractual arrangements, AI-generated notes should be configured to support these requirements without increasing clinician burden. As virtual and hybrid care models expand, leaders should consider how ambient AI scribes can be integrated into virtual, hybrid, and in-person clinical workflows. Ensuring alignment with organizational priorities is essential for securing sustained leadership support, justifying continued investment, and embedding the technology as a durable component of the healthcare system rather than a transient innovation.

Take-home: Long-term value requires multi-dimensional metrics, ongoing surveillance beyond the novelty period, total-cost-of-ownership accounting, and explicit alignment of ambient AI initiatives with broader value-based-care, telehealth, and workforce-sustainability strategies.

## Conclusion

Ambient AI scribes represent one of the most promising recent innovations for addressing the documentation burden that threatens clinician well-being, professional identity, and the quality of patient–clinician interactions. Early evidence suggests meaningful gains in documentation efficiency and clinician experience, yet these benefits are neither automatic nor uniformly distributed. For healthcare leaders, the central challenge is not simply whether ambient AI scribes can reduce documentation burden, but whether they can be implemented in a manner that preserves trust, supports clinical judgment, and aligns with organizational values. The greatest risks arise when these tools are deployed as isolated technical solutions rather than as parts of a broader, well-governed transformation of clinical work.

Translating this perspective into practice, our analysis using Leavitt’s Diamond points to a concrete and, in our view, minimally sufficient set of priorities for responsible adoption. Under Structure, institutions need executive-sponsored, multidisciplinary governance with clearly allocated accountability for AI-influenced documentation. Under Technology, they need pre-deployment validation stratified by specialty, language, and accent, together with contractual accuracy benchmarks, independent-audit rights, and data-portability and exit clauses. Under People, they need appropriate AI-literacy training, peer champions, protected note-composition opportunities for trainees, and explicit commitments that recovered time is reinvested in clinician well-being and patient interaction rather than throughput. Under Process/Task, they need phased, specialty-sensitive rollout governed by multi-dimensional key performance indicators that are sustained beyond the novelty period. Because these four domains are interdependent, partial implementation that strengthens one while neglecting the others is the most predictable route to failure.

Several research priorities follow directly from the gaps identified in this review. Prospective and ideally controlled implementation studies are needed to establish whether early efficiency gains are durable; longitudinal evaluation should track clinician well-being, retention, patient experience, documentation quality and safety, and cost-effectiveness rather than time savings alone; and dedicated validation of ambient AI scribe accuracy and safety is urgently required in procedural and surgical specialties, including hospital-based dentistry and OMFS, where the evidence base remains sparse. More broadly, as AI increasingly reshapes not only clinical documentation but the conduct of medical research, education, and evidence synthesis itself, the governance habits emphasized here, namely transparency, human verification, and explicit accountability, will become equally relevant to how the resulting evidence is generated and reviewed.58,59 Ambient AI scribes represent a genuine opportunity to return time and attention to patient care; whether that opportunity is realized will depend less on the sophistication of the underlying models than on the quality, foresight, and integrity of the leadership that governs their adoption.
