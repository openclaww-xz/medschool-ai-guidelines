## INTRODUCTION

Artificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly transforming health care delivery by enabling advanced clinical decision support, automating documentation, improving operational efficiency, and enhancing patient monitoring.1,2 This momentum has been reinforced by recent federal policy signals, including the White House's March 2026 National AI Legislative Framework,3 which emphasizes innovation, competitiveness, workforce development, public benefit, and appropriate guardrails, and the US Department of Health and Human Services' AI Strategy,4 which calls for stronger governance, risk management, and public trust in AI deployment.2,3 In response, health systems are increasingly adopting AI-enabled tools to improve operational efficiency, support workflow redesign, reduce documentation burden, and respond to a growing demands for quality and value-based care. AI technologies are also being explored as a means to augment the health care workforce and support clinicians in managing increasing care complexity.5 Although these technologies offer significant opportunities to improve patient outcomes and operational performance, their rapid adoption has outpaced the development of organizational structures needed to ensure safe, ethical, and sustainable implementation.6

Global and national organizations have emphasized that responsible AI deployment requires more than technical validation. The World Health Organization (WHO) identifies ethical governance, human oversight, transparency, equity, and accountability as core requirements to ensure AI serves the public good and protects patient rights.7 Large multimodal AI models have broad potential across clinical care, education, administration, and research, and they also pose major risks to health systems, law, and society, requiring strong ethics-based governance across design, provision, deployment, liability, and international oversight.8 Similarly, the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework calls for lifecycle-based risk management to ensure AI systems are trustworthy, reliable, and aligned with organizational values.9 Federal health policy initiatives further emphasize documentation of risk analysis, mitigation strategies, and governance processes for AI-enabled health technologies.10

Nursing leaders are uniquely positioned to guide organizational AI adoption.11 Nurses serve as primary end users of many AI applications and are responsible for coordinating care, managing clinical workflows, and maintaining continuous patient surveillance.12 However, despite their central role in care delivery, nurse executives often lack practical frameworks for evaluating AI technologies, assessing organizational risk, and determining how AI should be implemented and monitored across health care settings. To address this challenge, it is important to distinguish between system-level and tool-level governance. In this article, system-level AI governance refers to organizational decision rights, oversight structures, risk management processes, and lifecycle monitoring mechanisms that determine whether and how AI is adopted across care settings. Tool-level governance refers to the model- and workflow-specific evaluation activities (eg, validation, usability testing, and local performance verification) that occur within those system-level decision processes. This framework focuses on the system-level structures that ensure tool-level evaluations are performed consistently, transparently, and with nursing leadership exercising decisional responsibility.

National guidance from the Joint Commission and the Coalition for Health AI (CHAI) emphasizes that responsible AI use requires formal governance structures, risk management processes, bias assessment, workforce education, and ongoing performance monitoring.13 Yet translating these principles into operational decision making remains challenging for clinical leaders.14 To address this gap, this article presents a practical, nursing-centered AI governance framework developed through conceptual synthesis of widely recognized trustworthy AI frameworks (eg, WHO, NIST, CHAI) combined with nursing professional governance principles and lifecycle-based risk management concepts. Each trust domain was translated into nursing-relevant governance questions, operational decision processes, and monitoring expectations across the AI lifecycle (intake, evaluation, deployment, continuous monitoring, and retirement). The framework is intended to be adaptable to organizational maturity and AI use context and prioritizes governance decision and evidence requirements that nurse executives can routinely operationalize.

### Trustworthy AI principles and nursing governance

Across major frameworks, including WHO, NIST, and CHAI, there is strong consensus around the core characteristics of trustworthy AI in health care.15 These include clinical usefulness, safety and reliability, fairness and inclusiveness, transparency and explainability, privacy and security, and organizational accountability. Together, these domains establish the foundation for responsible AI use in health care systems.

Nursing professional governance is grounded in the professional obligation of nurses to hold ownership for practice and the resulting outcomes. Through structures, nurses exercise this ownership and demonstrate accountability for practice, competency, value, and knowledge.16 Contemporary practice environments look to this decisional clinical governance function to engage clinical (direct care) nurses in assuring decisions and actions represent the standards of the profession and affect the outcomes of the health care system.

For nursing leaders, the principles of AI and professional governance translate into governance design and operational decision-making processes. The central governance question becomes whether an AI technology improves care delivery and supports the workforce while meeting defined standards for safety, equity, transparency, and accountability. Because new technologies inevitably introduce new forms of risk, the goal of governance is not risk elimination but structured risk identification, mitigation, and continuous oversight through the eyes and minds of the nurses and other clinicians that use them.

Effective governance, therefore, requires an interdisciplinary approach that integrates clinical, operational, ethical, and workforce perspectives. Rather than limiting oversight to a single approval point during procurement or implementation, governance should function as an ongoing process with continuous evaluation throughout the entire AI lifecycle. Kim et al17 proposes a people-process-technology-operations framework for establishing AI governance in health care organizations, highlighting that governance must be embedded in operational structures rather than treated solely as a technical or compliance function. This approach allows organizations to monitor real-world performance, detect unintended consequences, and adjust implementation strategies as clinical environments evolve.

The NIST Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework reinforces this lifecycle perspective through 4 core functions—govern, map, measure, and manage—designed to help organizations systematically identify risks, monitor performance, and continuously improve AI systems over time. This lifecycle-based governance model aligns closely with clinical quality improvement approaches already familiar to nursing leadership, making it particularly well suited for integration into health care operational structures.18

### Core principles for nursing AI governance

Effective governance requires translating ethical principles into operational decision processes. Responsible AI frameworks consistently identify 5 domains that should guide implementation: clinical usefulness, safety and reliability, fairness and equity, transparency and accountability, and privacy and security. These principles are interdependent and must be evaluated continuously across the technology lifecycle rather than at a single approval point.18

For nursing leaders, these domains translate into a central question: does AI technology improve care delivery while protecting patients and supporting the workforce? Governance structures must, therefore, integrate clinical, operational, ethical, and workforce perspectives.

### A nursing-led system-level governance framework

AI policy and governance structures provide the organizational foundation for determining whether AI tools offer genuine clinical usefulness in health care delivery. Rather than focusing solely on technical capability, governance should ensure that AI applications are adopted only when they demonstrate meaningful value in improving patient care, supporting clinician decision making, enhancing workflow efficiency, or reducing administrative burden. Formal governance mechanisms allow health care organizations to assess clinical relevance, validate performance within local care environments, engage clinical users, and monitor real-world outcomes over time. For nursing leaders, this is particularly important because the usefulness of AI depends on its fit with care coordination, documentation practices, patient surveillance, and interdisciplinary workflow. Responsible AI governance in nursing must account for the interrelated domains of clinical usefulness, safety and reliability, fairness and equity, transparency and accountability, and privacy and security. These domains define whether an AI tool is not only useful but also trustworthy, ethically sound, and appropriate for integration into clinical care.

### Lifecycle governance workflow checklist

A practical nursing-led system-level governance workflow checklist includes the following:

- Intake and risk tiering
- Clinical usefulness and local workflow validation
- Safety/reliability evaluation and human oversight design
- Fairness/equity assessment across relevant subgroups
- Transparency/accountability and documentation readiness
- Privacy/security review and procurement controls
- Go/no-go deployment approval with workflow readiness
- Postdeployment monitoring, incident reporting, and continuous reassessment
- Iteration or retirement/decommissioning when criteria are not met.

For each step in the governance workflow, organizations need to identify responsible stakeholders, required evidence, approval criteria, and monitoring metrics. Defining these governance requirements in advance promotes consistency, transparency, and accountability throughout the AI lifecycle.

### Clinical usefulness

Clinical usefulness is the foundational requirement for any AI-enabled tool in nursing practice. An AI system must demonstrate meaningful improvement in patient outcomes, workflow efficiency, or decision quality compared with existing processes. Evidence suggests that AI applications can support early deterioration detection, risk stratification, documentation automation, and workload optimization, particularly in high-acuity settings.1,12 However, usefulness must be evaluated within the specific clinical environment in which the tool will operate by the clinicians that use the applications. An algorithm that performs well in one health system or patient population may not generalize to another.

For nurse leaders, assessing usefulness requires structured evaluation of clinical validity, integration with nursing workflows, and impact on cognitive burden. AI tools that disrupt established communication patterns or require duplicative documentation may undermine efficiency gains. Recent consensus guidance emphasizes that usefulness must be demonstrated prospectively and monitored continuously after implementation.19,20 Governance processes should, therefore, include clinical nurse engagement, pilot testing, clearly defined performance metrics, and ongoing review of real-world outcomes. This can be achieved through structured representation (eg, shared membership or joint committee participation) between AI governance bodies and the organization's established professional nursing governance structure. Companion membership helps ensure that clinical standards, accountability pathways, and frontline feedback are built into approvals, monitoring review, and postdeployment optimization.

### Safety and reliability

Safety and reliability are central to patient protection and professional accountability. AI systems used in clinical decision support must demonstrate technical robustness, predictable performance, and resilience under real-world conditions. The World Health Organization (WHO) underscores the need for human oversight, validation, and mechanisms to detect and mitigate unintended harm.7 Similarly, lifecycle-based risk management approaches emphasize continuous monitoring rather than one-time approval.9

For nursing leadership, safety governance includes validation against local data, monitoring for performance drift, clear escalation pathways when AI recommendations conflict with clinical judgment, and safeguards against automation bias.21 Studies examining AI-based decision support in critical care highlight the importance of reliability in high-risk environments where false positives or missed alerts can directly affect patient outcomes.22 Governance structures should incorporate incident reporting, algorithm performance dashboards, and interdisciplinary oversight committees to ensure reliability is maintained as patient populations and care practices evolve.

### Fairness and equity

Fairness and equity require that AI systems do not exacerbate existing disparities in health outcomes. Algorithms trained on incomplete or nonrepresentative datasets may produce biased recommendations that disproportionately affect vulnerable populations.23 The WHO and multiple expert consensus panels emphasize equity as a core pillar of trustworthy AI in health care.7,24

Nursing leaders, given their advocacy role and proximity to diverse patient populations, are uniquely positioned to identify inequities in care delivery. Governance mechanisms should, therefore, require bias assessment before deployment and ongoing evaluation across demographic variables such as race, ethnicity, language, socioeconomic status, disability, and geography. Transparent reporting of performance across subgroups supports accountability and corrective action when disparities are detected. Ethical data governance frameworks further stress that equity considerations must be embedded in system design, procurement decisions, and vendor contracts, not treated as an afterthought.10

### Transparency and accountability

Transparency supports trust among clinicians and patients. AI systems should provide explainable outputs that allow nurses to understand the rationale behind recommendations, particularly when influencing high-stakes decisions. National guidance emphasizes documentation of model development, validation methods, known limitations, and governance processes to ensure accountability.13,25

For nursing governance, transparency includes clear communication about when AI is being used, how recommendations are generated, and what role human clinicians retain in final decision making. Explainability tools, audit trails, and accessible documentation reduce uncertainty and strengthen professional confidence. Accountability structures should define roles and responsibilities for monitoring system performance, approving updates, and responding to adverse events. Without defined ownership, AI-related risks may diffuse across departments, weakening oversight. Effective governance assigns executive-level responsibility while ensuring frontline clinical feedback informs ongoing refinement.

### Privacy and security

AI-enabled health care systems depend on large volumes of sensitive patient data. Protecting confidentiality, ensuring secure data storage, and maintaining compliance with regulatory standards are non-negotiable elements of responsible implementation. Ethical governance frameworks emphasize secure data engineering practices, access controls, and transparent data use policies.7,10

For nursing leaders, privacy governance intersects directly with patient trust. Patients must understand how their data are used, particularly when AI models rely on secondary data analysis or external vendor partnerships. Security risk assessments, vendor due diligence, and cybersecurity safeguards should be integrated into AI procurement and lifecycle management processes. Ongoing surveillance for breaches, clear data stewardship policies, and workforce education further strengthen organizational resilience. By embedding privacy and security protections into governance structures, nurse executives help ensure that technological innovation does not compromise patient rights or institutional integrity.

### Operational implementation for nurse executives

Nursing-led AI governance strengthens performance in several ways. Structured oversight reduces implementation failure and regulatory risk while ensuring that technology advances patient safety, enables nursing practice aligned with professional expertise, and optimizes operational performance. Lifecycle monitoring supports continuous quality improvement and ensures that AI technologies deliver clinical and operational value. From a workforce perspective, effective governance helps ensure that AI tools reduce administrative burden rather than introducing additional complexity, supporting workforce sustainability and professional satisfaction.26

From a workforce perspective, effective governance ensures that AI addresses administrative burdens rather than adding complexity, supporting retention and professional satisfaction. Before go-live and during updates, health systems should assess the type and intensity of education required for each AI-enabled application and develop interprofessional training plans that combine didactic instruction, skills validation, orientation content, and ongoing competency review. At the organizational level, empowering nursing leadership to actively lead digital strategy elevates the voice of nurses in interdisciplinary decision making and ensures that technology investments are closely aligned with clinical needs and operational realities.

Successful adoption and sustainability of AI-enabled technologies depend on structured change management within system-level governance, with nurse leaders advancing trustworthy and responsible AI through stakeholder engagement, alignment with organizational priorities, pilot testing, frontline champion support, and continuous evaluation of safety, fairness, transparency, and workflow integration. More broadly, strong governance is essential for trust. Trustworthy AI requires visible human oversight, transparency, and accountability and core elements of professional nursing practice.27

### Why nursing leadership must drive AI governance

AI implementation is not simply a technological decision; it is an operational and clinical transformation with implications for nursing practice and clinical nurse competency. Even validated tools can introduce risk when deployed within local workflows. Poor integration may create alert fatigue, duplicate work, or delay clinical action. Misalignment between algorithm output and nursing workflow has been identified as a major contributor to implementation failure and safety concerns. Because nurses manage care coordination, documentation, and real-time clinical decision making, they are best positioned to evaluate whether a tool improves or disrupts practice and professional governance structures are operational constructs already in existence to support both accountabilities for design, implementation, and performance.

The AI implementation approach depends on formal structures that elevate the voice of direct care nurses. Clinical nurses can participate in vendor evaluation and selection, strategic planning, interprofessional workflow design, testing, validation, and postimplementation optimization. When direct care nurses and unit-based clinical educators are intentionally involved in evaluation and rollout, they often become subject matter experts and super-users who provide at-the-elbow support, gather feedback from peers, and accelerate adoption while preserving clinical relevance and usability.

Human oversight remains essential in AI-enabled care. Overreliance on algorithmic recommendations, commonly referred to as automation bias, may reduce critical thinking and increase the likelihood of error. This risk is heightened with emerging agentic AI systems that operate with greater autonomy or act on behalf of clinicians within digital environments. Responsible AI frameworks, therefore, emphasize that AI should augment, rather than replace clinical judgment, with clear guardrails defining human review, escalation pathways, and accountability. This expectation aligns closely with nursing practice, which prioritizes situational awareness, patient context, and holistic assessment.

Public trust considerations further reinforce the importance of nursing leadership. Recent national survey data indicate that patients are significantly more likely to trust AI-supported care when results are reviewed and interpreted by a clinician, including nurses.25 Trust is closely associated with perceptions of oversight, accountability, and transparency. As the most consistently present health care professionals at the bedside, nurses play a critical role in maintaining that trust.

The American Academy of Nursing advocates for ethical AI integration to enhance, not replace, clinical judgment. By leveraging AI as a transformative tool rather than a substitute, nursing expertise remains central to patient-centered, compassionate care.28 From a policy perspective, governance mechanisms should ensure AI remains aligned with person-centered, compassionate, and high-quality care rather than operating without adequate professional oversight.

Given the rapid pace of technological change, the statement supports an adaptive governance approach in which policies are regularly reassessed to maintain ethical integrity while enabling responsible innovation. Bias may arise from data limitations, algorithm design, or differential access to care resources. Fairness, therefore, must be evaluated not only through technical performance metrics but also through differences in outcomes, resource allocation, and access across patient populations.18

### Operationalizing nursing-led AI governance

Given the central role of nurses in care delivery, workflow management, patient advocacy, and quality oversight, nursing leadership is uniquely positioned to guide AI governance across the technology lifecycle. Yet effective governance requires more than leadership commitment alone. To translate governance principles into practice, health care organizations must establish formal governance structures and operational processes that support the consistent evaluation, implementation, monitoring, and oversight of AI technologies throughout their lifecycle.

A practical implementation approach begins with the formation of an interdisciplinary AI governance committee that includes nurse executives, nursing informatics leaders, frontline nurses, physicians, quality and safety personnel, information technology specialists, data scientists, and compliance representatives. This committee should oversee AI strategy, establish governance policies, review proposed AI applications, and monitor postdeployment performance. Through these activities, the committee provides ongoing oversight across the AI lifecycle and helps ensure accountability, transparency, and alignment with organizational priorities.

Organizations should also implement a standardized AI intake and review process. Proposed AI technologies should undergo risk tiring, assessment of clinical usefulness, workflow analysis, bias evaluation, privacy and security review, and validation of evidence supporting their intended use. Higher-risk applications may require more extensive pilot testing and governance review before deployment.

Before organization-wide implementation, AI tools should be tested in limited clinical environments to evaluate usability, workflow integration, user acceptance, and safety outcomes. Pilot testing allows organizations to identify unintended consequences and refine implementation strategies before broader adoption.

After deployment, governance committees should conduct ongoing monitoring using predefined performance indicators, including adoption rates, clinical outcomes, workflow impact, safety events, fairness metrics, and user feedback. Formal reassessment processes should be established to guide model updates, retraining, replacement, or retirement when performance standards are no longer met.

## CONCLUSION

Artificial intelligence offers substantial potential to improve health care delivery; however, realizing these benefits depends on strong, structured AI governance aligned with nursing professional governance networks. Effective governance must prioritize patient safety, equity, workforce impact, data stewardship, and the preservation of patient trust. International and national guidance consistently underscores that AI implementation requires lifecycle-based risk management, meaningful human oversight, transparency, and clear organizational accountability.

The system-level framework presented here centers explicitly on AI governance within nursing leadership in health care organizations. It operationalizes core governance domains: clinical usefulness; safety and reliability; fairness and equity; transparency and accountability; and privacy and security, within formal decision making, oversight, and monitoring structures. By embedding these principles into multidisciplinary governance processes, aligning with professional governance purpose and function, nurse executives can ensure that AI technologies are evaluated, implemented, and continuously reassessed in alignment with clinical standards, professional nursing practice, and ethical obligations. Nursing-led AI governance strengthens clinical judgment, supports the workforce, mitigates unintended harm, and sustains safe, patient-centered care across the technology lifecycle.
