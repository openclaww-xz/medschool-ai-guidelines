What is generative AI

Generative artificial intelligence (AI) is a technology for conversational information searches that “can create original content—such as text, images, video, audio or software code—in response to a user’s prompt or request” (IBM, 2024) and which “learns to generate more objects that look like the data it was trained on.” (Zewe, 2023) In everyday usage, GenAI typically refers to chatbot interfaces trained on large bodies of data and algorithmic models, which can create natural language responses and images. Examples include Copilot, ChatGPT, Gemini, Llama, Claude, Midjourney, Dall-E, and others. “GPT” stands for Generative Pre-trained Transformer, a reference to an algorithm developed in the late 2010s using neural network type processing to increase the accuracy and speed of generated texts and images.

Do many students use it?

Both students and instructors increasingly use AI as part of teaching or learning. A study from 2024 showed that 30 percent of students reported using generative AI and more than fifty percent reported positive attitudes toward AI. There is confusion, however, about which professors allow AI to be used in projects, and where the boundary lies between appropriate collaboration and cheating. For these reasons, students may either avoid using AI entirely or use it inappropriately.

What should I communicate to students about AI use?

The university does not have a blanket AI policy. AI use is governed by the
Data Classification Policy
, the
Academic, Scholarly, and Professional Integrity and Misconduct Policy
, and the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act of 1974 (
FERPA
).

Within that framework, instructors are responsible for setting and communicating their own course- and assignment-level expectations for generative AI use. There is no single approach appropriate for every course, discipline, or assignment. Generative AI tools themselves range from simple autofill in emails, through paraphrasing tools like Grammarly, to sophisticated generative chatbots like ChatGPT, so what counts as “using AI” is not always obvious to students.

We recommend your syllabus statement address:

what uses of generative AI are permitted and what uses are not, including whether that differs by assignment;

whether any use is required, and for what purpose;

what AI tools students are required to purchase or access, including any cost or account requirements;

what disclosure or acknowledgment of AI use you expect;

what will happen if a student uses AI outside your stated expectations; and

how these expectations connect to what students are meant to learn.

You may also want to tell students whether and how you use generative AI yourself, in designing the course, developing materials, giving feedback, assessing work, or communicating with them. Being clear about this, one way or the other, helps build trust and avoids misunderstandings.

How can I build student AI literacy and fluencies in my class?

Including AI or addressing it with your students will help prepare them for jobs or further work in most disciplines, in addition to building important technological literacy more broadly. AI use varies by discipline, profession, or enterprise. Please reach out to CETL for 1:1 consultations on integrating AI into activities and assignments in alignment with your course objectives.

The foundations of AI literacy are:

Knowing when to use AI and when not to

Knowing how to ask and refine questions with an AI chatbot (“prompting”)

Critically evaluating AI output

Conceptual understanding of generative AI models and algorithms

Identification of a wide range of risks in AI and AI enterprises

What are some AI tools I can use in my teaching?

UConn has a contract with Microsoft for basic access to Copilot. It is free to faculty, staff, and students.  Enhanced subscriptions can also be purchased by departments. In both cases, the contract affords privacy protections and includes an agreement that queries will not be used to train large language models. Due to these protections, it is recommended, though not required, that you use Copilot. Even so, all university employees should make sure to avoid using any software, including AI platforms, for anything to do with protected university data. This includes FERPA-protected student information.

This industry changes constantly, with new tools being added, enhanced, or removed.

The other most prominent chatbots include ChatGPT (owned by OpenAI), Llama (owned by Meta), Claude (owned by Anthropic), and Google Gemini (owned by Alphabet).  Each has its own algorithms and configuration of data sets on which the model is trained. For image generation, Midjourney and DALL-E (and SORA for video) currently dominate. With a paid ChatGPT account, you can access custom GPTs (models) for, e.g., data analysis, diagnostics, etc., or create your own “GPT” through detailed instructions. Copilot does not feature custom GPT creation, but there are ways to instruct it that come close, especially in Copilot Plus (paid). If you are interested in doing this, you may find this
Custom GPT Guide
helpful.

When deciding on a tool and working with it, follow these practices:

Understand FERPA and follow

Transparently communicate to students your definition of plagiarism and cheating

Communicate to students the risks of AI

Require students to demonstrate comprehension of what is “identifiable information” so they can avoid entering it into an AI tool

Should I use detection software?

Currently detection software is of limited use and may be detrimental because it generates false negatives and, more importantly, false positives. At best, an AI detector would only ever provide a
likelihood
of AI-generated text, not a definitive determination, and studies have noted significant deficiencies with their use. As such, UConn does not currently license any AI detection software, and similar functionality is not available in SafeAssign. Still, this technology continues to develop and CETL continues to review and evaluate them. For example, methods for protecting artists’ work and copyright involve detecting or in some cases distorting or blocking a file to prevent it from training image generation models like DALL E and Midjourney. Similarly, scholars are attentive to the need for transparency in what publishing companies do with their articles and books, e.g., selling them to AI companies. While this is more a copyright than detection issue, it points to a node of issues related to fair labor and academic integrity. The United States Copyright Office published
guidance on copyright
in January 2025.

Using an AI chatbot can feel natural and seamless. Require students to slow down, engage as active readers, and reflect on their engagement with these tools. You could ask students to use this
Template and rubric for students to track their use of AI
(Bradner, APA) and perhaps assign points to doing so. Make sure you communicate to students that this work supports the course learning objectives.

Another model is the “
AI marble layer cake
” approach (Cardamone), which borrows from the flipped classroom philosophy. Essentially this approach incorporates AI into both homework and in-class work, but the emphasis in class is on analyzing and discussing the AI-generated output students got when they did the homework.

The marble layer cake metaphor joins the “
AI sandwich
” idea (Ippolito). The very simple idea behind this approach is that students use AI as a brainstorming partner first, then spend time with other students in class, engaging in “human-to-human knowledge creation,” and finally use AI again as a writing partner.

What does generative AI do to assessment of learning?

While safeguarding the integrity of assessment practices is critical, it is equally important to recognize that cheating and plagiarism often stem from reasons other than lack of ethics. For example, students may not know what acceptable collaboration looks like. Acceptable collaboration may differ from instructor to instructor, or tool to tool. As well, students may be overwhelmed by the course material or other priorities in their lives such as jobs or family duties. There is a lot we can do, pedagogically, to scaffold assignments, make content accessible, and communicate the value and purpose of using generative AI critically and judiciously.

UConn’s University Senate, and the Office of the Provost, maintain guidelines for academic integrity and scholastic standards. The

offers information on handling plagiarism and cheating.

Zachari Swiecki, Hassan Khosravi, Guanliang Chen, Roberto Martinez-Maldonado, Jason M. Lodge, Sandra Milligan, Neil Selwyn, Dragan Gašević, Assessment in the age of artificial intelligence,

Computers and Education: Artificial Intelligence, Volume 3, 2022, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.caeai.2022.100075.

The goal of this article is to describe the opportunities and threats of AI-assisted assessment. The authors critique traditional assessment practices and examine whether AI can address some of these drawbacks. Drawbacks include: the burden of designing assessments; the one-size-fits-all application to student knowledge, skills, and backgrounds; the question of what assessments actually assess and whether they are assessing what
should
be assessed; and the problem that assessments might “be tailored to the culture of schooling rather than the cultures schooling is designed to prepare students to enter.” For a range of assessments from exams to creative projects, the article provides concrete examples of both AI’s affordances and limitations (and risks).

Additional Resources

AI in Education Resource Directory
This google doc is maintained by the POD Network, a professional society of educational developers in higher education. It crowdsources and curates links to teaching resources and related AI tools. There is a section in continuous development about field-specific resources.

Cornell’s AI in Education and Pedagogy Report
Assembled by Cornell administration, this committee’s report is succinct and comprehensive – and is one of the few resources analyzing how AI might appear in different fields and disciplines.

AI+Education = Simplified
Lance Eaton’s Substack newsletter and blog. Lance Eaton updates this blog with resources and reflections regularly.

Agile Learning
Derek Bruff’s blog. Derek is an educational development professional and consultant.

Exploring AI Pedagogy: a community collection of teaching reflections
The Modern Language Association and the Conference on College Composition and Communication (MLA-CCCC) joint task force on AI and writing collected about 60 teacher reflections on AI activities and assignments they’ve tried.

Zotero AI lists
Zotero allows groups to be created on a topic. In this link, members of a group on AI in teaching upload their presentations, papers, and assignments. The list is organized into sub-lists such as student engagement, reading, detection, disciplinary uses, etc.

IBM Prompt Engineering Course.
Hosted on the edX platform, this free short course provides an overview and examples of key terms in the practice of effective prompting, like few shot, prompt refinement, chain-of thought prompting, etc. You can also check Coursera for MOOCs (Massive Online Open Courses) on AI topics.

Humanities in the age of AI,
Anastasia Salter’s course syllabus, offers a reading list and assignments for anyone interested in this topic.

October 21, 12:00–1:00 AI Squared: Academic Integrity and Artificial Intelligence
Join Assistant Director of Community Standards, Brian J. Goepfrich, and Graduate Student, Lillian Schaefer-Calderon, for an engaging and informative session on the evolving role of Generative AI, especially Large Language Model tools, in academic settings. This presentation will cover key topics to help faculty better understand and navigate these emerging technologies in the classroom while upholding academic integrity.

Key topics include:

Understanding Generative Artificial Intelligence (GAI) and Large Language Models (LLM): Learn the basics of these tools, their potential uses, and the challenges they pose in academic environments.

Fostering Positive Use: Explore strategies for incorporating Generative AI tools constructively to enhance learning while maintaining academic integrity.

Setting Clear Expectations: Discover best practices for establishing course guidelines regarding the use of AI tools to promote transparency and fairness.

Addressing Misuse: Understand how to handle Generative AI or Large Language Models misuse in violation of Academic, Scholarly, and Professional Integrity and Misconduct Policy.

Student Perspective: Gain insight into how students view and use these technologies, presented from a graduate student’s viewpoint.

University Statistics: Review relevant university data on the impact and prevalence of AI tool usage in cases referred for academic misconduct.
