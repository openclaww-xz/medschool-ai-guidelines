UW Medicine AI Policy
Sep 11, 2026
|
DIO Blog

The real danger is not that computers will begin to think like humans, but that humans will begin to think like computers.

Sydney J. Harris

The much-anticipated UW Medicine Artificial Intelligence (AI) Policy has arrived!

The integration of Artificial Intelligence, including Generative AI (GenAI), presents tremendous opportunities to accelerate UW Medicine's strategic goals and advance its mission to improve the health of the public. At the same time, AI's advantages can be seductive. Significant risks remain though, including patient harm due to inaccurate AI-generated information, HIPAA and data privacy violations, and unintended impacts on learning and professional development.

As educators, many residency and fellowship program directors have approached AI with healthy skepticism. There are still a lot of unknowns, and leaders across graduate medical education have been carefully weighing the benefits and risks of this rapidly evolving technology. As with any major technological advance, educators are asking an essential question: How do we harness the benefits of AI without undermining the learning process itself?

Similar concerns emerged when calculators became widely available in the 1970s. The same occurred when personal computers entered classrooms and workplaces a decade later. While those innovations transformed education and practice, they pale in comparison to the potential impact of generative AI. AI is not simply changing the way we work. It is changing the way we access information, solve problems, and perhaps most importantly, when we decide to engage our own brains rather than deferring to a digital bot.

Many traditional educational practices are being challenged. Reading, writing, reflecting, and working through difficult problems can feel less necessary when AI can generate answers in seconds. Yet educators know that durable learning occurs through the struggle itself. The effort required to work through uncertainty, make mistakes, and reflect on experiences helps create the neural pathways that support lasting knowledge and sound clinical judgment. Struggle is not a flaw in the learning process; it is a critical component of it.

Will AI change this traditional approach to learning? Almost certainly. The more important question is whether it will enhance learning or erode it.

Residents and fellows are central participants in our learning environment. As AI becomes increasingly integrated into healthcare education, we must ensure that these technologies support learning rather than subvert it. The development of professional independence still depends on active engagement through reading, clinical experience, and continuous improvement. Learners must remain accountable for their own education and recognize that meaningful learning still requires effort. Faculty, in turn, must continue to support trainees by supervising their clinical care, assessing medical knowledge, skills and clinical reasoning; providing formative feedback, reviewing documentation, and correcting misunderstandings and mistakes.

As the UW Medicine Use of Artificial Intelligence Policy becomes part of the fabric of our organization, it is important that we get this right. The stakes are too high not to. The Policy establishes a framework for education and training, expands opportunities for responsible research, and strengthens governance around the evaluation and use of AI technologies. Familiarity with the Policy and associated standards will help all of us reduce risk, while promoting innovation that ultimately benefits patients and learners.

We will need to embrace AI technologies in our learning environments, but we must do so thoughtfully. The goal is not to resist change, nor is it to adopt every new tool uncritically. Rather, our responsibility is to ensure that technology serves as a complement to human learning, judgment, and professionalism. By understanding and adhering to the Policy, we can help ensure that as AI continues to evolve, risks are mitigated, and opportunities are maximized.

Appropriate oversight and feedback by faculty remain foundational to educational integrity and patient safety. However, effective supervision in the era of AI also includes a willingness to engage in intergenerational learning. Faculty bring experience, clinical judgment, and professional perspective, while housestaff offer valuable knowledge of emerging technologies. Together, this exchange strengthens both education and patient care.

AI technology will continue to rapidly evolve, and UW Medicine will continue to review and refine its AI policies, processes, and workflows. One principle, however, remains constant: AI is a tool. It does not replace the need for human judgment. AI-generated content may contain hallucinations, omissions, or bias. Ultimately, those who use AI remain responsible for evaluating, verifying, and standing behind the accuracy of the information it produces.

As we begin this journey with AI, Syndey Harris’ quote warns us against binary dehumanization and emphasizes the risks of losing connections with our ability to think, reflect and feel. Our challenge is not whether to use AI, but how to use it in ways that strengthen learning, preserve professional integrity and accountability; and ultimately improve the care we provide to our patients.
